# 70.8% (Thomas Armstrong's weblog)

**70.8%** (`70point8percent.blogspot.com`) is an American small-craft weblog written by **Thomas Armstrong**, a Delaware River member of the Traditional Small Craft Association, and named for the share of the Earth's surface that is water. Its 318 posts range over traditional and indigenous craft, designers, museums, festivals and voyagers, and thirteen of them touch this design or its designer. Its relevance here is concentrated in one autumn: an eight-part **"Microcruising"** series that set the [Paradox](/paradox.md) and its UK sailors alongside Serge Testa, Frank and Margaret Dye, Webb Chiles, Charles Stock and Bill Longyard as one entry in a canon of small-boat voyaging.

Like [intheboatshed.net](/references/intheboatshed.md), this is **outside coverage** rather than an owner's own record — and, being American, it is outside coverage from the other side of the Atlantic, written by someone with no stake in the design, who had never seen one, and whose account of the boats is entirely read off other people's websites. Two of its pages nevertheless carry primary material, because two of the principals answered him: [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md) left a signed comment, and [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md)'s own plans blurb is quoted in full.

## Its evidentiary value and limits

Its value is threefold. It is the **only source in this bundle that treats Paradox as one item in a deliberate survey of famous small-boat voyages**, so it records what the design's standing looked like from outside the builders' community in 2008. It captured, in passing, two **primary statements that exist nowhere else in this form** — Serjeant on the order in which the UK boats were built, and Bolduc's public ordering terms. And its photographs are dated: [Faith](/boats/faith.md) under sail and with her builder ashore, and [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)'s cabin, all published in September 2008.

Its limits are those of any second-hand account. Everything Armstrong says about the boats themselves is **read off other people's websites** — [smallsailboats.co.uk](/references/paradox-uk-website.md) and [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md), both named as his sources — so where he and a participant differ, the participant governs. He is careless with names, consistently writing **"Balduc"** for Bolduc and **"Seargent"** for Serjeant, and he mistyped the microcruising address as `microcrusing.com`, a link that never worked.[1] He describes North Carolina as the Bolducs' "native" state, which their own account does not support.[5] His series numbering is broken in three separate ways — no post is headed "part two," "part 7" is used twice, and the running head alternates between "Microcruising," "Micro/Macrocruiing" and "Macro/microcruising."

## Bill Serjeant on the order the UK boats were built (September 2008)

The most substantial thing on the blog is not in a post but in a comment. The day after the Paradox instalment went up, **Bill Serjeant answered it in his own name**, and stated the UK build order outright: **"Al Law was the first in the UK to build a Paradox sailboat. Derek Clark built the next which is now in Norway and others are being built here."**[4]

That is a dated, first-person attestation from the man who ran the UK Paradox website, and it settles as a plain statement what the rest of the archive establishes only by assembling dates — [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)'s "little Jim" launched at Poole in April 2003, [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" launched in August 2006 and sold that September to a buyer in Norway. It independently corroborates the Norwegian sale from outside both the mailing list and Serjeant's own site, two years after the event. The closing clause — "others are being built here" — is a September 2008 snapshot of a third and fourth UK build under way, consistent with the count on his own site.

He also gave, in the same comment, his own one-line verdict on his boat: **"I'm really pleased with mine which is easy to sail, cheap to keep and maintain."**[4]

## The distributor's plans blurb, quoted verbatim in public

The Bolduc instalment reproduces **Dave Bolduc's own ordering blurb, introduced as "From David"** and quoted in full.[5] It is the fullest public statement of the plans-ordering terms outside the mailing list, and it is dated to September 2008:

- The plans are **$40** and **twelve pages**, and Armstrong's own aside — "A steal" — is the outside reader's reaction to that price.[5]
- **No step-by-step instructions are included**; for those, a buyer is directed to **[Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s** building guide, with two of his e-mail addresses given.[5]
- That booklet **"first appeared as a 16 part series in 'Messing About in Boats,'" and is "worth getting if you are at all serious about building this neat little boat."**[5]
- Payment is by **money order, bank check or PayPal**.[5]

Each of those figures already sits in the bundle from other evidence — see [building plans](/references/building-plans.md) for the price and page count and [the "Building the Paradox" articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) for the sixteen-installment origin — but this is the distributor confirming them **in public, in his own words, on a date**, rather than in private correspondence or on his own site. It is the second independent corroboration of the "16 part" count after microcruising.com's own, and it corroborates from the distributor's side the recurring mailing-list finding that the plans are drawings and not a manual.

The same post is a dated status marker for two of Matt Layden's other boats. As at September 2008 the Bolducs **owned [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) and had cruised her to the Bahamas "at least 7 times,"** and **owned [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) and were restoring her** — the latter hedged by Armstrong himself, "if I've got it right."[5] The Bahamas figure is consistent with the seven voyages the Bolducs' own year-by-year record gives, the last of them in 2003.

## Paradox placed in a canon of small-boat voyaging

The series is worth reading as a whole for what it implies rather than what it states. Over four months Armstrong ran instalments on **Serge Testa**'s 11 ft 10 in circumnavigation, the **Around in Ten** race meant to beat it, **Frank and Margaret Dye**'s Wayfarer passages to Iceland and Norway, **Webb Chiles** and the 18 ft *Chidiock Tichborne*, **Charles Stock**'s 70,000 miles in the 16 ft 6 in *Shoal Waters*, and **Bill Longyard**'s self-designed 14 ft *Lucky Town 7* — and the instalment he chose to open with was **Paradox**, through Serjeant's and Law's boats.[3][6][9] For a design whose own community spent years arguing about how seriously it should be taken (see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md)), an unconnected American writer's answer in 2008 was to file it with the Dyes and Webb Chiles rather than with dinghies.

Two of the series' minor threads bear on this archive directly. The Testa instalment credits **all its photographs to "Bill Seargent@ SmallSailboats UK"**, evidence that Serjeant's site served outside writers as a photo resource well beyond its Paradox section.[6] And the *Around in Ten* instalment, which proposed six sub-10 ft boats racing around the world from Bermuda in January 2009, closes in a comment four years later: asked what became of it, Armstrong answered, "As far as I know it never went off."[9]

## An outsider's first reading of the designs

The blog's first Layden post is a short one and its interest is entirely in its candour about the reaction the boats provoke. **"When I first saw them, Matt's designs seemed a bit silly to me, but then most really good art is objectionable in some way when you first see it, because you're is not prepared to understand it"** — and then, on closer inspection, **"meticulously thought out and very practical boats which, Phil Bolger et. al. notwithstanding, are defining both a new aesthetic and new possibilities in design and cruising."**[1] It also records, as a plain fact of 2008, that **"Matt doesn't have a website"** and that the Bolducs were the route to his work.[1]

A companion post five days later gives the contemporaneous outside view of the **[Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md) collaboration**: Sven Yrvind is "a designer, builder and writer about boats, and a freind [sic] and collaborater [sic] of Matt Layden," and his current project "incorporates much of Matt's philosophy."[2] That is the same reading [intheboatshed.net](/references/intheboatshed.md) reached in February 2008 and that Yrvind himself set out in print that October (see [Catalyst](/references/ayrs-catalyst.md)) — three independent outside statements of the collaboration inside one year.

The blog separately gave the [Furled Sails podcast](/references/furled-sails-podcast.md) a contemporaneous notice, naming among its subjects "Serge Testa, Webb Chiles, Sven Yrvind, Dave and Mindy Balduc," and identifying the show as Noel Davis's, "who shares his microphone with Kristy" — outside confirmation that the Bolduc and Yrvind interviews were both live on the site in October 2008.[7] A later post ends by pointing readers at [Bill's Log](/references/bills-log.md) for coverage of the 2010 Jester Challenge — the two sources meeting in public.[8]

## Three photographs of the UK boats, September 2008

The Paradox instalment carries three photographs, none credited individually and all consistent with having come from Serjeant's site.[3] The first is **"little Jim"'s cabin**, shot from aft through the open hatch: the forward bulkhead with its round access port and oval opening, the instruments on the bulkhead face, the side bins, the tiller and mainsheet across the frame, a green hull side at the edge, and a dark green painted sole. The second is **"Faith" under way offshore** under a tanbark sail set from her yard, an outboard on her transom, her skipper's head just showing at the hatch. The third is **Bill Serjeant himself standing barefoot in the water beside "Faith,"** her name in script on the bow, mast stepped and sail furled, in a tidal marina under a footbridge.

The Bolduc instalment adds three more: two of **Little Cruiser** — one an interior looking forward — and one of **Swamp Thing under sail** in open water with a white sail.[5] The Layden post's single photograph shows a **beached Layden sharpie** on a sandbar, mast stepped and no sail set, a figure standing beside her; the blog's own filename for it is `cdenig1.jpg`, which points to [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md), though the post does not name the boat.[1]

## A name that is not this one

One post matches on the name and should not be chased. The **2011 Mid Atlantic Small Craft Festival** report prints the sailing order of finish, in which entry 9 is **"Steve Layden," sailing a boat named *Pigwidgeon*** — a different person entirely, at a Chesapeake festival, with no connection to Matt Layden on record.[10]

## Citing this source

Posts are dated and the blog is live, so citations are in the past tense and to publication date, consistent with the bundle's historical-archive convention. Cite in the form:

> [70.8%](/references/70point8-blog.md) — "[Post title]," Thomas Armstrong, YYYY-MM-DD — [detail].

Comments are cited to their own author and date, distinguished from Armstrong's post text:

> [70.8%](/references/70point8-blog.md) — comment on "[Post title]," [Commenter], YYYY-MM-DD — [detail].

The identifier is the post title plus date, which resolves at `https://70point8percent.blogspot.com/YYYY/MM/<post-slug>.html`. One caution on reconstructing an address: the **year and month in the URL are those of the draft, not always of publication**, so a post published on the 1st of a month can sit under the previous month's path. Where the two differ, the publication date is the one cited and the archive month is the one that resolves.
