# Paradox building plans

The full **building plans** for the [Paradox](/paradox.md) (distinct from the introductory [Study Plans](/references/study-plans.md)) have, since at least July 2001, been distributed by a single authorized seller, the only person authorized to sell Paradox plans.[1]

## Source and cost

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Distributor | Dave Bolduc |
| Address | 1736 Phillips Avenue, Greensboro, NC 27405, USA |
| Phone | 336-272-1801 (after 9:00 pm EST) |
| Web | `http://home.triad.rr.com/lcruise/paradox1.htm`, later `http://www.microcruising.com/` |
| Price | **$35 US** in 2001-2005, drifting to **~$40** by 2006-2008, including a build CD showing how to build the boat |

The plans are the authoritative document builders are urged to follow (see [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md) and [hull scantlings](/design/hull-scantlings.md), which quote the plan sheets). For the plywood quantities the plans call for, see the [plywood bill of materials](/design/hull-scantlings.md).

This was, by a wide margin, the most frequently recurring question in the archive: newcomers asked essentially the same question — where to buy the plans, and, once ordered, why an e-mail to the distributor had bounced — at least twenty times between July 2001 and early 2008, prompting one builder to note the question "has been answered so many times that people are starting to ignore it."[1][2][3][4] The answer was always the same authorized seller, distributing on Matt Layden's own behalf, at a price that held near $35 for years before drifting toward $40; e-mail is the way to order, addressed to `mbolduc@triad.rr.com` (the space after "mbolduc" is a deliberate anti-spam-bot insertion, removed before sending) or via PayPal to the same address, with the nominal fee already covering return postage.[5][6][7] The one recurring complication is seasonal: the Bolducs go cruising for a few months most years, so a buyer who writes in during one of those absences gets no reply and should simply wait rather than conclude the plans are no longer available — the [Micro Cruising group](/references/micro-cruising-group.md) was repeatedly offered as a further source of photos and files on small cruisers generally in the meantime.[5]

Two companion resources exist alongside the plans themselves. Don Elliott's paid **"Building the Paradox" booklet** — 16 articles, about 50 pages, $8.00 plus $2.00 US ($4.00 overseas) shipping, mailed from 711 Wisconsin Ave, Box 202, Tomah, Wisconsin — is a widely recommended companion (see [Building the Paradox articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) for the full description).[8] Opinion on its quality has been mixed rather than uniform, from "some useful pointers (and quite a lot of less useful stuff)" to a blunter "about 30% of the manual is readable."[9][10] A free, community-written alternative also exists — see [A community-written beginner's guide](#a-community-written-beginners-guide) below.

Newcomers fixated on the plans-buying step are sometimes usefully redirected: "buying plans is easy, sourcing materials can be a challenge," since ordinary hardware-store plywood won't do and marine or premium-grade stock, lumber, epoxy, fasteners, and sail cloth often have to be shipped in regardless of how quickly the plans themselves arrive.[11] See [materials sourcing links](/references/materials-sourcing-links.md) for the consolidated plywood, lumber, epoxy, and hardware supplier list that grew out of exactly this reframing.

A few smaller facts round out the picture. The Paradox's flat bottom draws repeated praise as "a great feature," including in comparison against an 18 ft 1979 Cape Dory Typhoon.[12] Asked whether any Paradoxes had been built in Europe or sailed the North Sea, the answer pointed to a list of Paradox boats built or under construction (`www.smallsailboats.co.uk/paradox/names.htm`), the same UK-hosted names list used elsewhere as the standing directory of known Paradox builds.[13] A secondhand plan set can occasionally be had more cheaply than a new one: one builder, having decided to buy a used boat instead of building, put his own plan set up for resale bundled with Don Elliott's build manual for $52 — a reminder that a [secondhand](/operations/secondhand-market.md) purchase can sometimes come with a freed-up plan set attached.[14] The distributor has also been open to a straight Paradox-for-Bolger-"Micro"-plans swap with a builder who already owned a Micro set, calling it "a sweet deal too."[15]

**Tools and skill required.** The tools needed to build a Paradox are modest — mainly a battery drill/screwdriver, a jigsaw, and a hammer — and the "how to build" article series above is "almost an essential" companion to the plans (see [is Paradox a suitable first boat?](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md)). The plans' built-in trial-fit-before-gluing sequence makes mistakes non-fatal, so the boat is difficult to mess up even for a self-described "bodger" rather than a trained woodworker: one such single-handed builder solved the problems of moving full plywood sheets and turning the hull over in a tight garage with roller skates (see his own "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" construction-photo gallery).[16] Steaming is used only to bend solid-wood strips laminated onto the bulkheads; a motor is optional, needing only 2 hp on the transom, and the boat can equally be sailed its first season without one, relying on a yuloh; the sides and bottom "go on very easily," though the bottom must be worked quickly once the glue is spread.[17]

**Plans and trailering.** Loaded-weight figures for towing a Paradox on its trailer are given at [trailering — tow weight and vehicle capacity](/operations/trailering.md).

## Why Paradox resists easy enlargement

The fullest designer-fidelity argument on record against simply scaling the Paradox design up — width fixed by the 4 ft plywood sheet, a full redesign really required, model testing before release — is covered in full at [stretched Paradox — Don Elliott's case against simple enlargement](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#don-elliotts-case-against-simple-enlargement), which also carries the numeric scaling rules of thumb for builders who want to stretch the design anyway.

**Reaching the distributor when e-mail fails.** Direct e-mail to the plans distributor has, on more than one occasion, gone unanswered — from spam filtering, a simple lull, or (as with a 2004 newcomer who could not get a reply at all) an address that simply would not go through.[18] Two further routes are on record: posting to the **Micro Cruising Yahoo Group**, since the distributor is more likely to see a message on their own group; and the [paradox-uk.co.uk](/references/paradox-uk-website.md) site, which republishes the contact address on its info page.[19][20][21] When one such newcomer's own attempts kept failing, a fellow builder offered to relay the request on his behalf, and Dave Bolduc later apologized for the trouble.[22][23][24] Making Matt Layden's own e-mail address public on one occasion prompted an explicit appeal to respect his privacy: Matt does not want to personally field every question that comes up, especially ones already answered in the group's archive, which is precisely why plans requests are routed to someone other than Matt himself — he still reads the list and occasionally posts himself, and members were asked to let him come forward when he judges it appropriate rather than seek him out directly.[18][19] A further, more complete recurrence came in November 2008, when a newcomer new to sailing altogether reported that both e-mail addresses he understood to hold the plans and a manual were returning as undeliverable — left unanswered on record.[25] A reply the same weekend pointed him instead to the [paradox-uk.co.uk](/references/paradox-uk-website.md) site's own building section for a current address, repeating the standing suggestion to also buy Don Elliott's construction book.[26]

## A community-written beginner's guide

Alongside the paid booklet, a free, community-written alternative exists: a short "Beginners guide to building a Paradox," posted to the `paradoxboatbuilders2` group's files section as its author's own build neared completion. Deliberately kept to about four pages by avoiding repetition, it distills what its author judged necessary reading, tools, and lessons learned building "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" from scratch, offered copyright-free and open to other builders' amendments and additions.[27] The guide drew immediate praise for its grounded, practical-experience angle — "words of wisdom garnered from actually going through the build, warts and all" — since nothing quite like it had been offered to the group before.[28][29] A revision 2, incorporating other builders' suggestions, followed within the day.[30]

A parallel, budget-driven build illustrates how differently two Paradoxes can come together once the plans are in hand. A Wisconsin builder who ordered Don Elliott's booklet in 2004, his only prior boatbuilding experience a Bolger Brick, worked in a shed just 7½ ft × 22 ft, tight enough that he needed to cut a hole in the end wall to get the finished boat out.[31] Committed to trailer-sailing rather than living aboard and to using scrap and reclaimed lumber wherever possible, he weighed 3/8 in plywood sides against the plans' 1/2 in and stock unglassed 1/2 in bottom before strip-planking the bottom's *inside* face over a plain 3/8 in ply skin, and finishing the topsides in three coats of alternating-color paint rather than fiberglass — a documented low-cost approach for a lake/river/stream boat unlikely to see saltwater.[32][33] A prior epoxy sensitivity from an ultralight-aircraft build (facial swelling, a rash lasting weeks) was the real complication: rather than risk a repeat reaction, waterproof glue and ring nails with any glassing hired out, or polyester resin as a fallback, were recommended over epoxy.[34] The completed strip-planked deck stage came in at **$113** in materials, with a starting ballast of about 200 lb judged sufficient for river and lake sailing.[35]

## A July 2006 recurrence, and a blunt assessment of the paid manual's quality

The plans FAQ recurred again in mid-July 2006, this time paired with a question about the paid construction manual's value.[36] The patience answer was, by now, standard — the distributor was most likely away cruising and would reply once he returned.[37] On the manual, opinion split more sharply than in any earlier recurrence: one builder rated only about **30% of the manual as actually readable** and recommended Jim Michalak's book from Duckworks instead;[9] another judged it worth the money for a first-time builder, containing "some useful pointers (and quite a lot of less useful stuff)."[10] A third pointed past the manual entirely, to a first-person build account published on Duckworks Magazine's website, plus the group's own links page as further reading.[38]

## A second September 2007 recurrence, and a pointer to Enigma for a larger sailor

The FAQ recurred again in September 2007, paired with a sizing question: a prospective builder in Spokane, Washington — 6 ft tall, 225 lb — asked where to find the plans and whether Paradox had ever been stretched a few feet longer and wider to fit a bigger sailor, worried he would otherwise heel over crossing his legs in the stock 15-footer.[39] The reply pointed him to a UK Paradox site with links to the plans distributor, and from there to a search of the group's own Files section and discussion archive — see [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md) — with practicing Paradox's building methods first suggested as good preparation before attempting a stretched build.[40] A second reply suggested a different route entirely for a sailor his size: Matt Layden's own [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md), pointing to the separate EnigmaBuilders Yahoo group and a builder's in-progress 15 ft Enigma prototype.[41]

## A second March 2008 recurrence, and delivery-time data points

A cluster of the FAQ in March 2008 adds the fullest delivery-time data on record. Two long-time readers making their first posts both reported e-mailing the distributor about plans without a reply;[42][43] a third builder's nearly simultaneous order drew an immediate reply, confirming the address was unchanged and the plans already on the way — the likely explanation, as ever, was that the distributor was simply away for a spell.[44][45] Asked how long delivery typically takes once ordered, the consistent answer was no more than a couple of weeks,[46][47] borne out by a New Zealand order that showed up quickly despite the distance[48] and a set posted from the United States that took fifteen days to reach Romania.[49][50] Once the plans were in hand, one questioner reported that Don Elliott's paid PDF booklet — bought early and left unopened on his hard drive for months — suddenly made sense once there was an actual set of plans to read it alongside.[51] One of the two long-time readers who had reported an unanswered e-mail eventually received his own plans about a month after first raising the question, pleased with their detail and looking forward to building and to testing the chine runners himself — the slowest outcome on record, though still consistent with the standing explanation that the distributor is simply away rather than unresponsive.[52]

An earlier, independently reported round trip corroborates the same two-week estimate from a different country entirely. A UK order for the full plans, placed by mail since no credit-card payment option was available, sent two $20 bills by airmail and had the plans back two weeks later, in October 2006 — a year and a half ahead of, and consistent with, the estimates above. That order's price was $40 for the full plan set (given elsewhere as under £22), with no designer's royalty charged against going on to build from them. The plans themselves proved to be 12 sheets of paper, slightly longer than A3 — a size noted as not easy to copy in the UK. The separate building-instructions CD-ROM, priced on its own at $14, was ordered the same way and likewise arrived within two weeks.[53]

## A May 2009 recurrence, and a proposed Duckworks-distribution model

The plans FAQ recurred once more in May 2009, when both authorized sources appeared briefly unreachable at once: a newcomer reported being unable to reach either the plans distributor or Don Elliott, their websites seemingly out of commission, and separately asked whether any builder held a spare plans/manual set to resell.[56] Dave Bolduc apologized for a delay in answering and sent the plans information and how to reach Don Elliott's family by private reply.[57] The same public exchange drew in a further prospective builder — an experienced boat builder aiming to start his own Paradox the following winter — seeking the same information after seeing Dave's reply on the list.[58]

The recurring friction around reaching the distributor directly prompted a considered proposal: sell the plans as a package with Don Elliott's CD building manual, with a royalty paid to Matt Layden and the Elliott family once or twice a year, and distribute them through Duckworks instead of directly through the Bolducs, who are often away sailing — Duckworks' own distribution fee runs only about 12%, based on prior experience with Jim Michalak's plans, getting plans to interested builders more quickly and reliably, since the hassle of obtaining them directly is thought to discourage some would-be builders.[59]

The same difficulty surfaced again the same day from further afield: a prospective builder in Mar del Plata, Argentina, having had no reply by e-mail, sent a postal request for the Paradox plans and building manual (and an Enigma set, if one existed) instead — already in contact with the builder of [Cachivache](/boats/cachivache.md) in Patagonia and hoping to join the group, he asked only for the price and payment method.[60]

## A July 2009 recurrence: still unreachable through Don Elliott's own address

The same difficulty recurred once more two months later, this time explicitly framed around Don Elliott's death: a newcomer, having tried without success to buy the Building Manual through Don's own e-mail/web address, asked how to obtain a copy, consistent with the standing pattern of delay and non-response already documented above and on [Don Elliott — after his death](/people/don-elliott.md#after-his-death).[61] When no one else answered, a fellow builder offered his own personal copy instead — Don had been very protective of his copyright, so the offer was conditional on the newcomer first getting clearance from Don's family.[62]

## A further August 2009 recurrence: a working route through Don Elliott's widow, and a bounced address

A newcomer, again prompted by having heard of Don Elliott's death, asked the same question a month later.[63] The answer resolved the July recurrence above without anyone needing the offered personal copy at all: the same builder who had asked in July reported having since obtained the Manual directly from Don's widow, Wanda Elliott, as a PDF for $14 by PayPal, cautioning that her own adjustment to her new circumstances might slow a further order down a little.[64] Trying the address given, though, a further builder got only a bounce — "no mailbox here by that name" — and asked whether anyone knew another way to reach her.[65] The bounce turned out to be a one-letter typo rather than a dead address: the correct address doubles the "t" (`donelliott@`, not `donelliot@`) — a mistake the builder who had reached Wanda successfully weeks earlier had made himself before getting it right.[66]

## An October 2009 recurrence: an unmet request, and an informal alternative to the manual

The same difficulty recurred once more within weeks: a new correspondent, delayed two weeks by no reply while trying to obtain the Building Manual, reported buying plywood the same day regardless so his own building partner could start on the bulkheads, still hoping the manual would settle the many questions expected once construction was properly under way.[67] A fellow prospective builder, similarly stalled since spring after giving up trying to reach a source that same summer and deciding to start his own project anyway on the strength of having built other boats before, offered to pay full price plus shipping for anyone willing to sell or copy their own manual.[68]

A different view of the manual's necessity followed: rather than chase down a copy, a builder could get everything needed to start from [Al Law](/people/alastair-law.md)'s own build website, with questions asked of the group as they came up along the way — in this view the manual is not strictly required and, being "overly detailed," may if anything slow a builder down rather than help.[69] Cutting through the request more directly, a further builder offered to make copies of his own manual for anyone who wanted one, free of charge beyond the cost of a beer — the first entirely informal, no-cost route to the manual on record, alongside the family's own paid PDF and the conditional personal-copy offers of previous recurrences (see [above](#a-july-2009-recurrence-still-unreachable-through-don-elliotts-own-address)).[70]

The correct address needed one further clarification beyond the earlier single-T slip recorded above: it takes **two Ls as well as two Ts** in "Elliott."[71] Buying a copy through another channel in the meantime, the correspondent who had opened this recurrence found the mast-layout sheet only "ALMOST" making sense even with the Building Manual open beside the plans — see [components/rig/mast.md — reading the mast drawing, a recurring point of confusion](/components/rig/mast.md#reading-the-mast-drawing-a-recurring-point-of-confusion) for the fuller answer this drew.[72] Doubt was then raised over whether Wanda Elliott could accept PayPal at all, most buyers by then having mailed a check or money order instead after she replied with payment instructions[73] — but the correspondent's own experience overturned that doubt directly: having already obtained a copy through another source, he separately sent PayPal payment to her address regardless and received a further copy by e-mail the next day, so the family's ordering process does after all take PayPal, closing out this recurrence with two independent working routes to the manual rather than one.[74]

## A further November 2009 account: a full order timeline, and independent corroboration of the MAIB serialization

A further newcomer, having also had initial difficulty ordering, posted an unprompted full account of the modern process end to end, "for the record." The manual is available on CD or as PDF files by e-mail; an enquiry to Wanda Elliott drew a reply within two days quoting her own standard terms in full — some eighty pages across sixteen chapters, illustrated with over 100 photographs, a new, enlarged section on making and installing the ballast weights, priced at **$14.00** plus a small CD-postage surcharge if that format is wanted, with PayPal "the fastest way to receive the booklet." Payment went out the same day as her reply, and the four PDF files (ranging from 59 KB to 3 MB) arrived by e-mail three days later — the fastest complete order-to-delivery timeline on record for this recurring saga.[75]

The same message passed on a discovery of independent value: messingaboutinboats.com's own website hosts a public archive index of past issues' contents (consulted November 2009) — a title/author/page listing rather than the articles' full text, but enough to independently corroborate, from a source outside this bundle entirely, the print citations already given for the [Messing About In Boats — the "Building Paradox" construction series](/references/messing-about-in-boats.md#don-elliotts-building-paradox-construction-series-19961997) and its [published design particulars](/references/building-paradox-series.md#the-published-design-particulars). The same online index also lists two further Paradox-related items outside the sixteen-part series proper: Matt Layden's own July 1995 debut design-and-specifications feature and a "Paradox...The Plans" item credited to Dave Bolduc — see [Messing About In Boats — provenance and citing](/references/messing-about-in-boats.md#provenance-and-citing) for the exact page numbers this independently confirms.[75]

A further prospective builder, from Newport, Rhode Island, confirmed the same working channel days later from the buyer's side: his own Paradox plans and Don Elliott's building manual, both sourced in the ordinary way, arrived together and quickly despite the recent Veterans Day holiday — a further data point that the modern ordering process, plans and manual alike, is working reliably by late 2009.[76]

A newcomer trying the same channel two days later got only a bounce.[77] The likeliest cause, on record from a fellow builder's own experience: **Yahoo's own webmail habit of inserting a space into a copied e-mail address** — worth checking for and stripping out before assuming the address itself is wrong.[78] The correspondent who had posted the original account confirmed the address worked reliably every time on his own end and offered to keep his write-up current from further reports, adding one further practical detail to it: Wanda Elliott's PDFs are sent as **two separate e-mail installments** where the complete set is too large to send at once.[79] Reassured, the newcomer confirmed he would retype the address and order that same day.[80]

A further reader of the same original account asked separately whether the manual could be paid for by check rather than PayPal, and to what address a check should be sent.[81] The answer, from the correspondent behind the original account: there is no fixed check-payment address on file — simply order the manual from Wanda Elliott by e-mail as usual and ask her directly what address to send a cheque to, the same ask-Wanda-directly channel that settles every other variation on this recurring question.[82]

## A March 2012 proposal: 3D CAD models to supplement the paper plans

A correspondent argued the paper plans, being two-dimensional, are harder to follow for some parts — the mast and the cabin top were named as particular sticking points — than an equivalent 3D model would be; framed the idea as no different in kind from the group's own acceptance of epoxy, GPS, and power tools over strictly traditional methods; recalled that old list messages already referenced various builders' own CAD models of the design; and proposed Google Sketchup, free and simple to use, as the tool for it, crediting a 2008 list post for the idea. As a worked example, he modeled Bulkhead 1 to the plans' exact millimetre dimensions, with no 5 mm offset and no allowance cut for the sheer clamps or chine logs, and uploaded it to the group's Files section; he further proposed that the distributor set up a members-only subgroup, open only to buyers of the plans, for sharing further such models.[162] Two days on, with no reply, he followed up with a bare one-word prompt.[163]

Two considered replies followed. ## Reading the plans: a first-time builder's backlog of questions

A prospective builder working systematically through the plans page by page, building a parts list along the way, compiled a representative backlog of the small interpretation questions that recur once a newcomer actually has the plans in hand (as distinct from the "where do I buy them" FAQ above):[83]

- **"P&S,"** used throughout the plans, stands simply for **port and starboard**. The question recurred in September 2008, drawing the same answer plus a plainer restatement — "both sides" — and a traditional memory aid: "is there any red port wine left?" ties the red port running light to the left (port) side and the green light to the right (starboard) side, worth remembering precisely when tired, seasick, or scared and least able to think it through.[84][85][86][87] That same recurrence spun off a run of further mnemonics and a buoyage aside over the following two days: one reply ties the color to right-of-way rather than position, since showing a red light normally marks the burdened, give-way vessel.[88] Not even the underlying "red, right, return" color convention is universal — at least one stretch of water, between the east side of Vancouver Island and the British Columbia mainland, reverses it, a reminder that a chart's own legend is the final authority in unfamiliar waters, not memory.[89] A different mnemonic ties the words' lengths together directly, since "red," "port," and "left" are all shorter than "green," "starboard," and "right."[90] British sailors have their own further shorthand for a buoy's shape, appending "in the can" to describe a flat-topped can buoy as against a pointed conical one[91] — though how the shorthand actually conveys shape went unexplained and unanswered on record.[92] The buoyage aside resurfaced over a week later with a fuller technical grounding: some thirty years earlier the International Maritime Organization tried to establish one worldwide buoyage standard and failed, since enough countries declined to change that the result was an agreement to disagree, leaving North America with a **lateral system** (the red/green, red-right-return convention) alongside a **cardinal system** based on compass direction in some areas; "red, right, return" is not an arbitrary mnemonic but a real rule — the red (starboard-hand) buoy should be on a vessel's right only when travelling in the direction of the flood tide, which is exactly what makes it confusing on a coast, such as British Columbia's, where the flood's actual direction is not obvious among the islands.[93][94]
- The plans' called-for glass quantities — **38 in woven roving** (three layers on the bottom) and **50 in 6 oz cloth** (one layer elsewhere) — are workable to estimate from the boat's dimensions, though builders vary in which fabric they actually use[83]; see [woven roving versus biaxial cloth](/construction/hull-planking.md#woven-roving-versus-biaxial-cloth-what-the-laminating-schedule-is-for) for the fuller fabric-choice discussion this same backlog's follow-up questions fed into. One builder who used **12 oz biax with no mat backing** (mat backing does not bond into an epoxy matrix)[95] reported it goes on in a single layer rather than roving's three, uses less epoxy overall, and is no harder to fill and fair than roving — on a 19 ft powerboat built the same way, beached and worked from regularly over several years of diving, the biax layup showed no wear-through anywhere; since the Paradox's bottom is flat, biax was judged workable for almost any builder regardless of experience level, whatever fabric the boat's intended use (lake, coastal, or ocean) might otherwise argue for.[96]
- The **mast, boom, and yard lengths** are not missing from the plans, as a first read might suggest — they are given on **page 12** together with the rest of the spar/rig drawing, consistent with the box-mast specification documented at [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md).[97] One builder's own mast, cut from clear 1×4 in doug fir porch flooring in 16 ft lengths, needed to be a little shorter than that to stow inside the boat through the stern opening (lightning rod removed first).[98] Drawing #12's spar dimensions are given **full size** for the fore-and-aft and sideways dimensions but **scaled** for the length dimension — a mixed-scale convention that lets a designer show fine cross-section detail and the whole spar's length on one page, and also makes fairing a long curve easier by compressing its length; a builder unfamiliar with spar drawings should study the page carefully with that convention in mind.[99] **What was believed to be a drafting error** was noted on the same sheet: extra dimension-arrow marks running to the right of the vertical dimensions (500 mm, 900 mm, 900 mm, etc.) that, if painted out, leave the dimensioning correct and make it easier to distinguish the drawing's two views (a side-section view at left and a half-end-section view at right).[100] A cautious approach to cutting the spar blanks is to trim them only to the lengths already given in the plans and leave final trimming until after the sail itself is made and can be measured against the boom — a sequence one builder followed for his own mast and spars while a new sail was still being sewn.[101] A hand-colored redrawing of the mast plan, produced to make the sheet easier to follow (shared only with permission, since its maker holds its copyright), and a separate full written description of how to read the same drawing, is also on record. The spar dimensions are unchanged between the original and the newer, taller sail plan; a boom built to the stock dimensions can have roughly 8 in trimmed off once the new sail is bent on and the boom measured against it, but that trim should wait until the sail is actually in hand rather than being cut in advance.[102][103] Sailed side by side on the same boat ("Zoë"), the original, flatter-cut sail proved good enough that it might still be in use today were it not for the newer sail's extra draft.[102]
- Reading Drawing #12's squared-off sections for the **boom and yard**, once past the length/width dimensions given at each spar's top and bottom, drew a specific worked answer: each square on the sheet is a **section through the spar at that location**, the same convention applying to the yuloh's own varying shapes. On the yard, the section **700 mm from one end measures 42 × 38 mm with 12 mm radius corners**; on the boom, the section **1,850 mm from the tack measures 47 × 38 mm**, and **950 mm from the tack, 43 × 38 mm** — figures a builder confused by the sheet's squares can use directly rather than re-deriving them from the taper. On the yuloh, the **flatter side sits on top when in use**, confirming the camber convention discussed at [yuloh](/components/yuloh.md#blade-camber-the-ford-nomenclature-and-pivot-socket-dimensions).[104][105]
- The **stateroom sole hatch**, like the cabin sole hatches, uses a **continuous (piano) hinge** in the stock design — a builder will be standing on it, so a full-length hinge is warranted, and a piano hinge is also easier to line up than a pair of ordinary hinges.[98][106] At least two builders departed from this in practice: one used the same piano hinge design as built but would use something less mud-and-grit-prone next time, suggesting a webbing hinge instead; another skipped the stateroom divider/hatch arrangement entirely, instead hinging a single floor down its centerline (port side fixed, starboard side lifting) with copper tacks and webbing for the hinge itself, and used the resulting full-width space under and across the floor for storage rather than a separate stateroom bin.[107] Whatever layout a builder chooses for the forward stateroom, every component needs to fit back out through the opening, since it is installed before the deck goes on; one builder built to the stock plan but found removing all of the woodwork there again a two-minute job, making it easy to try different layouts to see what suits before committing, and kept the stock central partition in that boat while reducing its height.[108]
- **Shelves are mounted to the bin sides** with a plain epoxy butt joint and epoxy fillets, or alternatively built as separate pieces resting on their own lip rather than fastened directly to the bin sides[106][107] — either approach is workable, since screws or nails driven into the shelf's end grain would not hold well regardless; one builder specifically reported four years' trouble-free service from the epoxy-fillet method, applied to the aft side of the bin face.[109]
- Several plywood parts called out in the plans' interior fit-out — the stateroom bin side and divider (9 mm), the breast hook and deck scabs (12 mm), the vent trunk baffle (12 mm) and sides/front (18 mm), and the aft bin dividers — are genuinely **not shown on the plans' "10 sheets" cutting diagram** and must be sourced as additional material; much of this interior detail is intentionally left to the builder's own discretion rather than fully dimensioned.[83][98] In practice one builder found the 9 mm stateroom bin side and divider on the sheet already labeled "Fore. Bin Sides," used left-over scrap for the 12 mm breast hook, deck scabs, and vent trunk baffle, and got the 18 mm vent trunk sides/front from left-over material after reordering the cutting layout — flipping one rudder piece and moving it, and reorganizing the transom sheet's layout since it was too narrow to cut as originally drawn.[106][110] Aft bin dividers, cut from 6 mm ply in that builder's case, likewise came from a spare 6 mm sheet bought so the cabin roof could be cut without a joint; beyond that one spare sheet, no additional plywood was needed to complete the boat, and a builder is encouraged to try various interior arrangements rather than commit to a single layout on the first attempt.[106]
- The **metal angle** shown on one of the plan sheets, and exactly how it is attached, drew only a brief answer on record: one builder reported simply bending up a roughly inch-long piece of stainless for the purpose.[106] A fuller reading of the same detail describes it as bent from a 2 mm by 25 mm strap (roughly 3/32 in by 1 in folded down its length), fitted to the underside of the water tank top to capture the bottom of a bin face that, unlike the aft bin faces, does not extend all the way to the floor — since the ballast in that area sits on the floor and needs to stay as far outboard as possible.[111] One builder who decided against internal water tanks altogether skipped the fitting entirely, instead running the floor to within about 1/4 in of the hull sides (leaving a small ventilation gap) and resting bin sides directly on the floor, so nothing could drop into the bilge from the side bins.[111] The fold itself runs **across** the strap's width, not along its length, forming a small bracket that stops the middle of the bin face from flapping loose and holds it down to the floor panel — the bracket is screwed to the floor panel, and the bin face simply drops into it during fit-out.[112] The engineering logic this fitting serves puzzled at least one builder new to the plans: the aft bins' continuous hinges support the sole's edge through the bin sides, which are themselves fixed to bulkhead cleats, but forward (where the tanks sit) there is no such hinge, leaving roughly 4 in of unsupported sole edge at that bulkhead once the angle-and-bin arrangement takes the bin face's load instead of the floor's — a detail that reads as unusual on paper but has not been reported as a practical problem by builders who have used it.[113]
- On glass yardage for the bottom and topsides, one builder's own estimate from memory was **roughly 18 yards of 38 in woven roving and 18 yards of cloth**, having used 60 in-wide 7.9 oz cloth because it was cheaper to source at the time than the plans' called-for 50 in width.[109]
- On **spar length trimming**, the plans' stock boom and mast lengths hold for both sail plans, so a builder need not commit to a shorter spar in advance: a drawing of the newer sail circulated among builders suggested the boom could come down by roughly 8.5 in and the mast by 4–4.5 in under the taller sail plan, closely matching a separate from-memory estimate of about 8 in off the boom[114][102] — but as with any such trim, cutting should wait until the sail itself is in hand and can be measured directly against the spar, and a builder should expect to build to the stock dimensions first regardless.[103] Builders who read Drawing #12's mixed-scale convention and its noted stray dimension-arrows for the first time have reported it takes more than one pass to fully parse, since the two differently scaled dimension sets and the sheet's two overlaid views (a side-section at left, a half-end-section at right) are not obvious until the drafting-error correction above is applied by eye.[115] A newcomer working through the plans page by page can still find the mast detail on sheet 12 impossible to interpret despite otherwise following the set without difficulty — asking, unresolved on the record, whether the mast is asymmetric and how the sheet's dimensions and centerline apply to the material — a further, independent instance of the same sheet's recurring difficulty.[116]
- Where a builder's own reordered plywood cutting layout — moving parts between sheets to make the metric stock go further than the plans' 4 ft × 8 ft-equivalent layout assumes — is shared with other builders, it has proven directly useful for planning cuts of the costlier, thicker sheets (18 mm) in particular, though reproducing exactly how a given layout fits every part onto one sheet can take some study from a drawing alone.[117] Worked through in full for the 18 mm sheet specifically: the vent trunk sides come off the 9 mm sheet alongside the foredeck, one from each of the two wedge-shaped scrap offcuts; for the 18 mm sheet, the bottom rudder piece is flipped about a horizontal axis and moved as far left as possible so it fits inside bulkhead 3, leaving room in the scrap above for the breast hook and, in the lower-right wedge, for the vent trunk front. Flipping the rudder piece this way also solves a second problem: the rudder's "nose" otherwise falls off the edge of the sheet as drawn, whereas the new layout leaves an easy gap to fill with a small infill piece once the two rudder halves are joined. For anyone without access to a CAD system to test a layout before cutting, taping together newspaper templates of each part and physically shuffling them on a full-size sheet mock-up was suggested as a simple way to find a working arrangement before committing plywood.[110]
- The **bulkhead 1 and bulkhead 2 support levels for the stateroom sole** appear mismatched on the plans — the sole sits on bulkhead 1's lower floor frame and on a cleat on bulkhead 2, but those two supports are not level, a roughly 20 mm difference that, read literally, would leave the sole out of level and could bind the sole hatch against the bulkhead as it opens.[118] The explanation is the hull's own **rocker**: bulkhead 1 sits considerably higher than bulkhead 2 once the hull's rocker is properly accounted for, and a floor built with that in mind comes out level despite the plans' two support points not lining up on paper — the difference in floor level between the two bulkheads can be checked directly against the waterline reference.[119] The plans also omit the bulkhead 1 cleats meant to secure the bin sides, and are inconsistent about whether a port-side bin exists at all (shown on one sheet, absent from another) — both left to the builder's own judgment in practice, one builder having skipped the plans' bin-side arrangement entirely.[118][111] On bulkhead 1, one builder placed the bin-side cleats **120 mm to port and 220 mm to starboard** of the centerline; there is no port bin forward of bulkhead 2 in that layout, only a nearly central divider some 120 mm to port of the centerline, with the cleat shown 390 mm to port on bulkhead 2 (sheet 7) belonging to the bin aft of that bulkhead.[120][121] A separate reading of the same bulkhead 2 sheet found the "12×19 Cleats P&S" label's arrows both pointing at the starboard-side cleats (fore and aft), which — read together with a port side bin actually being called for — suggests the arrow at the fore starboard cleat is simply misplaced on the drawing.[122]
- The **outboard bulkhead cleats** (against the hull panels) are dimensioned 25 mm × 35 mm with a bevel shown along one side (7 mm at bulkhead 2, for example); in practice, exact cleat dimensions are **not critical** — a builder can use whatever scrap stock is to hand and split it to approximate size, since a Paradox's structure tolerates this kind of minor variance well.[119] One builder used whatever offcuts were on hand, splitting a 50 mm × 25 mm piece into a pair of cleats where two were needed, or a 25 mm × 40 mm piece where that fit better, without any problem sourced to the exact sizing.[120] As a general philosophy for the plans' small ambiguities: "let the force guide you," and failing that, epoxy, silicone, gaffer tape, brass screws, ring nails, or the example of other builders can resolve most of them — the boat's own structure is considered over-designed enough that a few cut corners in the bin-side cleats are unlikely to matter, since the bin sides are only semi-structural (supporting the sole in places) rather than fully load-bearing.[123] The tank-vent and mast-ground fitting shown protruding from bulkhead 2 on other sheets is likewise something at least one builder omitted outright[111] (having decided against internal water tanks and a wooden mast's need for such grounding, given lightning-strike likelihood in that location); in the stock plans this fitting sits on the port side of the vent/lightning-rod run at the point where the stateroom sole hatch begins, so building it as illustrated would foul the hatch's operation unless the vent and ground-plate run is moved aside to clear it.

## The 12 oz biaxial-versus-woven-roving question, continued

## Citing this source

The plans themselves have no public URL: they are obtained only by mail order from the distributor described above (see [Source and cost](#source-and-cost)), by e-mail or PayPal, never downloaded or viewed online. The stable identifier is **the plans** as a whole, or, when a citation quotes a specific part of them, the **sheet or page number** the plans themselves use — e.g. "page 12," "Drawing #12," "sheet 5." Cite in the form:

> [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md) — [detail].
> [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md), page 12 — [detail].

The ordering-contact addresses given above (`http://home.triad.rr.com/lcruise/paradox1.htm`, later `http://www.microcruising.com/`) are live external routes for reaching the distributor to buy the plans, not URLs for the plans' own content.
