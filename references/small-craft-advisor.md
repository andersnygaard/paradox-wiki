# Small Craft Advisor

**Small Craft Advisor** (`smallcraftadvisor.com`) is an American magazine devoted to small sailing and rowing craft, and the outside publication that has covered this design and its designer more often than any other. Its relevance to the archive runs through four separate pieces published across a decade, in two different formats: three articles in the print magazine, and one first-person account by an owner on the magazine's own blog. Between them they supply both the fullest statement [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) has made in his own words about how his boats work, and the only published magazine account of a Paradox written by the person sailing her.[1][2][3]

## The four pieces

**"Enigma — The Matt Layden Interview" (May 2007), interviewed and photographed by Steve Isaac.** The single most-cited outside source in the archive, and the designer's own correction of the way his best-known invention is usually described: chine runners are "the most noticed part of the system I've been working on," not a foil that replaces a centreboard, and the "internet mythology" that they do is wrong. The full technical content is worked through at [chine runners — how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) and [chine runners — origin and evolution](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md); it is very likely the article Layden mentioned in April 2006 that he was writing, "with a fairly detailed description of Enigma and her origins."[2]

**"Matt Layden — A Profile" (May/June 2009), by Doug Cameron.** A companion profile to the interview, and one of the archive's few biographical treatments of a designer who avoids publicity; drawn on at [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) and [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md).[3]

**"Cedar Key Small Boat Meet" (Sept/Oct 2003).** A gathering report that devoted substantial space to the two Paradoxes present, "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" and "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)," recorded Zoë pointing higher and faster to windward than a Sea Pearl with its leeboard down, ran four photographs of the pair, and noted the "reclusive designer" himself at the same event — outside corroboration of a meet the archive otherwise knows from its participants. See [the 2003 Cedar Key messabout](/merits/cedar-key.md#press-coverage-the-2003-small-craft-advisor-cedar-key-article).

**"The Upside of Downsizing" (29 November 2013), by [Sean Mulligan](/people/sean-mulligan.md).** The owner of "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)" writing up his engine-less run down Lake Havasu and back for a national readership. It is the same sail his own blog and the mailing list report — the **Bosunator Challenge** — told a third time, five weeks later, to strangers rather than to fellow builders.[1]

## What "The Upside of Downsizing" is worth as evidence

Its value is not the sail, which is already well recorded from two contemporaneous first-person sources, but the **register**: this is a Paradox owner explaining the design outward, to a mainstream small-boat audience, and choosing which facts carry that argument. Several specifics are firsthand and appear nowhere else in the archive.

**A fourth owner-stated displacement figure.** "At 1460 pounds designed displacement, she is not the lightest girl at the dance, nor the ultimate paddle/yuloh machine" — some 50 lb above the ~1,400/1,410 lb design displacement the archive's other independent estimates converge on, and offered in the specific context of how hard the boat is to drive by hand. See [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).[1]

**The 9-inch draft as a sailing advantage rather than a specification.** The draft figure is on record in several places; here it is what let him "cut the corner of every twist and turn of the lake to within feet of the shoreline," with the kick-up rudder, in company with keelboats that could not follow.[1]

**Trailering and storage, timed both ways.** Setup and teardown at the ramp "around 20 minutes each way," and at home the boat "easily tucked away in the garage" with the trailer moved around by hand — a fuller statement of the routine recorded at [trailering](/operations/trailering.md) and at [Scout](/boats/scout.md).[1]

**The boat's wind instrument named.** Her anemometer was made by **Inspeed**, reading 26 mph apparent on the upwind leg — the only place the make of any Paradox's wind instrument is recorded.[1]

**A deliberate no-outboard position.** He had not fitted an outboard bracket and had not brought a motor at all: "I may or may not eventually add an outboard bracket to her, but knowing that my fate is not tied to the outboard is a liberating feeling for sure." See [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md).[1]

**How an owner reached this design in the first place.** He came to it as "a lurker on many microcruiser sites," naming the Bolducs' [Microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) and the [WaterTribe Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md) site as where he learned that micro-cruising existed at all, and SCAMP's arrival — promoted by this same magazine — as what finally decided him to try it. That is a documented route from published sources to a new owner, and this magazine sits on both ends of it.[1]

**A companion boat named.** **Jan Maslikowski**, sailing a **Balboa 20**, dropped his headsail for hours to slow to the Paradox's pace so the two could finish the night in company, and took the photograph that closes the article.[1]

## Its limits

The piece is a **magazine essay, not a log**: it carries no construction data, no rig or hardware detail, and its numbers are rounder than the same sailor's own contemporaneous account of the same day. Where the two disagree, the blog and mailing-list versions were written five weeks earlier and should be preferred:

| | "The Upside of Downsizing" | Mulligan's contemporaneous account |
|---|---|---|
| Course length | a "30-plus mile course," 40-plus with tacking | "around 50 miles," including tacking |
| Maximum speed | "surging at times to 5.5 knots" | 5.6 kt maximum |
| Paddling before the breeze filled in | "about 3.5 miles" | 4-5 miles |
| Upwind speed in the chop | "slowed to 3 knots" | about 2 kt, later about 3.5 kt |
| Apparent wind upwind | "26-mph apparent" | above 25 mph for about an hour |
| Displacement | 1,460 lb | "nearly 1500lbs" |

None of these is a contradiction of substance — they are the same day rounded differently — but the article should not be used as the arithmetic authority for a sail it recounts from memory.[1][4][5]

It also **never dates the sail it describes** (see below), never names the challenge, and never mentions why the challenge was named as it was — for **Bosun**, Mulligan's dog, who had died shortly before and who had been the informal ambassador of the Havasu Pocket Cruiser's Convention. A reader with only this article would not know that any of it happened on one particular Friday, or that seven other boats sailed the same self-imposed course.[4][5]

## The article's comment thread

The blog article carries a comment thread of **eighteen comments** running from the day of publication to June 2016, and four of them are evidence rather than applause. It is the only place in the archive where the **builder and the sailor of the same hull speak to each other in public**: [Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md), who sold "Scout" unfinished and never sailed her, wrote that she was "so proud of Scout, and so glad you are taking her out as much as you do. She is a capable boat"; Mulligan answered by naming her "Scout's true Mamma," crediting her with building the boat "from the ground up from nothing more than a set of plans," calling himself the boat's "custodian," and directing readers to her build blog.[6][7]

The thread also fixes two dates the archive otherwise carries loosely. **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)** commented on the day of publication that he would be "picking up my own Paradox in a couple of days," dating his collection of "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)" to about **1-2 December 2013**; Mulligan's reply asking whether he had bought "Mike's Paradox in Texas" is the earlier of the two occasions he put that question, the other being on the mailing list three days later.[8] And **Jackie and Mike Monies** announced in August 2014 that Mulligan and Henson would give a **joint program on "Scout"** at **Sail Oklahoma 2014** — see [gatherings](/merits/gatherings.md#sail-oklahoma-2014-a-joint-builderowner-program-on-scout).[9]

Answering a reader who asked whether the sail had been "a tune up for Florida," Mulligan gave the **earliest dated statement of his [Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md) ambition**: he was "positive that Scout is up to the task," Matt Layden having "proved that handily," while doubting he was himself "a quarter the sailor he is" — and confirmed that sailing progressively longer distances in the boat was partly a way of evaluating whether to attempt it. His Texas 200 report seven months later returned to the same reasoning.[10]

Comment threads are cited here as what they are — dated public statements by named people — and are weighed by who is speaking. Henson's and Mulligan's own comments are first-person primary evidence about their own boat; the rest are readers' reactions and carry no evidential weight beyond recording that the article was read.

## Provenance and dating

The blog article was published **29 November 2013**. Its page byline reads **"by admin"** — a site-account artifact, not an author; the article's own first line is "By Sean Mulligan," and the whole text is first-person from the owner of "Scout." It is cited to Mulligan.

**The sail itself was 18-19 October 2013**, some six weeks before publication. The article gives no date at all, and the reconciliation is worth stating because the piece reads as though it could be any day of that autumn. Mulligan announced the Bosunator Challenge on 12 October as "1 week from today," then wrote on the evening of **17 October** that "all of this will be put to the test **tomorrow** as we sail the Bosunator Challenge" — the later and more specific of the two statements. That fixes the sunrise start at **Friday 18 October 2013** and the 3 a.m. finish at **19 October**, which the article's own "100% illuminated full moonrise… within 20 minutes of sunset" independently corroborates: the full moon fell on 18 October 2013. His recap followed on 23 October, on the blog and on the mailing list the same day.[1][11][4][5]

The three print articles are dated by issue: **Sept/Oct 2003**, **May 2007**, and **May/June 2009**. None has been located online; the 2007 interview and the 2009 profile are both cited in the archive from partial text.

## Citing this source

The magazine and its blog take different identifiers.

**Print articles** — issue (month/year) plus article title and author:

> [Small Craft Advisor](/references/small-craft-advisor.md), May 2007 — "Enigma — The Matt Layden Interview" (interview and photo by Steve Isaac), Matt Layden — [detail].

**Blog articles** — post title plus date. The blog's permalinks are WordPress query-string ids at `https://smallcraftadvisor.com/our-blog/?p=<id>`, so the id is the stable address and is given alongside the title; "The Upside of Downsizing" is `?p=4119`:

> [Small Craft Advisor](/references/small-craft-advisor.md) — "The Upside of Downsizing," Sean Mulligan, post 4119, 2013-11-29 — [detail].

**Comments on a blog article** — the commenter's own name plus the article and the comment's date, which is what distinguishes it from the article itself:

> [Small Craft Advisor](/references/small-craft-advisor.md) — comment on "The Upside of Downsizing," Lezlie Henson, post 4119, 2013-12-01 — [detail].

Attribute blog articles to their real author, never to the "admin" byline. As a dated snapshot of a historical record, the content is cited in the past tense, consistent with the bundle's historical-archive convention.
