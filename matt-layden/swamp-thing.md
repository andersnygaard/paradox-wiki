# Swamp Thing

**Swamp Thing** is one of [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s small cruising boats, part of the Layden design family alongside [Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md) and the [Paradox](/paradox.md). Built in **1985**, she was **Matt Layden's first micro-cruiser to use [chine runners](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md)** — the tapered external chine log he first tried and named on this boat, replacing the fouling leeboards of his earlier [Terrapin](terrapin.md). Matt sailed her **engineless** from Connecticut down the Intracoastal Waterway and out to the Bahamas. She is "one of his prettiest designs," though her accommodations for one are "quite spartan," and she appears — with a photo of Matt aboard in the Abacos — in Ida Little and Michael Walsh's book *Beachcruising and Coastal Camping*.[1]

Her build year is not quite settled. [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) — the primary source this bundle follows — gives **1985**, and an independent illustrated survey gives **1986**.[3] The second date does not originate with that survey alone: in 2006 Dave Bolduc, whose own website carries the 1985 figure, said on the air that Swamp Thing was "built in 1986, two years earlier than our own boat," [Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md) being a 1988 boat. Both dates therefore trace back to the same firsthand owner, and the bundle treats the year as an unresolved one-year discrepancy rather than a third party's error, while continuing to follow the website.[20]

## Design particulars

| Particular | Value |
|---|---|
| LOA | 13' 2" |
| Beam | 3' 8" (44") |
| Draft | 7" |
| Sail area | 73 sq ft |
| Displacement | 900 lb |

Her notable details: **fresh-water ballasted**; **roller-furling main**; **all lines lead back to the cabin**; and **oars that stick out the sides for quick use** — the "oar holes on the sides" that remain her most-remembered identifying feature.[2][1]

An independent illustrated survey corroborates the design and adds detail on the oar ports: they let her be **rowed from inside the cabin**, "in similar fashion to" Phil Bolger's Birdwatcher, and the same survey confirms Swamp Thing as the **prototype for the chine runners** later carried onto Paradox and Enigma, and records the Connecticut-to-Bahamas passage as a **round trip**, sailed there **and back**.[3]

Swamp Thing received **considerable restoration effort** from the builders' community, part of the hands-on experience with Matt's boats that informs it.[4]

## The Gulf Stream rollover — and self-righting proof

The most dramatic episode in Swamp Thing's record is a **complete rollover in the Gulf Stream**, a real-world demonstration of the design's self-righting. One rough night mid-Gulf Stream, Matt was on deck signalling a nearby freighter with a flashlight; the ship passed too close and its wake upset already-confused 10-foot seas, knocking the boat onto her side.[5] Matt grabbed a **chine runner** for support, which pulled her over on top of him; she was "soon righted in the tepid waters off Florida," Matt climbed back aboard the flooded boat, bailed her out, and continued on to the Bahamas.[6][7] Had Matt been **inside** the cabin — as the boats are designed to be sailed — he "most likely would not have had any trouble."[5] This is one of two documented Layden-boat knockdowns (the other being [Terrapin](terrapin.md)'s off Maine); see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) and [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md).

## Sale history and the best-sailing of Matt's designs

Swamp Thing sold for **$600** a few years before late 2001; the buyer had been urged to buy her by someone who was ready to buy her himself otherwise, and who made clear that if she were ever sold on, "she's coming with me!"[8]

Swamp Thing is rated **"really the best sailing boat of all of Matt's designs,"** compared to an **Albert Strange canoe yawl** for her **very narrow transom**, sailing to windward "like a witch" and tacking "like a dream."[8] This is the most enthusiastic comparative performance endorsement of any single Layden design on record — rating Swamp Thing above Paradox and Little Cruiser on sailing quality, even though Paradox remains the best-documented design in this bundle.

## From Dave Gatan to the Bolducs: the 2004 rescue and 2005–2008 rebuild

Swamp Thing was owned and day-sailed around a home in the Florida Keys, but let fall into disrepair. By **2004** she was rescued, brought north from Key Largo (ahead of that season's Hurricanes Frances and Ivan) and given away. Her Keys owner, a longtime small-boat liveaboard (including a 23' Glander Cay) who never used an outboard and was an accomplished Flamenco guitarist, died around 2009.[9] An independent 2006 description fills that note out: Dave Gatan was a lifetime sailor who had lived aboard small boats in the Keys for many years — mostly the 23 ft Glander Cay, latterly a Rob Roy — who **carried no engine at all** and practiced sculling constantly, going out to scull around the anchorage in the middle of the night when a calm kept him awake. That habit once got his 23-footer a hundred yards clear of a freighter in the Gulf Stream after his wind died; see [why the technique has to be second nature](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#why-the-technique-has-to-be-second-nature).[21]

With the boat newly arrived by mid-September 2004, the question of what on her was original versus later-added — against a high-resolution scan of the Swamp Thing photo from Ida Little's book[10] — was resolved with help from copies of the image; two further photos of Swamp Thing at her new home are hosted on `microcruising.com`.[11][12] Swamp Thing's condition at that point was rot in both the bottom and the deck, "going to take a bit of work," though restoration was initially expected to wait until the following summer, since [Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md) was mid-way through being covered in fiberglass.[10] The documentation effort also drew interest from at least one other Paradox builder hoping to attempt a direct replica of Swamp Thing from detailed photographs or her plans.[13]

In fact the rebuild began **months earlier** than that "next summer" timeline — the boat's sad state ruled out leaving her sitting idle. Probing with a screwdriver turned up rot in both the plywood hull and the deck, plus problems at the stem and stern; the bad wood was cut away and new pieces spliced in, with rotten areas filled using epoxy, fiberglass, and microballoons. The **skeg** was removed, its original galvanized bolts — some twenty years old — rusted away almost entirely, and **new blades for the rudder and for one of her custom oars** were made, both of which had also seen better days. This work proceeded alongside finishing [Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md)'s fiberglassing, the two refits continuing in parallel rather than fully sequentially (see [Little Cruiser — a mid-October 2004 progress update](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md#a-mid-october-2004-progress-update)).[14][15]

A **multi-year rebuild (2005–2008)** followed, running in parallel with [Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md)'s own 2005 refit. Swamp Thing had arrived with **serious rot** in her transom, rear deck, coaming, and stem; the rotten areas were ground out and saturated with unthickened epoxy, rebuilt with new wood and glass, and eventually the **entire bottom was replaced with new ½" A/C-grade plywood** (glued with epoxy/cabosil and fastened with bronze ring nails), **five new floor timbers** fitted, a new rudder blade made, and the deck re-glassed. In **2008**, at Matt Layden's suggestion, her **chine runners were rebuilt about ¼" wider and ⅛" thicker — roughly 25% wider than the originals — to try to improve her windward performance**.[9] See [construction](/construction/index.md) for the epoxy/rot-repair techniques used and [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) for the runner iteration.

Midway through that rebuild, in July 2006, the work was still going and had a stated ambition behind it: Dave Bolduc was "steadily working on Swamp Thing to try to get her up to speed" so that she could be sailed **in company with Matt Layden's new [Enigma](enigma.md)** — the thirteen-footer and the twelve-footer together — with an Everglades Challenge entry floated as the occasion, tempered by a clear-eyed view of what four or five near-sleepless days would actually cost. The same account confirms her propulsion arrangements from the restorer's own hand: she carries **oars so she can be rowed**, and also **takes a sculling oar**, so both were to be tried once she was afloat again.[22]

## Rudder rebuild technique: beveled lead versus screwed lead, and a lead-melting caution

Ahead of the fuller 2005–2008 structural rebuild, a **new rudder blade for Swamp Thing** was made as an early, standalone repair — pulling 133 old ring nails and grinding the rotten bottom away down to the plywood/mahogany chine-log glue line first. For the new blade's **lead weighting**, the method shown on the Paradox plans (wood screws holding the lead in place) was set aside in favour of **beveling the mounting hole on both sides to lock the lead in mechanically**. [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) used this same beveled-lead method on both Swamp Thing and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) (rudder blade and centerboard alike), and it works well specifically **when the lead weight sits some distance from the trailing edge**, as on these designs' blades.[16] The technique has also been praised by builders working on other small-craft designs who have used it themselves.[17]

Melting the lead for the casting itself carries its own technique and hazard. A practical tip: start the pot with only a **small amount of lead**, then gradually add more as the existing metal melts and the total quantity grows, rather than loading the pot heavily from the start.[16] A serious safety warning comes from experience **melting down old lead pipe**: residual moisture trapped in the pipe can flash to steam and make the pipe fire a slug of molten lead like a gun barrel — in one case, a 6 in length of pipe threw molten lead about **10 ft**. The caution: always point any pipe being melted down away from anyone standing nearby.[18]

## Why she sails so well: the chine runners

What makes Swamp Thing a better sailing boat than Paradox or Little Cruiser was long an open question — candidate explanations included a **keel**, a **narrower beam**, or **lighter displacement**.[2] The answer is her **[chine runners](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)** (rather than keel, beam, or displacement), which make her **"the most manueverable of Matt's designs,"** tacking "on a dime."[19] See [sailing characteristics and rig balance — "Trim is very important"](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md) for the full cross-design comparison, which also contrasts Swamp Thing's easy tacking with Paradox's deliberately more directionally-stable, harder-to-tack hull form. This accounts for the maneuverability of her sailing; the separate windward-speed quality ("sails to windward like a witch") is attributed to the same chine-runner foils.[8]

## A widened chine runner still awaiting its sail trial (2012)

Years after her chine runners were rebuilt about a quarter inch wider at Matt Layden's own suggestion, Swamp Thing had still not been relaunched to find out whether the change actually helped — getting her back on the water so her sailing could finally be compared against her sister ships remained the stated goal as of March 2012. Her mast had by then also been raked back somewhat at some point, though whether that was a deliberate balance correction or simply done later is not established. See [the plans' "CA" label, and an unresolved centroid offset](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#the-plans-ca-label-and-an-unresolved-centroid-offset-2012) for the wider mast-balance debate this observation was made in reply to.[23]
