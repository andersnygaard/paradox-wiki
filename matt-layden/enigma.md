# Enigma

**Enigma** — formally "**Enigma 360**" — is [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s **sixth micro-cruiser**, built in 2005 after roughly a decade in which Matt "got married, found employment, and worked on a few smaller boats." She followed [Terrapin](/matt-layden/terrapin.md), [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md), [GJAC](/matt-layden/gjac.md), [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), and the [Paradox](/paradox.md) in the Layden build order, and is distinct from — but a direct design descendant of — the original Paradox hull that is this bundle's main subject.

## A lighter, hybrid design

Enigma marks a deliberate shift toward lighter construction than Matt's earlier boats. She is a **12-footer intended for coastal sailing**, built as a **stitch-and-glue plywood sharpie** with a distinctive **"V" bottom at the bow** — a departure from the flat-bottomed sharpie hulls of Terrapin through Paradox. She was built light enough that she "could even be hefted with aid onto the top of a car for transport to far away cruising grounds," using a **unique built-in axle system** and cart wheels for overland portaging.

Her design deliberately combines features proven on two different earlier boats:

- **Hull shape and proportion**: "remarkably similar" to [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s.

- **Rig**: uses [Paradox](/paradox.md)'s efficient [roller furling](/components/rig/furling.md) and distinctive low-aspect-ratio lug sail, rather than Little Cruiser's more complex battened, slab-reefed sail.

- **Chine runners**: similar to Paradox's [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md), but "made shorter and wider."

- **Dodger**: a fold-down soft cabin/dodger described as "an improved version of the one now on Swamp Thing" — implying Swamp Thing was subsequently fitted with a similar dodger, presumably during her later restoration.

A spoken statement four months after the 2006 race, from owners of two of the three parent boats, gives that same combination independently and adds one element to it. Enigma "is kind of like a combination of all the other designs that he's done before," carrying **[Paradox](/paradox.md)'s rig**, **[Little Cruiser](little-cruiser.md)'s rudder and basic hull shape**, and **[Swamp Thing](swamp-thing.md)'s dodger**. The **rudder** attribution appears nowhere in the microcruising.com account above, which credits Little Cruiser only with hull shape and proportion; every other element matches point for point. The same account frames the whole family as incremental rather than inventive — Matt "built the first one back in… the mid-80s, and then they just continued to evolve and improve over time."[91]

## The "Mini Paradox" working name and the 2005 construction-era discussion

While Enigma was under construction in the first half of 2005, the new boat was discussed under the informal name **"Mini Paradox,"** working from the construction photographs on [Matt's brother's website](/references/andrew-layden-website.md). That name appears **nowhere** in the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) record — the authoritative source on Matt's designs — which documents the **same 2005 boat** (same year, same **"V" bottom at the bow**, the same brother's-site construction photographs, and the same **"shorter and wider" chine runners**) only as **Enigma**. On the weight of that convergent evidence, "Mini Paradox" and Enigma are **the same boat**: it was the working name during the build, before the Enigma name was settled. No single source states the equation outright, but nothing in the record describes two different 2005 boats, and every distinctive detail lines up.[1][2][3][4]

That 2005 discussion — largely prompted by how hard the runners were to make out in the photographs — is the fullest contemporary account of the boat and the [chine-runner](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) thinking behind her:

- **Chine runners, present but easy to miss.** They are there, just hard to see in the photos; confirmed in person against a suggestion the hull might not float deep enough for them to work.[3][4]

- **Runner placement.** They **start farther aft and end a little farther forward** than a stock Paradox's, and are shorter — a real placement difference attributed to **end-plate effect** rather than the curvature of the chine.[5][6][7] Curvature is a weak candidate mechanism in any case: a boat's continual pitching constantly changes the curvature's orientation relative to its motion. There is "some" curvature in the runners but not much, leaving open whether it is functionally essential, an incidental result of following the chine to match the hull bottom, or a secondary hull-stiffening feature — the same open question recorded for stock Paradox runners (see [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#the-chine-runner-curve-cosmetic-fabrication-choice-or-hydrodynamic-feature)).[8][9][10]

- **A higher-aspect-ratio sail experiment.** Her sail is **shorter but wider** than the stock Paradox rig, used by Matt to explore the effect of aspect ratio; no sail dimensions are on record.[4]

- **Handling and accommodation.** Sailed two-up with Matt and his wife, she **"sails like a witch"** and is **very roomy for her size** below (no interior photographs are on record).[4][11] Her speed was credited mainly to being sailed by someone who knows how to sail a small sharpie well and to her light weight — a photo-based estimate of **~130 lb** for the bare hull (consistent with her 180 lb empty weight above), giving an all-up displacement with crew under a third of stock Paradox's, and a sail-area-to-displacement ratio thought better than Paradox's — rather than to the slight **V tortured into the bottom at the bow**.[10][12][13]

- **A chine runner as keel extension.** The runners sit along the chine much as a **low-aspect-ratio keel** is fitted to a centerline — ending abruptly aft and fairing into the chine forward — and their area **should be counted as a keel extension** when figuring a boat's center of lateral plane: adding the runners' profile to the chine curve and computing lateral resistance from the combined profile "would be real close."[13][14]

- **Extending the idea to a catamaran.** Chine runners belong only on the **outboard** side of each hull, not both sides of both: a sailing cat rarely sits level, so an inner-hull runner would ride in surface turbulence and the other hull's bow wake. Better to size the outboard runners larger, placing them by the boat's average heel angle — akin to the molded keel-form appendages on some beach-cat hulls, which give leeway resistance without a moving board's vulnerability.[8][15][16]

- **The modeling software.** The 3D renderings Matt posted of her were produced in **Rhino 3D**, a NURBS CAD package that handles chine and smooth hulls and accepts **RhinoMarine** (Proteus Engineering, makers of the NURBS marine package **FastShip**) for hydrodynamic analysis — though the machine that made the published screen grabs was not running RhinoMarine.[17][18]

| Draft | 0.155 m | 6" |
| Sail area | 5.5 sqm | 57 sq ft |
| Displacement | 267 kg | 588 lbs |

The 588 lb displacement figure is her **loaded/design displacement**, not a contradiction of the "180 pounds" figure quoted elsewhere — that lighter number is explicitly her **empty weight, without ballast**: "Tipping the scales at only 180 pounds empty and without ballast, she is ideally suited for solo coastal cruising as well as portaging over land."[19] The gap between the two figures is made up by [water ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md), crew, and gear.

Interesting details listed, matching the same five-point pattern used for Paradox: **chine runners**; **fold-down soft cabin**; **self righting**; **positive buoyancy**; **all lines lead into cabin**.[20]

## Racing history and change of ownership

Matt sailed Enigma on her **maiden voyage in March 2006**, entering her in the **Watertribe Ultimate Challenge** — a grueling 1,200-mile race around Florida that, that year, included a **40-mile portage** — putting Enigma's built-in axle-and-cart-wheel portaging system to a real test. She finished **first in her class and third overall**.[21][22]

The 40-mile portage ran overland **between St. George and the Suwannee River at Fargo**, with Matt pulling Enigma the whole distance on her integral two-wheel trolley, complete with cruising gear.[23]

Competitors watching the same race describe the portaging arrangement more concretely than the design accounts do: the trolley was **an axle protruding from the chine wings themselves**, onto which Matt mounted what looked like **pram wheels** — the chine runners doing double duty as the mounting structure. He hauled the boat the forty miles **in a shoulder harness**.[109] The same forum thread records the plainest outside summary of what Enigma achieved that year: **"the only sailboat to complete the Ultimate Challenge is Matt Layden's Enigma."**[110] Some of what it cost him is on record from the race's eventual winner, who came up the upper St. Mary's River behind Matt and found it "one of the toughest parts of the race" — paddling against the current through downed trees and branches on a river that "twists back and forth — five million miles to go five":[111]

> At this point in the race I was behind Wizard and Manitou and I kept thinking, how did Wizard get through this. What he described is not — can not be — what he did. He is too modest. I will tell you right now Wizard is superhuman to have gotten his boat up through some of those snags.

A watertribe.com magazine page recapping the race, "Wizard Enigma Log Book," pointed out on the mailing list in January 2023, is characterized as showing the portage cart setup coming close to disaster and probably costing enough time to have cost Matt the overall win outright; video that once accompanied the same page was reported no longer online.[123]

Two details of that race come from outside the results tables. The first is a **weather tactic**. A winter frontal system passing down the Florida peninsula blows first out of the north, then clocks east, west, and back to north — so a competitor leaving Tampa as a front comes through rides it downwind and then has a southerly behind him for a while afterward. Matt, credited by observers with an unusual attentiveness to that cycle, is reckoned to have worked the front deliberately and taken a substantial head start on the field, driving hard for the **St. Mary's River** while the wind hung in the south. The first three boats reached the St. Mary's just before it backed north again; everyone behind them was left beating into headwinds and paddling twelve-hour days for miserable mileages.[92]

The second is what Matt did once it was over. Rather than have the boat carried home, he **accepted a lift to the start and then sailed Enigma home** at the end of the race under her own rig — read by those who knew him as of a piece with a standing preference for human or wind power over any combustion engine, and possibly prompted by a remark of Sven Yrvind's about the outboard on a Layden boat's transom. See [Matt Layden — "Motors are fine as long as..."](/matt-layden/biography.md#motors-are-fine-as-long-as-matts-engine-philosophy) and [the Bolducs' firsthand record](/people/bolduc/matt-layden-authority.md#the-engine-question-and-sven-yrvinds-rebuke).[93]

Enigma was **the next iteration of the Paradox line — shorter and lighter, and capable of being carried on a car top** — a claim later illustrated directly in a photograph of her lashed to the roof of a Ford Focus.[24] She **led most of the WaterTribe Ultimate Florida Challenge**, and in 2006 Matt **broke his own 2003 solo Everglades Challenge record** in her — the same 2006 Florida circuit documented above, the Ultimate Florida Challenge's opening leg following the Everglades Challenge course.[25]

**In 2008, Enigma was sold on to new owners**, who went on to complete **three more Watertribe races** in her.[21]

Her buyer's reason for wanting her is on record, and it is a rule of the races rather than anything about the boat's sailing. WaterTribe entrants must launch **from above the high-water mark**, dragging the boat in themselves — and **[Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s 1,600 pounds could not be moved off a beach single-handed**. Enigma, at **180 pounds unloaded and 11 ft 10 in**, could. The purchase was made expressly to race, and it paid off immediately: a class win in the inaugural **90-mile North Carolina Challenge** in September 2009, then the **300-mile Everglades Challenge** in 2010, and ultimately the confidence to take her to the Bahamas alone in 2013. See [Dave Bolduc — joining the WaterTribe](/people/bolduc/dave.md#joining-the-watertribe-and-why-he-bought-enigma-to-race).[106]

Both of those races put Enigma under an outside eye for the first time since Matt sold her. A competitor in the North Carolina Challenge, anchored for the night in Taylor Creek, recorded that "Freebyrd's Matt Layden-designed Enigma passed us during the night" — Bolduc sailing on through darkness the rest of the fleet had stopped for — and saw him the next morning beating close along the beaches around Harkers Island in the 20–25 knot winds that drove the observer himself out of the race.[107] The following March, Enigma was back on the Everglades Challenge starting beach at Fort DeSoto, drawn up within sight of Elusion, the boat that had replaced her: "his older design, Enigma, is also on the beach for the Everglades Challenge, skippered by its new owner, Freebyrd (David Bolduc), a veteran of cruising the Bahamas in Matt's Paradox design."[108]

Set against the rest of the family in a comparative appraisal by an owner who had sailed one of Matt's designs for years, Enigma is judged **"perhaps the most versatile of his designs, since she can be used for day sailing and for cruising."**[26]

A **new prototype Enigma with a hard cabin** — rather than the fold-down soft cabin of the original — was being finished as of a 24 August 2012 blog post.[21]

A **video of the 2006 race** offered visible proof that the chine runners were working: Enigma's progress to windward in the footage showed the runners "really work," prompting interest in buying a set.[27] See [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) for the stock Paradox's own owner-reported pointing-angle data.

Further footage of the same Ultimate Florida Challenge, hosted on the race organizer Watertribe's own "ChallengeViewer" page, shows Enigma sailing directly toward the camera against a current with no keel and a square sail — visible proof, again, of her ability to head upwind. Matt raced under the call-sign **"Wizard"** for the event; a caption on the footage, remarking on how easily Matt struck, set, and reefed his sail on arriving at and departing Fort Clinch, coined the phrase **"the Wizard Shuffle"** for the effortless, practiced routine by which Matt got underway.[28]

Revisiting the same ChallengeViewer footage in 2006, Enigma's actual angle to the wind was estimated directly from the video, using the line of an incoming swell as a visual reference: roughly **45 degrees off the wind**, judged very acceptable for a cruising boat, on par with production cruisers still being built today.[29] Cross-checking against fixed shoreline landmarks visible in the same footage, Enigma appeared to **tack effortlessly** while Matt continued filming throughout the maneuver.[30] The boat's versatility was praised directly — few boats sail coastal water and also travel 60 miles up a river — and the chine runners were separately confirmed from the video to be clearly working.[31]

## Construction detail: no separate cabin sole

Working from the same race footage, Enigma's mast step appeared to be a short (about 3 in) plastic tube faired and glassed directly to the bottom panel, with **no separate cabin/cockpit sole** fitted over it — the crew instead sits directly on the bottom panel, a difference from Paradox's raised floor.[32] The likely reason is straightforward: keeping crew weight as low as possible matters more in a small boat of this kind, and omitting a raised sole saves a few pounds toward Enigma's light-displacement design goal (see [design particulars](#design-particulars) above).[33]

## Paradox and Enigma compared: overbuilt strength versus light-displacement simplicity

Paradox and Enigma answer different needs rather than one simply improving on the other. Paradox is an exceptionally strong, heavy-displacement vessel — heavy construction is part of how the design reaches her designed draft, further trimmed with lead ballast alongside water ballast. Enigma is a lighter, simpler boat by comparison, relying mainly on crew weight and a heavy plywood bottom to stand up to windward rather than on Paradox's mass and multiple ballast systems; she has far fewer parts, so her build time is only a fraction of Paradox's roughly year-and-a-half, against Enigma's few months. Which suits a given sailor is mostly a question of what is wanted: a lightweight day-sailer for one favors Enigma, while more comfort and a kindlier motion, at some cost in outright speed, favor Paradox — a far more able coastal cruiser and, for trips of up to three months away from base, a genuine live-aboard, at the cost of hauling around more weight than a fast 14-footer would.[34][35] For solo use specifically, Enigma's smaller size also makes her markedly easier to manage ashore and lighter to tow — advantages substantial enough to tip at least one prospective solo sailor toward building her instead of Paradox, alongside a still-unbuilt prototype's eventual promise of production plans.[36]

A further correspondent weighing the same choice in 2009 put concrete numbers on the hand-launch case specifically: constrained to launching by hand off a floating dock with a tide-dependent ramp — already managing a 300 lb, 16 ft open sharpie there with help at half tide — he found Enigma roughly 400 lb lighter at the design waterline than Paradox, with a stripped weight estimated at 260-300 lb, comfortably within his own 400 lb hand-launch limit, while still preferring Paradox's lower, better stability curve and its ballast stowed below the cabin sole rather than needing to be shifted for sleeping, against Enigma's own longer cabin. He ultimately had a road trailer available and so was not strictly bound by the hand-launch constraint, but the comparison remained close enough that he had not settled on either boat.[37][38]

A visual comparison drawn from the same *Small Craft Advisor* extract on Sven Yrvind's website noted Enigma carries noticeably more rocker than stock Paradox — "very Bolger-like" — where Paradox's own rocker is comparatively slight.[39]

### A 2009 comparison for a cruising couple, and an owner's firsthand verdict

A further correspondent in October 2009 asked how an extended Paradox and the Enigma 460 would compare for a couple wanting a comfortable, trailerable cruiser.[40] One reply judged the two boats close in size — the 460 nearly matching a stock Paradox's length, with Paradox's own interior width partly eaten up by its side bins — and the 460 markedly lighter and structurally simpler; an extended Paradox ballasted to sit on her lines was judged monstrously heavy, restating the same towing-weight objection already established for a scaled-up Paradox (see [Building Paradox larger — a 2009 coda](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-2009-coda-two-further-declared-attempts-a-towing-weight-fix-and-the-enigma-460-alternative)), with the reply's own crew sticking to a more conventional boat for now rather than either design.[41] A second reply agreed Paradox's thicker plywood, extra bulkheads, and near double bottom make her the harder build of the two, pointed again to his own comparison table, gave the 460's main-cabin length as 7'7" (already established above), doubted either boat suits a couple as well as [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) has for the Bolducs, and called the 460 "the latest evolution of Matt's designs, incorporating the best of his previous boats."[42]

Dave Bolduc — who by 2009 owned and sailed the original Enigma 360 himself, alongside his own long Paradox and Little Cruiser cruising experience — then gave the fullest firsthand comparison of Paradox and Enigma on record. Paradox, in Matt Layden's own words as Bolduc recalled them, is **"a 40 footer shrunk down to 14 feet"**: her interior is fitted out for living aboard, with shelves, nets, and bins giving every piece of gear a place, a galley aft, and two large water tanks amidship replenished in the field by a built-in rain-catchment system; her heavy construction is built to withstand serious weather and collisions alike with rocks, oyster bars, and reefs, and her thick insulation lets a sailor live comfortably in any weather for months on end.

Enigma, by contrast, is lighter and faster to build and transport, but far more spartan inside. Packing gear aboard Enigma feels "more like being in a kayak" next to the simple fill-the-tanks-and-load-the-bins routine of Little Cruiser: everything goes into separate small nylon bags stuffed behind leecloths alongside the water bottles, and with no bilge to collect stray water, delicate gear needs its own waterproof bags. Without Paradox's insulation, Enigma also collects a fair bit of condensation on her walls, leaving a sailor damp overnight or in rain.

On performance, the verdict was unreserved: Enigma 360 "sails to windward well," a claim proven that same month at the **North Carolina Challenge**, where she won her class in a windy, windward-heavy contest; like Paradox, she is well balanced and tracks nicely. Both boats were judged winners in their own right, with the hope that Matt would one day make Enigma's plans publicly available.[43]

### A 2013 update, cruising Enigma firsthand, and a reaffirmed verdict

Four years later, cruising Enigma himself through the Bahamas — the engine-free 2013 crossing recorded in full at [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md#biography) — Bolduc reaffirmed the same verdict from lived experience rather than recollection: Enigma is "very spartan" next to Paradox, everything needing to be stowed behind leecloths for want of bilges, and, lacking both water tanks and rain catchment, calling for frequent resupply. All the same, "Paradox is pretty much the finest all weather microcruiser out there," even while still having a great time in "the smaller sistership."[99]

### A 2015 windward verdict, and an improvised downwind boom vang

Ranking all four of Matt Layden's small-cruiser designs directly against each other, Bolduc judged Enigma's own windward performance the best of the lot — "just my feeling. Matt probably would disagree" — crediting her lower overall deck profile and an especially well-made sail; sailing her to weather, "she just climbs and climbs and climbs up wind."[103] Downwind, Enigma shares a real weakness with Paradox and Swamp Thing: none of their roller-furling booms has anywhere to fit a permanent vang, so running before a strong wind the boom lifts in gusts and rolls the boat excessively — a problem Little Cruiser's vanged balanced lug sail does not share. On his own engine-free Bahamas passage aboard Enigma, the fix was an improvised temporary vang, a split length of PVC pipe slipped over the boom and the sail's own end; full construction detail, and its later refinement into a padded "vang hoop," is at [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#an-improvised-temporary-boom-vang-and-why-the-stock-boom-has-none-august-2015).[103]

Pressed a few days later to say whether Enigma's sail was better *designed* than Paradox's or simply better *made*,[104] Bolduc clarified the earlier verdict: Enigma's sail is not actually any better than a well-made Paradox sail — the real variable is workmanship, since plenty of lug sails sewn by amateurs and professionals alike lack the draft needed to pull "these heavy little sharpies" along. Matt Layden himself shaped the sail on this 12-footer, and it carried Bolduc through **three WaterTribe Challenges** and the round-trip Bahamas cruise recorded above — firsthand confirmation that he was among the "new owners" who bought Enigma in 2008 and went on to complete three further Watertribe races in her (see [Racing history and change of ownership](#racing-history-and-change-of-ownership) above).[105]

### A February 2013 flotation retrofit: sealed compartments, tea jugs, and secured gear

Unlike Paradox, Enigma relies on sealed compartments in the bow and stern for buoyancy rather than built-in foam, a design difference that carries real risk: holing the bow, or losing one of the 5-gallon-bucket hatches sealing those compartments, could leave the boat unrecoverable in a capsize or even sinking — the reason behind Matt Layden's own standing rule, given at delivery, never to sail the boat without the hatches fitted.[98] The bow and stern compartments were originally packed full of plastic Arizona-tea jugs for buoyancy, later swapped out for gear once the boat changed hands; the change prompted foam added under the deck for extra insurance, and tie-downs securing heavy items like the toolbox and anchor so a knockdown could not hold the boat upside down or hole the hull with loose gear.[98] The same rethink extended to how the boat is bailed out — see [bailing and bilge pumps — a 2013 dewatering comparison test aboard Enigma](/operations/bailing-and-bilge-pumps.md#a-2013-dewatering-comparison-test-aboard-enigma) — and fed into a wider, general discussion of how much emergency buoyancy a boat needs, at [plywood grade and foam flotation — a February 2013 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-february-2013-recurrence-calculating-how-much-emergency-buoyancy-is-needed).[98]

### A rowing-shell collision, and what it implied for Paradox's own thicker hull

A **40 ft rowing shell**, travelling at speed, once collided with Enigma, damaging an area roughly **10 in by 6 in**; cutting out the bad wood to make the repair found little delamination beyond that zone, a repair written up in full separately.[100][101] Water began pouring in through the hole as soon as the two craft separated, and the boat had to be heeled onto one side to slow the flooding while under tow to shore — a reminder that, unlike Paradox, Enigma has no reserve of hull thickness to buy time once holed.[100]

Weighing what the same impact would have done to Paradox's own, much thicker sides, Matt Layden judged after examining Enigma's damage that a rowing shell likely would not have pierced the bigger boat's hull at all — but that the impact could just as easily have delaminated the plywood core over a considerably wider area, a harder repair to make good. Enigma's thinner, lighter sides in effect acted as a crumple zone, letting the shell penetrate easily and both craft glide together to a slower, less abrupt stop; Paradox's greater thickness trades that easy penetration for keeping the water out in the first place, at the cost of a potentially larger, though still survivable, repair.[100]

For a delamination repair of that kind, the proposed method is to drill a series of holes partway through the hull from the inside of the damaged area, pour epoxy in through them, and press the separated layers back together with weights while the hull is braced on its side; once cured, the holes are filled, faired, and painted over.[100] A second builder's simpler standing kit for smaller holes favors a full repair once hauled out over a quick permanent plug: a pre-drilled, epoxy-coated scrap of plywood, a dozen torx-drive screws, and a tube of fast-cure adhesive sealant, carried aboard to slow a leak enough to reach shore rather than to fix it outright.[102]

### A 2011 recurrence: construction methods and build time contrasted directly

A newcomer weighing the two designs in July 2011 asked directly why Paradox builds have historically run 18-24 months of nights-and-weekends work against Enigma 460 completions inside a year, some inside six months, and whether the difference comes down to construction method or simply Paradox's shelving and floor framing.[81] The answer contrasted the two methods in those terms for the first time: Paradox uses chine-log construction, Enigma stitch-and-glue, with neither method inherently faster than the other and deck/cabin attachment essentially the same on both — the real time cost is Paradox's much heavier scantlings (thicker hull plywood, heavy-duty bulkheads, longitudinal stringers, essentially a double bottom) plus her interior water tanks and shelving, against Enigma's two plywood bulkheads simply cut to fit.[82] A further reply agreed Enigma would likely take less time to build, being a different boat altogether, adding that no spec drawings for Enigma existed publicly even as of mid-2011 — the same plans-not-yet-available status already recorded above,[74][75] restated more than two years on.[83]

The same thread produced a further proposal two days later: since a stitch-and-glue hull goes together quickly, perhaps Matt Layden could be persuaded to draw up stitch-and-glue plans for Paradox herself, giving a builder the basic hull shape fast as motivation to push on through the boat's many tedious small parts afterward — though this runs up against a standing debate over whether stitch-and-glue is as strong as chine-log construction.[84] A reply judged a clever builder could work out a stitch-and-glue Paradox design without Matt's own involvement, avoiding in particular the difficulty of bending the chine logs, and that stitch-and-glue done properly should if anything be the stronger of the two methods.[85]

### A 2020 head-to-head: an owner's own Enigma 460 against his own Paradox

Chris Curtis, who by 2015 owned both the Enigma 460 "Fantasma" and the Paradox "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)," raced the two against each other directly in February 2020 and, this time, "beat the pants off" his own Paradox — crediting the margin not to any inherent superiority in the Enigma's shape but to her being hundreds of pounds lighter and a few feet longer. It was a livelier result than his own earlier pre-purchase test of the same two hulls, when "Scout" (then still "Tardis," under Sean Mulligan) had been more heavily loaded and the performance gap had come out minimal; full account at [boats/scout.md — a pre-purchase test sail against an Enigma 460](/boats/scout.md#a-pre-purchase-test-sail-against-an-enigma-460).[117]

### A 2025 comparison: an owner's Enigma and Paradox, sailed side by side

Asked in 2025 how the Enigma compares with the Paradox, and how much ballast she carries, André-François Bourbeau gave the fullest single side-by-side verdict on record from an owner who sails both regularly, alone and with friends.[127] He framed the difference as a car analogy — the Paradox "like a big pick up truck," the Enigma "like a little runabout car." Aboard the Paradox he feels as secure as in a keel boat, "stable, like a train on a track," confident enough to fish standing up on the leeward side; his own Enigma, smaller because she is smaller, carries **180 lb of removable lead ballast near the chines** and rights herself from a knockdown by body movement alone even with water briefly over the side — "great secondary stability" — but leans away under him the moment he reaches over the side, and demands more attention under sail generally, "part of the fun." The Paradox is considerably faster on every point of sail and, in his judgment, the more relaxing boat to helm. With her lead removed the Enigma can be portaged overland on her own wheels, impossible on the Paradox, and launches without rigging in a minute or two against roughly fifteen minutes to rig the Paradox with practice; climbing aboard the Paradox to step the mast has become more work for him as he has aged, a difficulty the Enigma does not share. Both boats sleep and camp him comfortably, with more room in the Paradox and a tighter fit for his folding Brompton bicycle aboard the Enigma. His verdict for a prospective buyer choosing between the two turns on the waters intended: inland lakes and rivers favor the Enigma, large lakes or coastal work the Paradox.[128]

### A 2025 continuation: reverse-engineering from photographs, and the George Van Sickle blog reconfirmed live

The same thread continued directly. Working from nothing but two photographs found online, a correspondent building an Enigma of his own asked how to derive the sail and rudder dimensions and the mast's rake, then proposed scaling Paradox's own sail proportionally from 100 sq ft down to 56 sq ft — a ratio that lands almost exactly on the Enigma 360's own tabulated sail area, above — and reducing the rudder by the same proportion, asking whether the result would leave the boat balanced under helm; he intended to buy the Paradox plans as his baseline.[129] The scaling question went unanswered directly, but the reply that came instead pointed again at the resource this page has already documented as the design's standing unofficial reference: George Van Sickle's own Enigma 460 construction blog, reported to have gone offline, with an offer of the 2012 PDF backup made of it at the time;[130] a further correspondent who had just bought the Paradox plans himself — the same David Krogmann whose purchase advice the group's Facebook side had credited back in January 2025 (see [building plans — a January 2025 recurrence](/references/building-plans.md#a-january-2025-recurrence)) — asked for a copy too, along with the blog's possible Wayback Machine address.[131] The blog turned out not to be gone at all: it was found still live, at `e460.blogspot.com`.[132]

Closing the thread the next day, André-François Bourbeau pointed the correspondent to his own public Facebook page (searchable as "Paradox Sailboat") for the full photographic record of his Enigma's construction, and gave a build-time figure: about four and a half months, working at least three hours a day, six days a week.[133]

## An outside designer sails an Enigma — and capsizes her (March 2016)

The archive's one evaluation of an Enigma by a **working naval architect with no stake in the design** comes from March 2016, when **Graham Byrnes** of B&B Yacht Designs had an Enigma brought to his shop in Vandemere, North Carolina, behind the car of a WaterTribe competitor, and sailed her.[112]

Two findings came out of it. The first is a **measured tacking angle**: "I am still amazed that the chine runners work as well as they do but my best guess was that she tacked through about **120 degrees**." The figure is explicitly an estimate — his GPS battery was flat and he was trying to take readings on a phone from inside the boat, "inside and forward because there is no cockpit." It sits well below the 90–110° that stock Paradox owners report (see [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#pointing-angles-owners-report)), but Byrnes was sailing an unfamiliar boat once, in strong wind, without instruments, and by way of comparison measured his own Core Sound 17 Mk3 at about 100° between tacks in 10 knots the following day.[112]

The second is a **capsize**, and it is the fullest firsthand account on record of what happens when one of Matt Layden's decked micro-cruisers goes over. It was windy, and **during a bad jibe she capsized and "came close to a complete inversion."** Byrnes was inside at the time. He records that **"it would have been easy to swim out through the submerged hatch"** and that there was **"a pocket of air so that I could breathe"** — he stayed in only to keep a phone dry. His companion **worked the boat back onto her side** from outside, a dinghy came alongside, the sail was dropped, and she was bailed out with a cut-down plastic water bottle. The boat was sailed home. Photographs of the capsize accompany the post.[112]

That the boat did **not** self-right from near-inversion with her hatch open is consistent with, rather than contrary to, the design family's stability claims, which are stated for a **closed** boat — compare [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md) and Matt Layden's own standing instruction, given at delivery to a later Enigma owner, **never to sail her without the compartment hatches fitted** (see [A February 2013 flotation retrofit](#a-february-2013-flotation-retrofit-sealed-compartments-tea-jugs-and-secured-gear) above). Byrnes gives the boat's length as **14 ft**, which matches neither the 360 nor the 460; the figure is treated here as an eyeball estimate and is not used as a dimension (see [messing-about.com forums — its limits](/references/messing-about-forum.md#its-limits-and-two-specific-cautions)).

The same visit fed directly into B&B's own chine-runner testing programme — see [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#an-outside-shops-chine-runner-test-programme-2016) — and had been prefaced by Byrnes's observation of a Layden boat under way: **"I never expected them to be that good but I saw one of his boats tacking in light air and it was better than I expected."**[113]

## A third-party clone: "Explorer"

Beyond Matt's own boats and the Enigma 360/460 family documented above, at least one independently built Enigma clone has circulated in private hands under her own name, **"Explorer."** Her builder sold her at the end of his own first outing in her; the buyer later died, and years afterward the original builder reacquired his own boat — a modest, secondhand footnote to the design's reach beyond Matt Layden's own boats, surfacing in the same message as a critique of Michalak's "Robbsboat" and a chine-runner-versus-daggerboard opinion (see [similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-phil-bolger-redesigns)).[44] A further, unbuilt concept along the same lines — an "open source" Enigma clone modeled on Michalak's Robbsboat hull rather than a scratch design — was floated in January 2023 and is recorded in full at the same comparisons page.

Two further, unconfirmed pointers to amateur Enigma-inspired builds surfaced the same week, in reply to the same newcomer's question: a YouTube video showing construction photographs of a boat named **"Southern Cross"**, and a second, hedged report of an Enigma built in Brisbane, Queensland, by scaling dimensions off video and photographs rather than from any plan set. Neither builder is named in the messages, and neither claim carries independent confirmation.[124][125]

## A modern built example: "Goliath"

Beyond Matt's own boats and the Enigma 460, at least one fully documented modern build of the smaller Enigma 360 survives in the Facebook-era record: **[Dave Fahy](/people/dave-fahy.md)**, on Australia's Gold Coast, built and launched his own Enigma 3.6, "**Goliath**," in **March 2021** after an eighteen-month build.[45] Consistent with the light-displacement, crew-weight-as-ballast character noted above, Fahy found his hull tender in early sailing and progressively increased her ballast well beyond his original 50 kg casting, settling on roughly **45 kg of lead each side, amidships** — a substantial addition that steadied the boat measurably and afterward let him sail sitting on the lee side with minimal weight-shift needed in gusts.[46]

## The Enigma family: the 360 and the plywood-cabin 460

The [aiki.pbworks.com wiki](/references/daves-wiki.md) — a prospective Enigma builder's working notebook — gives the fullest technical account of the Enigma family, and distinguishes the original **Enigma 360** (2005) from a later, larger **Enigma 460** (2007) — the numbers denoting each boat's length in centimeters.[49]

### Enigma 360 — fuller particulars and construction

The wiki's 360 figures broadly match the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) ones above, with minor source-to-source variation (it gives length **12' 3"** where microcruising.com gives 11' 10"/3.6 m — 360 cm is ~11' 10", so the shorter figure is likely nearer right; sail area **56 sq ft**, vs. 57–58 elsewhere — independently corroborated on-list[50]; empty weight 180 lb; payload 420 lb; draft 6"; ideal speed **3–4 kt**). The same discrepancy was flagged directly on the list — 12' 3" works out to over 370 cm, against 360 cm being closer to 11' 9¾" — prompting the question of whether anyone had actually measured the boat, restated the next day by pointing back to the same wiki page as the figure's own source without an independent measurement[51], and a follow-up asking whether the 12' 3" figure includes the rudder, which drew a direct answer: **Enigma's length is 12' 3" plus the rudder**.[52][53][54] It adds a construction schedule: **¼" and ⅜" (hull bottom) plywood** with fiberglass — the bottom ⅜" ply under **two layers of 9 oz tight-weave glass** plus chine tapes (6"/4"/2.5"), the sides in **two layers of 3.25 oz glass** lapped onto the deck; no keel; self-righting; ballast by **tubular sand bags secured to the floor**.[55]

A separate line of reasoning reached the same 12' 3" figure independently, worked from the panel layout described in Matt Layden's own *Small Craft Advisor* interview: both of Enigma's sides come out of a single **4 ft by 12.5 ft sheet**, leaving only about **3 in** to take up the curve of the boat's side at a 3' 3" beam — just workable, and consistent with a 12' 3" hull length. That native beam was judged a bit narrow for an average-sized crew member, with **3' 6"** suggested as more comfortable, prompting a joke about how wide a reader would need to be to need it; increasing the draft to **7 in** was floated as a way to take up the extra weight of a larger crew, pushing displacement to roughly **700 lb** and sail area to about **60 sq ft**, while remaining car-toppable in principle.[56][57]

**A professionally built 360, offered for sale (2012).** A partially completed Enigma 360 with a hard top was offered for sale in Fort Pierce, Florida, in July 2012 — correcting an earlier, mistaken report of a *Paradox* for sale, traced to a small classified photo in *Small Craft Advisor* issue 76 that had been misread by its perspective and hard cabin. [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md), relaying the listing, identified the hull as the work of Don McGregor, a well-known professional boat builder in Florida, about 85–90% complete; McGregor was selling because health issues had left him without the energy to finish it, through his son Lew, a longtime family acquaintance, since the builder had no e-mail of his own. Asking **$2,800**, including a trailer and the materials needed to finish the spars.[97]

### Enigma 460 — a longer, plywood-cabin variant (2007)

In **2007** Matt designed the **4.6-metre Enigma 460**, roughly **3 ft longer** than the 360 and — unlike the 360's fold-down canvas dodger — fitted with a **plywood hardtop cabin like Paradox's**. Matt drew it for a builder who built the prototype, was conducting sea trials by **summer 2010**, and documented the build on his own blog; Matt planned to offer the plans for sale once the prototype was proven. Design particulars (per the wiki):[58]

| Particular | Value |
|---|---|
| LOA | 4.6 m / 15' 1" |
| Beam | 1,160 mm / 45.6" (3' 9") |
| Draft | 186 mm / 7⅜" |
| Displacement (DWL) | 458 L / 1,010 lb |
| Empty weight, rigged (est.) | ~120 kg / 260 lb |
| Ballast | min. ~110 lb (est. ~300 lb needed) |
| Max. payload (est.) | ~450 kg / 1,000 lb |
| Sail area | 8.86 m² / ~95 sq ft |

The same particulars were independently restated on-list, corroborating the wiki's figures and adding the maximum-payload estimate above.[59] Against widening Enigma to fit a larger crew, a counter-argument held that cruising clutter — visible accumulating inside the cabin over the course of Matt Layden's own Everglades Challenge video footage — would make life aboard a scaled-up Enigma more restrictive for anyone bigger than Matt Layden's own build, however the raw dimensions worked out.[60] That concern was clarified as being about shoulder room specifically, not seat or beam width.[61]

The **crew compartment** (between the fore and aft bulkheads) is **7' 7"** long, with sitting headroom for a six-footer — "there is going to [be] more interior space than I thought. I can sit upright athwartship with several inches of clearance overhead (I am 6 ft.)." Like the smaller Enigma the 460 has **three watertight compartments** (main cabin plus forward and aft storage) with watertight bulkhead openings. The prototype's construction schedule: bottom **two sheets of 9 mm (⅜") Okoume**, sides two sheets of 6 mm (¼") each, three scarfs; bottom exterior in **two layers of 10 oz glass** (one layer inside), bulkheads glassed 4 oz both sides; the rudder was **CNC-milled**; ballast **220 lb from six steel plates plus 160 lb from two 80-lb sandbags**.[62]

A later on-list restatement of the same comparison put the 4.6 m boat's expected empty weight at roughly 260 lb, against a minimum 110 lb of removable ballast — some 80 lb heavier than the original 3.6 m boat's 180 lb empty weight, buying a good deal more usable space and payload in return — alongside a stated hope of building one eventually and towing her on a small utility trailer.[63] Whether widening the beam at the chine, rather than scaling it up proportionally, would let a lightly loaded 4.6 m boat sail faster or plane on occasion remained an open question, alongside how her higher-aspect chine wings and spray dodger would perform in practice.[64]

### Paradox vs. Enigma 360 vs. Enigma 460

The wiki's own side-by-side comparison (figures rounded/estimated):[65]

| Dimension | Enigma 360 | Enigma 460 | [Paradox](/paradox.md) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | 12' 3" | 15' 1" | 13' 10" |
| Beam | 3' 3" | 3' 9" | 4' 0½" |
| Draft | 6" | 7⅜" | 9" |
| Empty weight | 180 lb | ~260 lb | — |
| Displacement | — | ~1,010 lb | 1,410 lb |
| Ballast | ~100 lb | ~300 lb | ~400 lb |
| Sail area | 56 sq ft | ~95–100 sq ft | 100 sq ft |

The key design contrast the wiki draws: unlike heavy-displacement Paradox, Enigma is a **light-displacement** boat that "relies on her crew's weight as ballast to maintain stability and because of her light displacement she could even plane."[65]

A January 2017 firsthand comparison from an owner of both hulls, **[Chris Curtis](/people/chris-curtis.md)**, sharpened the contrast further: with only about a one-foot difference in waterline length, his Enigma 460 appeared to carry roughly **twice a stock Paradox's interior volume** — though for a solo cruise he was expected to still favor the Paradox, leaving the Enigma at home when sailing with company.[114]

### Chine-runner evolution and reception

On Enigma the [chine runners](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md) took a "new shorter and deeper shape especially created for this lightweight boat" and "have evolved into **higher-aspect foils**," and Matt reported that they seem to be **an improvement over Paradox's runners**. One assessment on record: "I am now convinced that the chinerunner concept, created by Matt in **1982**, is the greatest innovation for small cruising boats in the last century" — dating the concept's *creation* to 1982, three years before the 1985 [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) that microcruising.com calls Matt's first chine-runner boat. The two figures are not necessarily in conflict (conception vs. first built boat), but remain unreconciled.[65]

### Plans

Even before Matt's own April 2006 announcement (below), builders following the 2006 race were already hoping Enigma plans would eventually be sold, and were prepared to buy a set; designers' general exposure to **product-liability litigation** if someone were harmed using a boat built to their plans was pointed to as the likely reason for any hesitation, alongside continued hope that Matt would decide to sell plans given the evident demand.[66]

**Matt Layden** noted in April 2006 that he was "working on an article for Small Craft Advisor with a fairly detailed description of Enigma and her origins" and would "have construction plans out later this year with some minor mods based on experience in the prototype." A letter from Matt to the same effect was relayed to the list — "Builder's plans will be available for Enigma soon… I plan to make some minor changes in the model and draw up building plans later this year" (cf. Matt's later "yes, but it may be a while" answer in [building plans](/references/building-plans.md)).

That article duly appeared as **"Enigma — The Matt Layden Interview"** in the **May 2007 *Small Craft Advisor***. Only a partial scan survives, but in it Matt gives his **fullest first-person account of the chine-runner / lifting-body system** that Enigma and Paradox share (see [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md)).[67]

A year on from that announcement, with plans still unpublished, a further inquiry drew the report that Matt was still working on the design, with interested builders advised to write to him directly.[68] Enigma's small size and modest interior — likened to a smaller Paradox without the fitted-out shelving, and carrying little or no ballast — was viewed as purpose-built for the extreme event Matt entered her in rather than as a general-purpose cruiser, though still judged roomy enough for relaxed, laid-back cruising by a larger sailor; multiple examples were wanted to let a group sail independently rather than crew one larger boat together.[69]

A possible reason for the continued delay surfaced the following week: Matt had meanwhile been **working closely with WaterTribe founder Steve Isaac on the design of Isaac's own Tridarka Raider**, a collaboration disclosed in a *Furled Sails* podcast interview with Isaac — see [Matt Layden — racing beyond Paradox](/matt-layden/biography.md#racing-beyond-paradox) for the full account.[70]

News of the **Enigma 460** prototype reached the list directly at the end of March 2007, relayed from the separate EnigmaBuilders Yahoo group: its builder, prototyping the design himself, gave the project its own blog (`e460.blogspot.com`) to document progress, with plans for the 4.6 m Enigma expected once the prototype was completed and proven — matching Matt's own confirmed intention, at the same time, to eventually produce plans for the original 3.6 m Enigma (Enigma 360) as well.[71] By December 2008, George Van Sickle's Enigma 460 prototype was reported "very close to launching."[72] The Enigma 460 was also recommended directly, in March 2009, as an easier and quicker-to-build alternative to scaling up Paradox itself — provided reinforced with a layer of biax cloth — pointing again to the same construction blog as evidence of a real, if still-prototype, build in progress; a follow-up question asking where the plans could actually be bought drew a direct answer a few days later — not yet, since the design remained at the prototype stage, with plans expected to follow once the prototype itself was proven. See [Building Paradox larger — a 2009 coda](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-2009-coda-two-further-declared-attempts-a-towing-weight-fix-and-the-enigma-460-alternative) for the fuller exchange.[73][74][75] A 2012 secondary source also links the same construction blog under the name "Enigma 260," almost certainly a mislabeling of the 460.[76]

### An unofficial DXF hull layout, and a 2011 update on both Yahoo groups

Independent of Matt Layden's own eventually-published plans, the EnigmaBuilders Yahoo group had at one point circulated a set of DXF files laying out the hull's dimensions and bulkheads; imported into CAD software to work out full-size measurements and transposed onto plywood, with online photographs of Matt Layden building his own Enigma used alongside them as a reference, they let one builder reach hull, bulkheads, cockpit, and deck stage before the build stalled — the remaining mast, sail, steering, and rudder work was to be worked out from the Paradox plans instead — while waiting for Matt to release official Enigma plans, a wait that never resolved; the build was never completed, and by July 2011 the original EnigmaBuilders group itself no longer existed on Yahoo, though the DXF files survived in private hands and were offered around the list again.[86] A successor group, `enigma_sailboat` on Yahoo, had by then already been started to preserve most of the original group's information, the same DXF files included, in its own file section.[87] Whether the DXF files represented Matt Layden's actual design or a fan-made approximation was raised.[88] Gary's own answer, a few days later, was equally uncertain — he does not know the full story behind the specifications, but the boat "works and looks exactly like the Enigma Matt built," and the files "appear to be the real deal."[89] Thanking him, Mark separately reported he was already talking to someone about having a stock Paradox built for him at a reasonable cost, while giving his own long-held impression that Enigma performs better to windward, whatever Paradox's other advantages — wishing he could own both. See [Zoë — considering a commissioned rebuild](/boats/zoe.md#considering-a-commissioned-rebuild) for the fuller arc of that commission.[90]

### A December 2011 recurrence: sea trials completed, still no plans

A further inquiry in December 2011 asked once more whether Enigma 460 plans were becoming available, this time reporting the prototype's own sea trials as completed rather than merely under way, and separately asking whether the draft plans drawn for its builder might be sold as-is, since Paradox plans already covered every element the two boats share; the same message noted the newer `enigma_sailboat` group (already established above) still fields the same plans-status question from its own members every few months.[94] The message had gone out to the whole list rather than to Matt Layden alone, an oversight promptly corrected;[95] a further member asked to be kept posted on the outcome too.[96] Unanswered on record.

### A March 2018 recurrence: a personal reason offered, not a commercial one

By March 2018, a correspondent weighing a secondhand-boat purchase assumed plans still did not exist for any of Matt Layden's other designs once the boat he had been considering sold to someone else first[115] — met with the plainest reason offered on the list to date, without further elaboration: Matt "has not been motivated to do so, for personal reasons," a franker account than the litigation-liability caution and the unfinished-prototype waits offered in earlier years.[116]

### A December 2020 recurrence

The same question resurfaced once more in December 2020, nine years after the previous recorded inquiry — where a set of Enigma plans could be found — and again went unanswered on record, the pattern of an unmet, recurring demand holding through to the archive's most recent pages.[118]

### A January 2021 answer: no official plans, but a builder's own notes online

The question drew a real answer the following month. Another correspondent, replying to the same December 2020 post, reported he too had been unable to find any Enigma plans.[119] Two more replies then settled the matter for good, arriving independently at the same conclusion: no official builder's plans for Enigma were ever issued — Matt Layden's only published construction plans were for Paradox itself, and a builder wanting to reproduce the design would either have to reverse-engineer it from an existing hull or work from the informal notes and photographs already linked above (see [the external construction-photo link](#the-external-construction-photo-link)).[120][121] One of the two, working from exactly that resource and a test model that "looks right," reported starting a lighter Enigma-based boat of his own that winter, wanted for reaching places without a boat ramp.[121] The [aiki.pbworks.com wiki](/references/daves-wiki.md) was pointed to again as the fullest unofficial technical resource on the design, still "no plans, though" — the same reply named an Enigma 460 build blog (already documented above) and reported plywood bought years earlier for an Enigma build being redirected toward a Gorfnik instead.[122] After inquiries recorded across 2007, 2008, 2011 (twice), 2018, and December 2020, this is the first firm, sourced answer the plans question ever received in the archive.

Two years on, that same reverse-engineered build was reported finished: an Enigma completed and sailed through two full seasons, the build itself recalled as a roughly four-month project worked at four to five hours a day, taken up as a pastime during the COVID pandemic.[126]

## Cedar Key trials and the first car-topping test, in Matt Layden's own words

Matt's own account of Enigma's maiden trials survives in full, written up after taking the still-unfinished boat to a small-boat gathering at Cedar Key on 7-8 May 2005: stability and sail-carrying power were "better than expected," she proved unexpectedly dry compared with her "larger cousins," which tend to spit spray from the weather chine in a chop, and she pointed well and tacked reliably in about 12 kt of wind. The same trip doubled as Enigma's first-ever car-topping test. Empty, she weighed **130 lb — "well under the car's rated 220 lb roof load"** — and was not difficult to load single-handed, lifting one end at a time with a temporary roof-bar extension; the rig stowed inside the boat, with portable ballast and loose gear kept low in the car for stability. Loaded, she looked "a little tall but no worse than a small SUV carrying a canoe." Positioned well aft on the car for aerodynamic stability, 300 highway miles home produced "no major handling difference," beyond "a little flutter in truck wakes" — with a tonneau cover, to keep rain and drag out of the open cockpit, noted as a future project. Unloading used a temporary extension bar and a safety line running from the roof rack to the boat's stern to keep her from sliding aft as the bow was swung out and lowered, the flat-bottomed transom then stable on the ground beside the car while the stern followed. Glen Maxwell's own Paradox, "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," was present at the same Cedar Key gathering — Matt's own words call her "big cousin Paradox."[24] A photograph of the same car-topped boat later circulated on the list as direct proof of Enigma's car-topability.[77] A later on-list recollection recalled the same fact independently — Matt Layden having put Enigma on a compact car, "pictures somewhere" — while a pointer to the separate EnigmaBuilders-as-Designed-by-Matt_Layden Yahoo group's own home page supplied a further photograph and reconfirmed her 180 lb empty weight.[78][79] A further recollection specified the loading method itself: Matt used a system of extensible rack beams, so that only one end of Enigma needed lifting at a time — matching the single-handed, lift-one-end-at-a-time technique already given above in Matt's own words.[80]
