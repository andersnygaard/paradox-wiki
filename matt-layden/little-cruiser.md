# Little Cruiser

**Little Cruiser** is a cruising boat whose owners distribute the [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md). She is **"very similar to Paradox"**, a [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) design in the same family along with [Swamp Thing](swamp-thing.md), cruised **two-up** with gear and stores. She has been **rebuilt and improved**, with photos of that rebuild posted to the group files so builders can compare the two designs.[1][2]

Because a similar boat is cruised successfully two-handed, Little Cruiser is repeatedly used as a real-world reference for what a Paradox-type boat can do — including the fact that carrying two people plus gear pushes the boat somewhat over its nominal single-hander [design displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).[1]

## Design particulars

Built in **1988** and nicknamed **"SOST" (Sun of Swamp Thing)**, Little Cruiser was Matt Layden's one departure to a **bow-mounted centerboard** — chosen to maximize interior space rather than [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) — paired with a battened, slab-reefing balanced lug. The particulars recorded on [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) are:[3][4]

| Particular | Metric | Imperial |
|---|---|---|
| LOA | 4.62 m | 15' 2" |
| Beam | 1.4 m | 4' 7" |
| Draft (board up) | 0.23 m | 9" |
| Displacement | 726 kg | 1,600 lb |
| Sail area | 11.6 m² | ~120 sq ft |

The design carries the unusually specific coefficients **Cp .56, D/L 242, disp volume 709 L**. Distinguishing features include **30 gallons of fresh-water ballast in four tanks**; a mast **supported by struts** so it lowers easily for low bridges; **inside steering and reefing**; **foam insulation and flotation** giving positive buoyancy; and **extremely robust construction with a 1"-thick plywood bottom**. The deck-stepped mast uses an "A"-frame tabernacle, and the boat carries a folding dinghy. Matt Layden lived aboard her for four years, sailing the Bay of Fundy to the Bahamas, and wintered aboard in Cape Cod — once **freezing her into a saltwater pond** to test cold-weather living.[3][4] Physical evidence of that winter surfaced years later, after her second owners stripped her paint ahead of the 2005 refit: bow-wave-shaped cuts found up forward, showing Matt had sailed her back out through the ice rather than waiting for a thaw.[59] An early Small Craft Advisor interview describes her as **ballasted with several hundred pounds of lead plus an equal weight of gear stowed securely in the bilges**, with **2" foam insulation for flotation**, and claims ballast and stores right her to **165° of heel**; the water was stored in individual square cubes fitted to the bilge.[5]

Her sail plan invites a further comparison: close to a junk rig but without any sheetlets, a configuration one rule of thumb holds unnecessary below about 100 sq ft — a threshold her own roughly 120 sq ft sail sits just past, leaving open whether a set would help. Her deck-mounted mast, raised and lowered on hardwood struts to clear low bridges, and her large rudder paired with a small forward centerboard — trading chine runners for cabin room enough for two to bed down — together read as close kin to several Phil Bolger designs, Cartopper among them.[58]

## As inspected at purchase in 1992 — and how she got her name

The fullest description of the boat as a buyer first met her was written twenty years later, and it reads as an inventory of why the design convinced them. Her **bottom of 1-inch plywood** served two purposes at once, as **ballast and as protection during hard groundings**. Her **centerboard sat all the way forward in the bow**, unlike most sailboats', which both left the living space unobstructed and formed **a solid partition to which a folding dinghy could be secured**. **Up to 2 inches of foam insulation** gave positive buoyancy if she flooded and kept the interior feeling dry and comfortable in almost any weather. Her **large fresh-water ballast tanks could be topped up through hoses connected directly to drains along the toe rails** — a rain-catchment arrangement whose value the couple only appreciated later, sailing far from civilization where rainwater is the only supply. Under the floorboards was **enormous space for storing cans of food**, with **strong latches** holding everything in place, since the **majority of the boat's ballast was its stores** and a knockdown must not shift them. Steering from **inside a pilothouse** struck them as a luxury; under the **navigator's seat, which doubled as a chart table**, sat a **small stove on gimbals**.[57]

Two things follow from that account. The couple's own contribution before her first season was a refurbishment rather than a rebuild — they **replaced all the running lines**, which fed neatly inside the cabin — and, once everything had been made to look new, **it was [the Bolducs](/people/bolduc.md) who christened her "Little Cruiser."** The name is therefore theirs, not Matt Layden's; his own name for the boat was the **"SOST" (Sun of Swamp Thing)** nickname recorded above.[57]

Her mast is given here as **14 feet**, a figure the design-particulars table does not carry.[57]

## Cruising record and toughness

Little Cruiser changed hands in **1992** and, over the years documented on the owners' site, sailed to the Bahamas **seven more times** — **eight winter Bahamas cruises** plus repeated ICW passages Connecticut↔Florida, **over 10,000 miles in all** (each Bahamas cruise averaging ~800 mi over ~2.5 months). Her one-inch bottom has shrugged off collisions with reefs, jetties, docks, steel pipes, and other boats. On the **1998** Bahamas trip she hit a reef at dusk; next morning there was "a ugly looking 3/8 inch deep gash that was at least 8 inches long in the bottom," yet she sailed on and was **beached** (she has no keel) to repair it at leisure. After twelve years' ownership she was still called "one of the best boats for the job," simple and cheap to maintain and towable behind a small four-cylinder car.[3]

Interviewed in 2006, her owners gave the same tally from memory and made explicit whose trip was whose: Little Cruiser "went to the Bahamas eight times, seven with us and one with Matt," and had also been **sailed engineless to the Bay of Fundy** and through the hurricane described below — the eighth crossing and both of those being [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own voyages, before the 1992 sale. Their own seven trips ran roughly **a thousand miles apiece**, seven thousand miles at least in all.[47][48] Under sail she **averages about 5 knots** and has been kept going **up to twenty-four hours at a stretch** — nothing like the day-in, day-out pace of a WaterTribe racer, but enough to put sixty miles between anchorages.[49] Reefed for a 15 kt breeze on the 2006 Outer Banks cruise, crossing Pamlico Sound from Oriental toward Swan Quarter in a short, steep three-foot chop, she held 4.5-5 kt generally and touched **6 kt surfing** down the occasional wave before settling back to about 5.5 kt.[60]

Her nine-inch draft cuts both ways in daily use. Her owners report running aground *more* often than a deeper boat's crew would rather than less, simply because nine inches invites trying things a keelboat's crew never would; the escape is that with both of them over the side — some **260 lb of crew** — she floats up another inch or two and can be lifted clear. Pinned into the shallows by a stiff breeze there is nothing to do but wait for the tide, which on a flat-bottomed boat at least means sitting upright and comfortable while doing it.[50]

## A hurricane in the Exumas, under Matt Layden

Little Cruiser's hardest recorded night belongs to her first owner rather than her second. Before the boat changed hands, [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) rode out a **late-season hurricane in the Exumas** aboard her — placed by the Bolducs in November, at an anchorage they name on the recording as "Case Key," a spot Matt later pointed out to them himself and which they describe as a perfect hurricane hole. He was lying there in company with other boats when the storm brought winds of **100-plus**; Little Cruiser was **driven off her anchorage and blown back into the mangroves**, and Matt afterward simply **pushed her back out into deep water** by hand. Other boats in the same anchorage were driven aground and stayed there until they were salvaged. (The anchorage name is given only as heard on the recording and is not corroborated elsewhere in the archive.)[47]

The episode shaped the Bolducs' own hurricane doctrine in the opposite direction: their conclusion was that if a hurricane were coming they would leave the boat in the most secure place they could find and go, rather than ride one out afloat in any size of vessel. That resolve was confirmed by what they saw of Hurricane Andrew's aftermath while cruising through shortly after it — Homestead reduced to unrecognizable piles of rubble, and direct hits at Cat Cay and Chub Cay that had leveled both and rolled up the mangroves.[47]

## A gale off Bimini, 1993

Little Cruiser's hardest recorded night under her second owners came the year after they bought her, on the 75-nautical-mile passage from Chub Cay to Bimini. A cold front built the wind overnight from 15-20 kt to at least 40 kt; running downwind under her last reef, she held hull speed (5 kt), surfing regularly in breaking seas for the whole 17-hour crossing. Partway through, Dave Bolduc became too seasick to hold the helm, and Mindy Bolduc took it, seeing the boat and her husband through the rest of the passage herself.[61] Set beside Matt Layden's own hurricane in the Exumas above, it stands as the second of the two most severe weather events in the hull's documented history: one under her first owner, one under her second, both survived without damage worse than a shaken crew.

## Construction scantlings and the 2005 epoxy/fiberglass sheathing refit

Little Cruiser is built of thick **AC-grade plywood**: a **1"-thick bottom**, **1"-thick framing** throughout, **½" plywood sides**, and a **⅜" plywood deck**.[3] The one construction fault on record is that she was **not originally sheathed in epoxy/fiberglass**, so the plywood developed surface checks over time.[6] In **2005** she was stripped to bare wood, sealed in epoxy, and sheathed fully in fiberglass to cut yearly maintenance — the bottom getting **five layers of 10 oz glass**, with **1.7 oz Kevlar leading edges** added to the centerboard and rudder, along with a new copper ground plate and a multi-position Lexan rear window.[3][7] (This is separate from the finding that her original build used generic epoxy with no delamination after ~10 years — see below.) Her quarter-inch Lexan windows had already proven tough in practice: a 17 ft powerboat once struck her broadside, and the window held.[62] The boat returned to full cruising service, **recommissioned in 2006**, marked by a **three-week Outer Banks cruise** — Core Sound, Pamlico Sound, the Neuse River, Croatan Sound, Roanoke Sound, and Albemarle Sound — the first cruise logged after the refit.[8] The owner's own retrospective account calls the work **a year-long refit** and puts the cruise at **275 miles in three weeks**, launched from the couple's usual spot at **Cedar Island in April**: down Core Sound to Cape Lookout and an anchorage in sight of the lighthouse, over to Beaufort, then **sailed and sculled through Adams Creek** — part of the ICW — to the Neuse River and the village of Oriental, sculling a measured **1.8-2.5 kt** over the ground and up to **almost 3 kt** in short bursts along the way,[63] up to Swanquarter before dark, and another seventy miles north on Pamlico Sound to Roanoke Island and finally Silver Lake harbour on Ocracoke Island, the last approach made after dark, a sixty-odd-mile jump that ran out of daylight, felt into an opening perhaps thirty feet wide by listening for where the breaking seas fell quiet and steering for the gap, a technique learned years earlier sailing with Matt Layden and used here in earnest for the first time.[64] Anchored beneath the harbour's own old lighthouse (one of the oldest in the Outer Banks), Dave went sculling around Silver Lake's own calm water the next morning, finding the place changed since an earlier visit thirteen years before, when it had been nearly empty; by 2006 hotels and condos had filled in what was once quiet ground, though it was still worth the stop off-season.[65] By then, he wrote, "long trips were now routine." The "year-long" framing independently supports reading the technical work and the recommissioning as two milestones rather than one contested date.[57] Her sail has its own long history (1988 Matt Layden original → 1994 professional → 1998 owner-made → a 2008 rebuild), documented in [sailmaking](/construction/sailmaking.md).[9]

The refit was planned roughly a year ahead, drawing on the ground-plate and glassing methods used building "Zoë" (full technique detail — copper flashing, mastic sealant, chine reinforcement — lives on [boats/zoe.md — Systems and fit-out](/boats/zoe.md#systems-and-fit-out)). Bonded deck hardware was stripped by heating a thin metal spreader — or the fitting itself, for metal parts — with a blow torch and slipping it underneath, avoiding deck damage, rather than prying off parts bonded with 3M 5200. The glassing plan settled on **6 oz glass on the deck and sides**, **biaxial/mat glass on the bottom**, several layers of **bias-cut 2–4 oz glass** for tricky shapes like the toe rails, and a considered but likely narrowed-down combination of **1.7 oz kevlar with a 2 oz glass overlay** — prized for abrasion resistance while still leaving a sandable layer before reaching the fuzzy kevlar cloth — reserved for just the rudder and centerboard tip given the expense of the fine-weave kevlar, with the rest of the boat finished in plain glass.[10][11] By mid-September 2004 the glassing job itself was underway, with Little Cruiser expected sailing again "once more" by the following summer.[12]

## A mid-October 2004 progress update

By mid-October 2004 the hull was just about ready to glass, with [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md)'s hull being stripped in parallel — around the same time Matt Layden and the [original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md#surviving-hurricanes-frances-and-ivan-2004) came through Hurricanes Frances and Ivan safely.[13]

## No formal plans exist; a cutaway drawing was offered

No plans or drawings were ever made for Little Cruiser. After photos of her interior (looking forward and aft) circulated on the builders' list, a free **complete cutaway drawing of the interior** was offered — for a book then being prepared, provided enough photos, rough sketches, and dimensions were supplied — but it is not recorded whether the drawing was ever completed.[14][15]

The plans question recurred at least twice more, in June 2002 and April 2003, and each time went unanswered beyond the same baseline fact: no formal plans for Little Cruiser exist. Only the basic measurements Matt gave personally are held by the Bolducs, who had "talked to Matt Layden several times about drawing up a set of plans for a larger sharpie," but Matt had not yet produced anything. Only the unfulfilled 2001 offer to draft a cutaway from the owners' own photos and sketches stands on the record.[16] See [stretched Paradox — "Lil Cruiser Plans"](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) for the fuller thread, including a 10-20%-Paradox-stretch recommendation as a practical alternative and an independent 10%-scaling corroboration.

## Chine and rudder cross-pollination with Paradox

Little Cruiser's rudder design was cited as a possible improvement for Paradox: after an unsuccessful [forward chine-extension experiment](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md#a-forward-chine-extension-on-paradox-tried-and-not-recommended) on Paradox, Matt Layden was considering testing a **"Little Cruiser style" rudder** on Paradox, since the balance point of the two rudder types differs. See [rudder](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size).[17]

A newcomer who had read through the archive raised the same idea again in 2006 — whether Little Cruiser's rudder, with its slightly reduced area, might improve Paradox's tacking, and whether her standard gudgeons and pintles (as opposed to Paradox's custom-molded gudgeon) might be a simpler off-the-shelf alternative.[18] Paradox is considered the refined result of a long series of Matt Layden designs, and any change is best discussed with the designer directly before being tried; a professional boat-builder some 30 years experienced, moderating a related small-boats group and himself building a Paradox, was recommended as a further resource, alongside a pointer to the [rudder and chine experiments](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size) page's fuller measured comparison of the two rudder designs.[19] Paradox's rudder works in conjunction with the [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) to provide lateral resistance in place of a keel or centerboard, and her center of effort sits further aft than on most sailing boats — the sail's leech overhangs the transom — so any change risks throwing the finely tuned design out of balance; whatever pintle and gudgeon fittings are chosen, they should be substantial, and bronze made exactly to plan has proved reliable.[20]

The cross-pollination has run the other way too: Matt Layden himself once suggested fitting Little Cruiser with chine runners for the best of both worlds, though the change was never made, her interior space already being at a premium for a microcruiser. [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) made the equivalent change on his own **[Hogfish](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)** designs — the newer of the two carrying a daggerboard as well — and has been happy with the result.[51]

## A 2015 tacking and handling comparison across all four Layden designs

A firsthand comparison across all four of Matt Layden's small-cruiser designs — Paradox, Little Cruiser, Enigma 360, and Swamp Thing — ranked Little Cruiser's own tacking and course-holding directly against her sisters' for the first time. For holding a straight course completely hands-off, Little Cruiser needs "a lot more constant attention" than any of the other three, Paradox winning that comparison "hands down"; for tacking speed the ranking reverses, Little Cruiser coming in second-quickest behind Enigma and Swamp Thing and ahead of only Paradox, the slowest of the four; for cabin comfort, Little Cruiser ties with Paradox at the top of the four, both boats' insulation and ample stowage set against Enigma and Swamp Thing's more cramped, if shallower-draft, cabins.[51] See [operations/sailing-characteristics.md](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#dave-bolduc-directional-stability-as-a-deliberate-trade-off-not-a-flaw) for the fuller four-design comparison, including the Paradox-specific tacking-reluctance debate it bears on.

A newcomer to the mailing list, quoting the same source's own firsthand account of easing rather than slamming the tiller through a Paradox tack (see above), asked in turn how Little Cruiser's bow-mounted centerboard affects her sailing — whether it makes tacking harder than a more centrally-located board would, whether it adds weather helm when reaching, and whether it needs lifting downwind to guard against broaching.[52][53] The bow centerboard likely does make tacking "a little more challenging at times": in strong winds it would be pulled up fully as the boat passed through the eye of the wind, to help the bow blow across onto the new tack — though most of the time she handled just fine regardless. Downwind, the opposite of the question's own assumption held true: sailing offshore in breezy conditions with a big following sea, the board was put **down** rather than lifted, which tracked the boat "straight as an arrow"; the board's other benefit is that it locks the bow in one place while sculling. Whether reaching brings extra weather helm was left unanswered on record.[54]

The bow board's own origin, per the same source, was a deliberate experiment rather than an interior-space choice alone: it was built to test whether any sailing performance could be gained from a bow-mounted board of the kind Sven Yrvind fitted to his own smallest Bris. Matt Layden's own conclusion and this source's are much the same: with this style of boat and a single lug rig, windward performance in deep water is about equal between chine runners and a bow centerboard, and the chine runner wins out in very shallow water.[55]

Trim aboard has always been a shared, point-of-sail-dependent job rather than one crew member working the boat around a passenger: heavy gear is kept amidships at rest, shifted forward when beating and aft when running downwind in heavy air, a joint practice both Bolducs applied together.[66]

## Purchase price and refit cost

Little Cruiser **sold for $1,600**, and she required **some refitting** after **3 years of full-time cruising**. This is the only purchase-price data point on record for Little Cruiser, and it contrasts with the cost for Matt to *build* a Paradox from scratch (~$2,000, 3 months — see [build effort](/construction/build-effort.md)), giving a rough sense of secondhand vs. new-build economics for Layden's small-cruiser designs.[21]

## Sail: larger and more complex than Paradox's

As a comparison point for scaling Paradox's rig to a stretched hull (see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)), Little Cruiser's sail is a useful real-world data point. Little Cruiser is **15' 1" long**, longer than Paradox's stock **13' 10"**, and carries a sail of about **120 sq ft** against Paradox's **100 sq ft** unstretched. That makes her roughly a **10% linear stretch** of Paradox (working out to about 15.2 ft, or 15' 2 1/2", on corrected arithmetic) — close enough in length that her sail is a reasonable real-world data point for what a 10%-stretched Paradox's sail area might need to be. (Compare an independently worked square-root scaling estimate of 100→110 sq ft for the same stretch, in [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).)[22][23]

Reefing is comparatively simple in practice: her stock furling gear needs about **30 seconds** to reef, manageable underway, though Matt reefs and raises Paradox's own sail noticeably faster, evidence that furling speed is as much a matter of operator skill as of the gear itself.[67]

Little Cruiser's sail plan, photographed and posted to the group's files ("David's Pictures"), carries these measurements, all in mm:[23][24]

|---|---|
| Luff | 1900 |
| Yard / Head | 2870 |
| Segmented leech (top to bottom, 3 sections) | 1590, 1615, 1620 |
| Foot | 3560 |
| Overall height (foot + 130) | 3690 |
| Diagonals | 4335, 3920, 3820 |

### Far more complex to build than Paradox's sail

Little Cruiser's sail is **far more complex** to construct than Paradox's simpler sail:[25][26]

- The **leech is segmented** (not a single straight edge), and the sail uses **battens** and **many patches**, all adding significantly to construction time.
- The panels are **vertical** and **broadseamed** (edges cut with progressively more curve to build shape into the flat cloth, rather than cut flat) — see the sailmaking article and rough sketch at `http://home.triad.rr.com/lcruise/reviewb.htm`.
- The sail's **two horizontal seams on the leech are tapered**.
- The **head**, **luff**, and **foot** also have some round (curve) built in.
- The sail is **beautiful to look at**, but gains little in **performance** over Paradox's simpler flat-cut sail.
- The sail was still **evolving**: planned future changes included moving to **5 oz sailcloth**, adding more taper to the leech seams, and moving the forward portion of the top batten to the throat. An intermediate plan floated in 2003 called instead for the same **4.5 oz cloth** Matt Layden used on his own sail, judged more durable and stretch-resistant than the original Sailrite 3.9 oz Dacron.[68] Nearly three years after the original plan, in August 2004, the boat's next sail did in fact carry 5 oz cloth instead, a shorter top batten, and a tighter leech tabling — explicitly **not** adding more draft, sticking with the already-tested design rather than experimenting with more depth (raised at the time as a question of whether Matt Layden's own posted sail dimensions should gain more draft). Matt himself remained happy with his existing sail, while still considering a touch more draft — see [rig and sail — how much draft a Paradox lug sail should carry](/components/rig/index.md#how-much-draft-a-paradox-lug-sail-should-carry) for the Paradox-specific side of that same discussion.[27][28][29]

This is the clearest documented contrast between Paradox's intentionally simple, flat-cut lug sail (see [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md)) and a more elaborate, higher-performance-oriented lug sail on a sibling Layden design.

### A firsthand account of the 1994-to-1998 sail replacement

The bare sail-history note above — 1988 Matt Layden original, 1994 professional, 1998 owner-made — gained its fullest firsthand telling twenty years on. Little Cruiser's original, home-made sail had worn out by the mid-1990s and was replaced with one from a well-known New England sailmaker; that $700 sail, of "fancy" Dacron, was soon found to be cut too flat, and the boat measurably slower for it. Bolduc raised the problem with **Matt Layden** and with **Mark Balogh**, a professional sailmaker and small-boat designer in his own right, and the two of them inspired him to try sewing a replacement himself: he took careful measurements off the faded original, read what few books he could find on the subject — *The Sailmaker's Apprentice* among them — and bought a cheap secondhand commercial machine, a **Singer 143-W2**, to sew it on. The resulting sail was not perfect, but its mistakes were correctable, it carried the boat as swiftly as before, and the process taught him a great deal. His own conclusion is a caution rather than an endorsement: sailmaking isn't for everyone, and for many builders it is wiser to find a sailmaker who genuinely understands what a heavy-displacement microcruiser's sail needs, rather than sew one at home regardless.[56]

## Construction material: generic epoxy, no delamination after ~10 years

Little Cruiser was built using **generic "Defender Industries" epoxy** rather than a name brand like West System — and, after almost **ten years** of hard use, the boat and her spars had experienced **no delamination whatsoever**. Matt Layden is believed to have used this same generic epoxy on all of his earlier boats too, making Little Cruiser's long service life informal evidence that expensive name-brand epoxy is not a strict necessity for a durable small-boat build (see [epoxy brands, chemistry, and cure behavior](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md)).[30]

## Systems and fit-out

Little Cruiser's transom tiller-rope opening is sealed with a vinyl "boot" fitted around the line: a simple fix that has by now worked for **ten years** running, with little water reaching the baffle behind it beyond an occasional splash; a wave breaking squarely on the stern might let in more. A low-stretch double-braided tiller line replaced the original three-strand dacron partway through that span, noticeably tightening the steering.[69][70]

Her auxiliary power has run on two outboards across her ownership: an old 4 hp Evinrude, fed by a direct fuel line needing no pumping, and the current 3.3 hp Mercury, which needs pumping roughly hourly; extra ballast is carried forward to compensate for the newer motor's weight.[71][72] That transom-mounted outboard, visible in a photograph Dave Bolduc sent to designer-voyager [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md), drew Yrvind's one criticism of an otherwise admired boat: "I like everything except for that motor back there. You're not sending a good message." Dave took the point: sailors who tow their boats to Florida behind cars are burning fossil fuel to go sailing either way, he reasoned, while granting that boats of this kind are meant to be driven under human or wind power regardless.[73] Matt Layden's own reaction to the same exchange (and his choice afterward to sail his own Enigma home under her own rig rather than have her trailered) is told in full at [the Bolducs' Matt Layden firsthand record](/people/bolduc/matt-layden-authority.md#the-engine-question-and-a-photograph-sent-to-sven-yrvind).

Little Cruiser's non-skid deck is resurfaced yearly, broadcasting PETTIT 9900 Skidless Compound (a fine silicone-oxide grit) over wet paint with a salt shaker, vacuuming off the loose grit once dry, then recoating; plain sand works too but is harder to remove.[74] Her yuloh-retaining pipe-stowage rails bolt on removably rather than being permanently epoxied.[75] Below, her built-in tank compartments are run as individual-container storage for water and canned goods rather than as a single tank, with a removable, gasket-sealed top.[76] Her own trailer, rated for 1,400 lb, needed welded reinforcement and larger tires after the original skinny tires blew out, against Matt Layden's own solution for his Paradox, a cheap used heavy-duty powerboat trailer bought outright.[77] A personal locator beacon, bought "recently" as of 2006, replaced years of carrying none at all.[78]

## The removable back window: design rationale, and Paradox builders' adaptation ideas

Little Cruiser has always had a **fully removable back window**, in contrast with Matt Layden's choice to make Paradox's own back window **permanent**. The case for a permanent window: a bonded window adds to the cabin's overall structural strength, and a loose or removable window risks letting in a sizeable amount of water around its edges if the boat is pooped or rolled.[31] One detail from that case — using the partly-open window as a ventilation splash guard — was singled out as a particularly good idea.[32]

Weighed against that, Little Cruiser's own long record argues for the opposite choice: in thousands of miles of cruising, mostly along the U.S. East Coast and the Bahamas, she has never been pooped, knocked down, or rolled with her window removable — though bonding the window shut with **3M 5200** would likely be prudent before a long ocean crossing of the Atlantic or Pacific.[31][33] In everyday coastal and island-hopping use, the removable window earns its keep three ways: it makes entering and leaving the boat **easier and safer**, it lets the crew reach across the rear deck to work an outboard's controls, and — most importantly — it lets **far more air** into the cabin, a real comfort gain in the tropics. On balance this is extra comfort at some cost to maximum seaworthiness, and most builders are still better served following Matt Layden's plans and building Paradox's window permanent.[31] The Bolducs' own installation is documented with photos in their 2005 refit pages (cross-linked above as [7]).

Several Paradox builders picked up the idea for their own boats. A **hinged** back window was proposed as a way to combine the ventilation benefit with instant sealing for heavy weather — hinged either at the top (swinging in) or, potentially better, at the **bottom and swinging out**, since a top-hinged window swinging inward risks banging the crew on the back of the head in an unexpected wake or wave. In the bottom-hinge version the window would swing back down and out over the aft deck like a door, doubling as a seat (a similar washboard arrangement had worked this way on another builder's "Folksong," though it was doubted the same trick would suit Paradox's internal line-steering arrangement). One builder working on a boat called "Enuf" took the idea further still: hinge the window from the bottom, swinging down and outward onto the rear deck for easy sealing, while also shrinking the Lexan pane and enlarging the wood frame — potentially building the window entirely of wood with two small fixed ports let into the side supports instead of one large clear pane.[34][35][36][37]

Against the idea, one Paradox builder could not think of a single time he would actually have used a removable window on his own boat: in fine weather the cockpit-less cabin's crew sits outside anyway, and once the weather turns nasty they go inside, at which point a sealed window matters more than an openable one — an owner's-eye counterpoint to the ventilation case above.[38]

A further, later view doubts many builders who have fitted the removable window actually sit at it while under way, since the seat and its own backrest are more comfortable regardless; its real value comes at anchor, sitting with feet up on the seat while chatting to a neighboring boat. For a sailor who really wants air while under way, the simpler alternative on offer is dabbling bare feet in the water over the transom.[39]

The idea resurfaced independently in 2010, framed a little differently: rather than ventilation or safer boarding, the appeal was sitting out on deck with feet still in the cabin for a more open, social feel, and the same practical questions were asked again — how easily the stock design could be modified, and whether waterproofing would be a problem.[42]

Several builders have tried some version of the idea. The closed-in feeling it answers is really only noticed when rain is battering the roof, at which point being sealed inside a dry box is welcome rather than confining; sitting out through an open aft panel this way suits chatting to a passing boat or a neighbour at anchor, but should never be done under way, since it puts a sailor's head directly in the boom's path during an unintentional gybe. One owner weighed the same modification while building his own boat, judged it easy enough to retrofit later if it ever proved wanted, and never has.[43]

Not every owner feels the need for it: the boat's all-round windows already give a 360-degree view open enough on their own, with no boxed-in feeling to answer in the first place.[44]

One owner did build it. Fitting a removable panel at the aft end of the cabin roof was one of only a handful of departures made from the stock plans on "JoHanna," with no certainty yet that it would prove satisfactory and reverting to a permanent panel the stated fallback if not; the modification generated substantially more work than simply following the plans, and was sealed with a labyrinth-type seal expected to keep her fairly watertight, if not quite as secure as a permanently fixed panel would have been. Full build context at [JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md#a-removable-aft-cabin-top-panel-tried-may-2010).[45]

A structural caution followed from outside the Paradox community itself: the stock cabin's strength comes from its being a continuous box shape, so cutting away the rear portion of the roof and window for a removable panel weakens that structure and calls for reinforcing both rear corners of the cabin where the opening is cut — with Little Cruiser's own rear-hatch photographs suggested as a model for how to manage the change.[46]

## Ground tackle example

Little Cruiser's anchoring setup is cited as a model for the Paradox. Her current working ground tackle is a **9 lb Danforth main** and a **25 lb 3-piece Luke storm anchor**, **both stored below the floorboards**; only a **light lunch hook** is kept on the bow, after an 11 lb Bruce on the bow roller was found to alter the boat's trim too much (a 4 lb Fortress had earlier served for short stops). When Little Cruiser was first acquired, the Bruce anchor **simply hung off the stern** — ready to grab and drop when anchoring — before the arrangement was later refined. Matt Layden's stern-throw/walk-forward anchoring method, developed on his own Paradox, has been **used identically and successfully on Little Cruiser**. See [ground tackle](/operations/ground-tackle.md).[40][2][41]
