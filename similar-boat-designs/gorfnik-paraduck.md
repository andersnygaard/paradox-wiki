# Gorfnik

**"Gorfnik"** is a **"Paraduck"** — a modified **Puddle Duck Racer** hull built with a fully enclosed, sitting-headroom cabin — built by **André-François Bourbeau** of **Saguenay, Québec**, who is himself an owner and builder of a [Paradox](/paradox.md). Her cabin resembles a smaller version of Matt Layden's Paradox design, though the hull underneath is a Puddle Duck Racer's, not a Paradox's.[1] She is registered with the Puddle Duck Racer class as **PDR #952**.[2]

## A seventh of a Paradox's weight, and where that lets her go

"Gorfnik" weighs about **200 lb**, against roughly **1,410 lb** for a Paradox — light enough to be launched from an ordinary dinghy trolley rather than a boat trailer, letting her reach put-in spots a Paradox cannot, since a Paradox needs a slipway with vehicle access.[3] Free building plans for "Gorfnik," a 6.3 MB PDF, were made available for download, alongside a set of photographs.[4]

## The designer's own account

Announcing "Gorfnik" to the Paradox builders mailing list in July 2015, ahead of the Bill's Log writeup above, **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)** described her as built for when a Paradox is too much boat — small lakes without a ramp, for example — a cross between a Puddle Duck Racer and a Paradox that took 4 to 6 weeks to build and carries most of the Paradox's advantages in a much simpler build: sailed from inside like a Paradox when it rains, comfortable enough to sleep in, though considerably slower and, for exploring small lakes, none the worse for it.[7] The hull is his own design on the standard PD Racer base, 8 ft long and 4 ft wide, with a reefable sprit sail, and fits in a pickup truck or on a standard trailer — small enough that two could share a single flatbed trailer and split the transport cost between them when sailing together, a consideration that first drew him to the design;[7][43] the name itself was chosen simply for being weird and not already taken.[7]

Writing on the Paradox Sailboat Facebook group, Bourbeau describes "Gorfnik" as his own design: a minimalist, sail-from-inside variant of the **Puddle Duck Racer** hull, carrying over ideas from the Paradox, and he travels with two or sometimes three Gorfniks loaded on the same trailer so friends can come along.[5] His longest trip aboard a Gorfnik has run four weeks, and he finds the type sees more use than his Paradoxes simply because it is smaller and easier to launch.[5] Comparing the two designs after further seasons of sailing, he sums the Gorfnik up as, in effect, a stable, square-shaped live-aboard canoe — roomy enough inside, and stable enough, that he can lean out and wash his hands while cooking — but one with **nil secondary stability**: leaned too far, or caught by a hard puff without releasing the sheet, she can flip instantly and without warning, unlike a boat that heels progressively and gives a sailor time to react.[6]

## Cabin design: from a considered Ocean Explorer to an 18-inch rear cabin

Before settling on "Gorfnik," Bourbeau had considered building an "Ocean Explorer" pocket cruiser instead; he was drawn toward an enclosed rear cabin after seeing John Nabor's "Tenacious Turtle" (built for the Texas 200) and Scott Widmier's Everglades Challenge Puddle Duck Racer, alongside his own Paradox, concluding, like Widmier, that a rear cabin was the only sensible way to make an 8 ft hull liveable.[8] Widmier's Puddle Duck used a 16 in high cabin base and left his knees cramped while sleeping; cardboard mock-ups led Bourbeau to an 18 in high base for "Gorfnik," which also reduced cabin height by 2 in and let the forward window sit at a shallower, better angle for airflow, since the cabin's total height is set by sitting-up headroom.[8] The forward window's angle and placement proved especially important for clearing the knees while sleeping and for reaching the forward space without contortion; balancing the boat was complicated by the reefed sail shifting the center of effort, and making the folding hatch waterproof took repeated attempts.[8]

## Flotation, cockpit width, and early sailing trials

"Gorfnik's" two side flotation boxes, 8 in wide like Widmier's Puddle Duck, leave a 32 in wide cockpit — about the same width as a Paradox's.[8] The flotation chambers are meant, in theory, to keep the cockpit above water if the boat capsizes onto her side; as of the design's announcement this had not yet been tested, pending a deliberate test-flip once the water warmed in northern Québec.[8] On an early trial she went to weather well in 15-knot winds and 2 ft waves, with headroom left to take more.[8]

## Building the cabin panels

"Gorfnik's" hull plan came from the Puddle Duck Racer class's own free plans, credited to David "Shorty" Routh.[9] Her cabin's four identical panels were cut 18 in high from ¼ in fir exterior-grade plywood, then sandwiched together and sanded as one stack so all four came out the same shape, with the fit checked by inverting the panels' order within the sandwich and re-sanding.[9] A router bit with a bearing offers a faster alternative for getting four sides the same shape: shape one panel by hand, then trace and trim each further panel directly off the first with the router, cutting the shaping effort well below sanding a full stack, though it requires owning a router.[38] A handheld circular saw, set so the blade barely cuts through and with the saw's back lifted slightly to steer around tighter radiuses, cuts plywood curves faster than a jigsaw and leaves smoother lines needing little further sanding.[39]

## Reinforcing screw points before assembly, and crucial build measurements

Since screws will not hold in ¼ in plywood, an extra piece of plywood was glued at each of four points that would later need a screw — the inspection hatches, the cabin's front and rear support posts, and the seat — along with support blocks for the port and starboard leeboards; the extra strength there proved necessary once the leeboards struck rocks under sail.[10] Crucial build measurements, taken from the front or rear of the boat: the leeboard pivot centred 47 in from the front; the rear cabin post's rear edge 12 in from the rear; the front cabin post, set at a 40-degree angle at the deck, with its front edge 42 in from the front; the 6 in-diameter inspection/storage hatches centred 56 in from the front; the seat support's top 9 in above the deck and 9 in above the bottom horizontal line, running from 61 in to 79 in from the front; and the back-rest support, at a 12-degree angle, extending 7 in in front of the rear cabin post at the bottom and 1 in at the top.[10]

## Painting flotation chambers before assembly, and a dry summer's test

The insides of the flotation chambers were painted before assembly, easier to reach that way, though not the areas later glued together — painting the non-visible inside chambers guards against condensation eventually rotting the plywood from within. Plywood dividers were also added inside the inspection-hatch storage areas so gear could not roll out of arm's-length reach, with a 1 in gap left at the bottom of each divider so that any water reaching the flotation chambers would flow to where it could be sponged out through the inspection hatches. After a full summer in the water, not a single drop was found there.[11]

## Hull assembly: a standard Puddle Duck Racer procedure

Putting the hull together followed a straightforward, standard PD Racer procedure.[12] The two end pieces were fitted **outside** the side pieces, lengthening the finished boat to **8 ft and a half inch** overall, with the deck sitting ¼ in short at each end to be filled later with an epoxy fillet.[12] The bottom, **3/8 in** plywood against **¼ in** sides, was fastened with stainless steel screws every 6 in and **PL Premium** construction adhesive — judged much easier to work with than epoxy.[12] Framing used **1½ x ¾ in spruce** at deck level and **¾ x ¾ in ash** at the bottom, the ash chosen there for following the hull's bend more easily.[12] Deck supports were cut from leftover oak hardwood flooring — heavier than strictly necessary, but justified by regular use of the front deck for reefing the sail and boarding from a beach.[12] The front deck support, centred 12 in from the bow to line up with the mast (also centred at 12 in), was widened to 3 in and drilled to double as mast support; the rear support's front edge sits 12 in from the transom, butting the rear cabin posts; and the centre support is bevelled 40 degrees at its rear to meet the slanting forward deck posts, its slanted edge falling 42 in from the bow.[12] The front lower transverse framing member is a 1½ in-thick, double-bevelled ash piece, its two bevels following the hull's front and bottom slopes so a stainless steel anchoring bolt can pass through it and tighten horizontally against a flat surface; the same bolt also passes through a triangular, 8 in-long hardwood piece epoxied to the boat's outside centre.[12] A ¾ x ¾ x 12 in hardwood block was added to each corner to take four large carrying handles.[12]

## The mast step, and a watertight mast-tube fitting

The mast step is an **8 x 3 x 1½ in hardwood block**, its underside shaved at an angle to match the hull's curve so its top sits parallel to the deck; it was glued and screwed to a larger plywood patch for a wider support area, held in place with temporary wedges while the epoxy cured.[13] A **1¼ in ABS plumbing cap**, glued to a length of 1¼ in (inside-diameter) ABS pipe standing 1 in proud of the deck, keeps water from entering the boat through the mast hole; inserting the 1¼ in (outside-diameter) aluminium mast forces any water already in the pipe upward and clear — a lesson learned the first time, after a rainstorm, when a fast insertion produced a face-splash, corrected simply by inserting the mast more slowly thereafter.[13] The mast itself measures **12 ft 6 in**, cut down by trial from an initial 14 ft until the sail just cleared head height.[13] A standard, 3.5 lb aluminium tube, tried first, bent too much under load even with an added forestay; it was replaced with a heavier, thicker-walled **20 ft length of 1¼ in best-grade aluminium tube (7 lb)**, which still bends under load but by a judged-correct amount — enough to spill wind in strong gusts.[13] The 3/8 in stainless steel anchoring eye bolt through the forward lower crosspiece must be strong, since it also winches the boat onto its trailer and serves for anchoring and towing.[13]

## Interior paint sequence, and cockpit layout

The hull's interior was painted, two coats, before the deck went on — a sequence that paid off later, since finishing the paint job from inside the closed boat proved possible but no fun under an 18 in-high ceiling.[14] Spots that would later need glue — the three port and starboard uprights, the front and rear cabin framing, and the rear back-rest framing — were deliberately left unpainted.[14] Before painting, a ½ in plywood piece was added at the rear to take the rudder fittings, and a cross piece at the starboard front to take the yuloh holder; the seat supports were then glued and screwed in, notched at their back ends so the seat can flip up into a back-rest for sailing from below — the same detail used on Bourbeau's own Paradox.[14] The deck was glued down with PL Premium and a few stainless steel screws, and the cockpit space cut out the next day: the front corners left round, with the middle front corner set an inch back from the centre framing piece to form two small shelves; a 2 in shelf runs along the sides as an elbow rest while sailing, and a 3 in shelf at the back, extending from the rear transverse deck support, forms a small sloping cubby for odds and ends.[14] As on a Paradox, this all-around shelf collects any window-condensation water and keeps it from dripping into the boat.[14]

## Leeboard-pivot tubing: keeping the flotation chambers dry

Before the deck went on, two 3/8 in (inside-diameter) by 8 in aluminium tubes were installed through the port and starboard flotation chambers to take the two 10 in, 3/8 in stainless steel bolts serving as leeboard pivots — an idea drawn from Scott Widmier — so that any water reaching the pivot bolt is led to the boat's interior rather than into the flotation chamber itself.[15] In practice the fit between tubing and bolt is close enough that not a drop of water has ever entered the boat through the 8 in gap.[15]

## Cabin size as a deliberate compromise against windage

Asked whether the cabin could be extended further forward to add interior space, given the apparent spare room between the cabin's front and the mast, Bourbeau judged the cabin's size as designed the right compromise: keeping it as small as possible minimises windage, and the deck space it leaves free doubles as storage for the hatch in fair weather, a spot for muddy boots, the cooking surface, and anchor storage.[16][17] The cabin's 15 in height likewise balances minimising windage against leaving enough room to stand-up paddle or yuloh the boat.[17]

## Cabin interior: uprights, a back-rest support, and a rear cubicle divider (Step 8)

The cabin uprights were fitted by drilling, then sawing and filing out a tight space for them, gluing each to the sides — left unpainted there for the purpose — and screwing them in against plywood already backed in place for exactly that reason. At the back, a 3 in wide plank, slanted at the front, supports the back-rest/cubby plywood; the back-rest support butts up against the seat support so the seat can flip up into the notch at its end without falling off the back, and at the top is cut at the right angle to glue directly to the rear cabin support. A 10 x 32 in piece of plywood, with a semi-circle cut into it 4 in from the bottom and starting 7 in from each side, divides off a rear cubicle, leaving a 7 ft sleeping space; glued to the back of the rear cabin framing and set 1/2 in off the bottom, it lets the cubicle floor be swept into the main part for pickup with a dustpan, and, by the same reasoning, would let water flow back into the main space for bailing and sponging in case of a capsize.[18]

## A second, independently drawn plan set

Working from the build posts alone, without seeing Bourbeau's own drawing, a fellow builder began drawing up his own full set of "Gorfnik" plans, thanking Bourbeau for the cabin-length detail already given and noting he had not considered the extra area that height adds for wind to catch — having already guessed at his own cabin height and drawn it at 18 in.[19] Checking the sketch against the actual build, Bourbeau confirmed the cabin height at 15 in, the back-rest support extending 6 in above the deck, and the rear-cubicle divider described above.[20] Working the numbers through, the fellow builder reported his cabin supports, seat, and back-rest support probably drawn correctly, with bracing on either side of the leeboard hole still to add; his 18 in sides plus the 15 in cabin gave 33 in of headroom — checked with a yardstick at his desk and judged enough so long as he did not add much of a cushion to sit on — and left a question on record, unanswered, about whether to fit ventilation to match Paradox's own.[21] Reviewing his own latest drawing a few days later, he wondered whether he had drawn the cabin's front and rear support posts too large, at 1½ in square, rather than ¾ in square — answered directly: the cabin frame supports are 1½ x ¾ in, and the backrest support ¾ x ¾ in.[22][25] Days later he uploaded his own finished CAD drawing for the sides and airboxes, made using QCAD, to the group's Files area, crediting the boat as originally built by Bourbeau.[23] Following up the same day, he worked out Gorfnik's total plywood needs as far as he could tell: two sheets of ¼ in for the sides and airboxes, a further full sheet of 3/8 in (possibly ¼ in) for the bottom, and another ¼ in sheet for the decking, without yet figuring how much would be left over from the main hull to build the front and rear ends, the rear storage space, and the cabin covering. He also noted a class advantage the design keeps despite its cabin: unlike the Australian ("Oz") PDRacer variants, Gorfnik remains PD Racer-class legal and could be registered on the class website once built in 3D — raising the possibility of building the bare hull first as a simple PDRacer to sail and test, adding the cabin only afterward. He still did not know when, or even whether, he would start building it himself.[24] Holding off on posting further updated plans until everything is worked out — a couple more changes to the cabin supports, and the window sizes still to add — he plans a shareable PDF of the complete plans once ready, with the cabin top, leeboards, and rudder assembly still to be built; curious how the rudder is controlled from inside, guessing it is with ropes as on a Paradox.[27] Redrawing Gorfnik's own side view to add a window, he wondered whether its placement — starting level with the bottom of the 1 in top support, as drawn — was correct, and worked out for himself along the way that the plan's 1 x 1-1/4 in pieces along the cabin's back top account for the 1/4 in plywood thickness; unanswered on record, he closed by reflecting that drafting up plans from the build posts alone is harder than it looks, and guessed free plans would draw more builders to the design.[28]

## The unfinished plans posted publicly, and a name correction

A week later, the same builder posted his own still-unfinished PDF plans to the group's Files area outright — mostly copy-pasted mailing-list and `microcruising` group messages with his own drawings and calculations added — hoping to draw further interest in "this simpler Paradox-like boat," and intending to post a final version and delete this one once it was ready.[29][30] The reception mixed praise with a correction: since Gorfnik is technically a Puddle Duck Racer under her cabin, cross-posting the whole build series to the PuddleDuck class's own group was suggested, and the plans' credit for the "Tenacious Turtle" was flagged as wrong — her actual builder is Jason Nabors, not John Nabor as first credited above.[31] A corrected edition followed the next day with the name fixed and a little more material added, the earlier file left up rather than deleted right away — an early entry in the same dated-revision naming sequence that runs through to the finished plans linked at the top of this page.[32][33]

## Cabin windows, ventilation, and a hard-wearing bottom (Step 9)

After a break for a week-long canoe trip, cabin plywood went on with cutouts for windows — 7 in openings on the sides, 9½ in in front — with 1 in square top supports doubling as the supports the windows' bottoms screw into, and a ½ in square batten running all around where the deck meets the cabin. At the rear half of the cabin top, three 1¼ x 1 in battens were glued on to block and support the 1 in tall folding hatch to be added later; the extra 2 in this adds raises the rear of the cabin, where the builder's own head sits, to 35 in total (18 in boat, 15 in cabin, 2 in hatch). A 4 x 30 in screened window at the rear stays open at all times for ventilation when riding with the hatch closed, with a 2 in plank inserted at a 45-degree angle to keep water out; since the hatch extends an inch over the rear of the cabin, this alone is enough to keep rain out, needing no separate means of closure. Waterproofing followed: sides and deck painted with epoxy, lightly sanded, then painted with polyurethane, with 4 in wide fiberglass tape added along all edges above the waterline. At the bottom, two 3/8 x 2 in plywood "runners" were added and the entire bottom and all four corners professionally sprayed with Line-X bedliner, giving a bombproof bottom that can be dragged across rocks or cement ramps without a second thought — at a judged cost of 4-5% in speed and about 30-40 lb of added weight, worth it for how hard the boat is meant to be used.[26]

## Leeboard and rudder construction (Step 10)

Both leeboards and the rudder were built from solid black ash, with their working edges covered in 6 oz fibreglass and epoxy — tested against rocks struck at full speed with no damage. Each is weighted to sink instantly rather than float: brass screws driven around the perimeter of a 5 in hole give melted lead (scrapyard car-tire weights, bought for $10) something to key into once poured, melted outdoors over a camp stove and cast after levelling, wearing heavy leather mitts, a face mask, and an apron — bombproof construction that held up undamaged when the leeboards once jammed between rocks in a rapid. Standard kitchen cutting boards, cut into 8-9 in circles, support the swivelling pivot points; their thickness was chosen specifically to compensate for the thickness of the Line-X coating sprayed onto the hull's bottom and up the sides to the waterline (Step 9, above) — without that coating, a thinner material such as a foam "crazy carpet" sled would have done instead. All three foils — both leeboards and the rudder — share an airfoil cross-section. The leeboards run 34 in from pivot to tip (44 in overall, 9½ in at the widest point, from 1 in thick stock at the start); the rudder runs 28 in from pivot to tip (32 in overall, 8 in wide, from 1 in thick stock at the start).[34][35]

Running two leeboards rather than one lets each be used differently by point of sail: a single board, either one, suffices on short tacks or in light wind; on a long tack, especially while reaching, lowering only the leeward board works better; close-hauled in stronger winds, both down make the boat point higher. The weight of a board on each side is also credited with adding stability in heavier weather, besides simply looking better.[34]

## Steering: tiller, swivel, and pulley routing (Step 11)

The tiller is glued and screwed to a solid rudder-support block, which also carries a swim step — judged essential for getting back aboard from the water. The rudder-support swivel is built from two 3/4 in eye bolts and two 3/4 in eye screws through a 3/4 in pipe, originally plain steel (already showing rust) with a change to stainless steel planned. Steering runs on lines exactly as on a Paradox: from the tiller's drilled end, around the cabin through two pulleys fixed to cabin uprights under the front window, then through two more pulleys above the rear deck positioned so the angled line matches the tiller's own arc, carried up to the right height on wooden battens and passed through short brass tubes epoxied in place. Dimensions: steering board 15 x 1¼ x 8 in; tiller 20½ x 1¼ x 1¼ in; swim step 4 x 8 x 1¼ in, foil-shaped in cross section with a rounded front; mainsheet cleat centred 15 in from the rear of the cabin; mainsheet through-point 2 in from the starboard cabin side, 5½ in above deck; steering-line through-points 2 in from the cabin sides, 3 in above deck; pulley/yuloh-pivot battens 1 in wide by 1¾ in high, 11¾ in long to port and 22 in to starboard; eyebolts 2 in and 12 in from the bottom of the steering board.[40]

## A second builder's leeboard shape guidance

Drawing on Bourbeau's own posted dimensions and photographs, a second builder worked up his own leeboard drawing, close to the original as far as he could judge, with rounded corners sized to whatever round can or lid happened to be on hand, or done freehand with a sander — most of the rounding being cosmetic, so if it looks right, it probably is. The forward side, at least underwater, should be rounded, with the airfoil section's thickest point set about a third of the width back from the front (roughly 3⅛-3 3/16 in), tapering the rear side down almost to a point.[36]

## A second builder starts his own Gorfnik

Having spent weeks drawing up his own set of plans from Bourbeau's build posts (above), the same builder made the first cut on plywood for his own Gorfnik on **26 August 2015** — a second, independent build of the design getting under way. Laying out the sides used the standard lofting method: light nails pushed partway into the plywood, with a thin batten bent around them to trace the bottom's curve; the first side cut this way was then used as a pattern to trace the matching shape onto a second sheet for the boat's other side pieces. The centre section, drawn at 18 in, was deliberately left curving a little past the 18 in marks at its widest point rather than cut as a flat strip, avoiding a flat spot that would keep the bottom from fitting exactly; the sides were left rough-cut, to be clamped together and finished to shape as one stack. The build deliberately used ordinary, non-marine-grade plywood rather than the stock spec — reasoned as an inexpensive way to prove out the plans, generate the build photographs still needed to finish drafting them, and get a season's use before the wood gives out, with a full-specification Paradox itself preferred over a marine-ply Gorfnik if spending thousands of dollars either way.[37] A boil test run on a scrap of the same interior-rated plywood beforehand showed a layer could be pried apart with some effort, confirming it as genuinely lower-grade material; unlike a previous boat's underlayment plywood, whose outer face veneer was paper-thin, this cheap plywood's three layers are at least of uniform thickness.[41] No epoxy is planned for the build at all, at most a little fiberglass and polyester resin on the edges if that proves worthwhile — a deliberate trade-off against the intended waters, small rivers, ponds, and a small lake with no current and no waves beyond other boats' wakes, where a plywood failure would be a low-stakes inconvenience rather than a safety concern.[41] The circular-saw technique above proved equally effective cutting a radiused transom on an unrelated boat, though it came too late to use on Gorfnik's own already-cut sides.[42]
