# Paradox compared to other small-boat designs

Paradox has repeatedly come up for comparison against other small cruising and beach-camping designs, most often Jim Michalak's and Phil Bolger's, and also against John Welsford's larger "Swaggie." The comparisons converge on a consistent shape: Paradox trades interior room and build cost for strength, weight, and a closed-cabin, self-righting approach to genuine offshore conditions, while the alternative designs below generally offer more space and an easier build at the cost of some combination of stability, seaworthiness, or beachability. For Paradox's own place in the historical sharpie lineage — a separate question from these cross-design comparisons — see [Sharpie lineage: is Paradox a sharpie?](/design/sharpie-lineage.md), not repeated here.

## Jim Michalak's AF3

Michalak's **AF3** was raised as a similarly beach-landable design offering more interior space, including room enough for a dog in the cockpit.[1] Weighed against Paradox, the AF3 was judged less stable, though possibly a better boat for lying out in the sun; a capsize-recovery account for the AF3 was separately pointed to for context.[2][3] Set against that comparison, Paradox was judged the right boat on the strength of others' experience, if still a little too small for some tastes — prompting, in one case, a suggestion to consider a small catamaran instead.[4]

## Jim Michalak's Robbsboat, and Phil Bolger redesigns

Michalak's **Robbsboat**, published on Duckworks, was separately raised as a possible Paradox alternative — wider for her length than Paradox, and reflective of Michalak's broader habit of reworking other designers' hulls, among them numerous Phil Bolger redesigns.[5] One of those, the **Cormorant** — a 31 ft light expansion of Michalak's own **Caprice** design — was framed as an answer to perceived shortcomings in Bolger's **AS29**: the AS29, though probably more seaworthy, slaps badly at anchor, carries a lot of weight, does not beach well, and steers poorly under power because its motor sits aft of the rudder post. The Cormorant, by contrast, is light, dries out on a beach without crushing its bottom, and locates its motor well forward of the rudder post for more authoritative steering; a long slotted cabin top similar to the Birdwatcher's, with a removable forward panel, gives through-ventilation and standing headroom without a Bolger box's high windage. Michalak's simple, iterative approach to solving these problems — light weight, beachability, and modest seaworthiness for coastal and Bahamas-style cruising — was judged increasingly persuasive on its own terms, without displacing Paradox itself in the comparison.[6] The pointer drew only a brief acknowledgment before that thread moved on to unrelated build progress.[7]

The Robbsboat comparison recurred again in 2008, raised specifically against a headroom worry: offered as a quicker, cheaper "dumbed down" Paradox clone with **42 in** of headroom against Paradox's own roughly 37-39 in.[8] That "dumbed down" characterization drew pushback in turn: any boat is what its builder makes of it, with the level of interior detail left up to the builder to include or leave out rather than being an inherent property of a given design — a Robbsboat, on this view, could in principle be fitted out in just as much detail as Paradox.[9]

The exchange closed on a personal note: pointing out an easier, cheaper alternative was not meant as an insult, and the reply admitted a bias toward Robbsboat, having long thought about building one without yet doing so, while holding that anyone with the determination to finish any boat earns admiration regardless of the design chosen.[10] A further reply added a fuller boatbuilding history: a completed Robbsboat, since sold; a hybrid design under way crossing a Bolger Brick hull with Paradox's own features; and a Matt Layden Enigma clone sold at the end of a first outing and reacquired years later after the buyer's death (see [Enigma — a third-party clone: "Explorer"](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-third-party-clone-explorer) for the full account) — alongside a view that a Robbsboat would out-point Paradox on a chine-runner hull's real limitation against a proper daggerboard, and still calling Paradox a boat he loves.[11]

A further, chine-runner-specific critique of Michalak's own view of chine runners — plus the daggerboard-versus-chine-runner opinion just mentioned — is covered separately at [chine runners: how they work — Jim Michalak's "Robbsboat," and a critique of his view of chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-a-critique-of-his-view-of-chine-runners).

Raised again in December 2009, against a tall/large-sailor fit question: Robbsboat's main interior room is **4 x 8 ft with no shelves**, substantially larger than Paradox's own interior — "much like the Enigma460 is today" — though Paradox remains the nicer boat; with chine runners, a Robbsboat would come close to matching Paradox's own performance on the water (see [crew size and cabin fit — a further height/weight question, answered](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#a-further-heightweight-question-answered-december-2009)).[12]

That comparison drew both doubt and a firsthand rebuttal within days: one view held the design itself unbuilt and therefore untested, reasoning that a boat combining sail-from-inside steering, self-righting, and shoal-draft cruising in one package sits outside Michalak's own design experience, and recommending a design "perfected over long personal experience" instead;[43] another, already holding a set of Robbsboat plans, confirmed no one has yet built one, while judging the design looks like a straightforward build regardless.[44]

That claim was corrected within a day: a Robbsboat has in fact been built, though not yet finished — her chines are already glassed, with the mast, rudder, and cheek complete, and only the leeboard still to make, which will likely be replaced with chine runners instead, judged as easy to build as a leeboard, or easier.[45]

A separate, still-prototype **Michalak 16-footer** shares several AS-29 traits worth noting alongside the Robbsboat comparisons above: an inboard rudder with an end-plate almost identical to the AS-29's, which similarly frees the centerline for a motor in its own free-flooding well; a single offset external board plus provision for a bow centerboard in place of the AS-29's twin bilgeboards; a balanced lug rig with control lines led inside the shelter below deck, as on Paradox; and an open slot-top cabin amidships giving 5½ x 8 x 4½ ft of living space. Judged a good candidate for chine-runner experimentation as well as for Paradox's own roller-reefing sail plan, though not pursued further; the design remained unadvertised, available directly from Michalak for $25.[13]

## The "lake boats, not sea boats" verdict, and a Frolic2 counterexample

The Robbsboat pointer resurfaced in mid-2006 as a cheap, simple alternative buildable in 3/8 in plywood and possibly roomier inside than Paradox, though none of the handful then under construction had yet been finished; Michalak's own building book for the design was again recommended as highly regarded.[14] Michalak himself, asked directly about his own boats, characterized his designs as built for lake sailing rather than genuine sea conditions.[15] That verdict was answered with a counterexample rather than accepted outright: a Michalak **Frolic2** had been entered in the WaterTribe Everglades Challenge — the same ocean-and-mangrove race Paradox and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) have both completed — and finished in good shape, suggesting the lake/sea distinction is not absolute.[16] Pressed on whether Michalak's designs are proven for long-distance, single-handed coastal cruising, on the reasoning that a boat suited to its purpose is surely the better starting point, the point was conceded "somewhat," while maintaining that a sailor can do anything in anything, given enough skill.[17] Pressed further on whether the Frolic2's Everglades finish undercut the "lake boat" verdict, the fuller answer was that her two entrants never really left sheltered water or faced serious seas — Frolic2 remains a comfortable, capable design on her own terms — and that Michalak, like Bolger, tends to describe his own small boats conservatively as a matter of course, plausibly for liability reasons, even though several are self-righting from a 90-degree-plus knockdown and unsinkable unless holed or caught with the hatches open; both designers hold that a boat's seaworthiness is inseparable from her captain's skill and judgment, not a property of the hull alone.[18]

## Matt Layden's own offshore record

Matt Layden's own record of offshore work has been raised directly against that same designer caution. Michalak has said he would not take a Paradox to sea, but by the same reasoning he is not the offshore sailor Matt Layden has proven himself to be: Matt is credited with having sailed, rowed, sculled, and paddled over 25,000 miles in small boats, including one winter spent aboard a boat in ice and snow, and is understood to remain reluctant to publish plans himself for liability reasons — the same caution attributed to Michalak and Bolger above.[19] The general lesson drawn from the comparison is to take capability claims one step at a time, matched against an honest evaluation of the sailor's own experience rather than either bravado or a designer's disclaimer.

## Adapting a Robbsboat toward Paradox's offshore formula

A further technical exchange considered what it would take to adapt Michalak's cheap, simple Robbsboat hull toward Paradox's own offshore-capable formula: adding [chine runners](/chine-runners/index.md) (with or without a leeboard) and the Paradox sail rig was judged a reasonable starting point, along with substituting a steel bottom plate for internal ballast. Matt's own [reefing system](/components/rig/index.md), which closes the boat up as securely as Bolger's own storm-tactic comparison to a corked, sealed bottle once the hatches are fully closed, was judged the piece still missing from a scratch-built alternative, and not something to trust without first learning a new hull's own capabilities incrementally. Set against Paradox, a Robbsboat would be faster and cheaper to build and offers more interior sleeping room, but is neither as strong nor as heavy — a real disadvantage for offshore jaunts, where a collision is more likely to end badly for the boat.[19]

## Phil Bolger's Black Skimmer and Martha Jane

A Robbsboat build was separately weighed against a Bolger-designed **Black Skimmer**, which looked promising but was judged hard to right after a capsize.[20] Bolger's own upgrades to the Black Skimmer since the original plans were flagged as a reason to check with him directly before building — the original design (still the version sold by Dynamite Payson) is comparatively complex to build, has low cabin space, and carries a tall, heavy mast that is not easy to step. Bolger's **Martha Jane**, which supersedes the Black Skimmer, was recommended as more practical, more trailer-friendly, and roomier, if less handsome to look at — fitted with tabernacled masts and water ballast, and, in a later-upgraded form, a pilothouse with standing headroom down the central slot plus sponsons on either side of the cockpit for self-righting from a 90-degree-plus knockdown, along with an improved leeboard design. A Robbsboat, though, would still get a builder out on the water sooner than either Bolger design.[21]

The AS29 recurred again in a 2009 exchange prompted by an unrelated, far larger scaled-up Paradox live-aboard project (see [design/larger-paradox.md — a far larger, unsanctioned build](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-far-larger-unsanctioned-build-doubling-every-stock-dimension-for-a-live-aboard)): offered as a proven 29 ft live-aboard design "similar to a large Paradox,"[22] it drew a question, from a 26 ft sharpie owner unfamiliar with the design, comparing its pictures to a larger version of Bolger's own **Martha Jane** (23 ft) and asking whether the AS29 shares her vertical sides, leeboards, self-righting ability, and blue-water suitability[23] — met only with a pointer to search further, rather than an answer.[24] The same scaled-up project separately drew a mention of the AS29's **walk-through bow transom and cockpit**, offered as a way other "live-aboard" designs address getting back aboard a much larger, higher-freeboard hull from the water.[25]

The same 26 ft sharpie owner returned to the AS29 days later, once another participant suggested its underwater hull shape — judged "much like the paradox" — as a reference for guessing scantlings on a scaled-up Paradox by comparison;[26] in reply, the AS29 was traced to an earlier Bolger design, the **"Jessie Cooper,"** preferred for lacking the AS29's blunt bow (see [design/larger-paradox.md — the wider debate](/design/larger-paradox.md#the-wider-debate-a-do-not-scale-doctrine-and-why-the-interest-in-enlarging-paradox-specifically-persists) for the full exchange and its underlying motive).[27]

The same AS29/"Jessie Cooper" comparison drew a further, chine-runner-specific debate in March 2009, prompted by a new correspondent's dream of a chine-runner, junk-rigged AS-29/Paradox hybrid; see [how chine runners work — a 2009 revisit](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-2009-revisit-a-chine-runner-conversion-floated-for-a-bolger-as-29jesse-cooper-hybrid) for the full exchange.[28]

A firsthand cruising account, posted to a separate Bolger discussion group and relayed to this list, added real sailing data for a Jessie Cooper: her owners cruised her across the **Bay of Biscay** to Spain and on to Germany, finding her load-carrying capacity better than most 26-footers and no worse than a CAL 26, crediting the square hull's genuine stowage advantage; sailed a few inches below her designed waterline for an extended period with no noticeable loss of performance; her bottom built in three layers of 1/2 or 5/8 in plywood, glassed in two layers of 10 oz cloth over the whole hull plus tape at the chines for extra abrasion resistance; and provisioned at one gallon of water per person per day, with a small Katadyn 40 watermaker recommended for an actual Atlantic crossing. A hard-chine hull of Jessie Cooper's own proportions was judged buildable in as little as three months of full-time work.[29] The account's author, posting to this list directly for the first time shortly after, confirmed the connection: having lived aboard Jessie Cooper for nearly five years, he went on to a longer Bolger-designed successor, "Loose Moose 2" (an AS39), later lost, and was by 2009 discussing a third boat, "Loose Moose 3," with [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) — to be a "Hogfish Maximus" of his own; see ["Hogfish Maximus" — the Hogfish family](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#the-hogfish-family) for that thread in full.[30]

## John Welsford's Swaggie

An 18 ft John Welsford design, the **Swaggie**, has been raised as a bigger alternative in the same spirit as Paradox — sharing most of the same traits, but without a water supply replenished by rain.[31] Her draft of about 0.8 m is close to Paradox's own with the rudder down; with the rudder raised, Paradox can sail in under half that depth.[32] Paradox's smaller size, lower cost, and shallower draft were judged the better fit for calm, stump-strewn lake sailing and river cruising, and a Swaggie was thought likely more of a handful to get on and off a trailer than Paradox is with the right trailer.[33][34] Video of the first Swaggie's own launch reinforced that trailering verdict: towing her looked distinctly awkward, needing a substantial truck and trailer and lacking Paradox's easy grace — a case for building a Swaggie only to keep essentially afloat rather than trailer regularly, launched once for an extended ocean cruise rather than towed to and from a home ramp on ordinary outings.[35]

A further reply set Swaggie's own specifications directly against Paradox's: her far deeper 2 ft 8 in draught and over 4,000 lb of traditional-cruiser displacement mark her as a genuinely different kind of boat, where Paradox, heavy for her size, still draws only 9 in. An 18 ft version drawn up from scratch on Paradox's own lines was estimated at around 10 in of draught and 2,400 lb or less, with headroom scarcely more than the stock boat's own — since Paradox was designed for genuinely unsupported cruises of up to two months, where most Paradox and Microcruising list members' own cruising ambitions run closer to a week or two within easy reach of civilization.[36]

A reply to that comparison clarified what its own "close, but no cigar" verdict had meant: a Swaggie's real appeal lay in also being sailed and reefed from inside with a similar underlying stability, not simply in being small for her capability, while still preferring Paradox's own trailerability and expecting to want a Swaggie one day regardless, for a long, unsupported offshore cruise.[37] A follow-up correction narrowed the trailerability verdict to its actual source — video of the first Swaggie's own launch, not firsthand experience — allowing there might be a better way to trailer her than that footage showed, and recalled, without being sure which of two fellow owners had said it, that the matching-draft comparison recorded above had already been made on the list.[38]

The same Swaggie/Birdwatcher pointer recurred once more in a February 2009 "Larger Paradox" exchange, prompted by a newcomer weighing a 28 ft scale-up (see [design/larger-paradox.md — the wider debate](/design/larger-paradox.md#the-wider-debate-a-do-not-scale-doctrine-and-why-the-interest-in-enlarging-paradox-specifically-persists)); the same exchange separately noted John Welsford had drawn a smaller, 21 ft custom design for a named client, "Charlie's Resolution" — a further data point on the range of sizes the Swaggie family covers.[39]

A separate February 2009 recurrence, this time prompted by a headroom question rather than the scale-up debate (see [crew size and cabin fit — a further newcomer's fit question](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#a-further-newcomers-fit-question-and-a-stretched-paradoxswaggie-pointer-february-2009)), placed "Charlie's Resolution" within a wider **"Sundowner" class** of Welsford's own 21-footers, with the class's own construction followed in a "Sundowner Diaries" build log on Welsford's home page alongside the Swaggie's own construction logs and video, some of it posted to YouTube.[40]

## John Welsford's Fafnir

Another Welsford design, the **Fafnir**, has been raised as a bigger, blue-water-oriented alternative — carrying more weight and wider by almost 2 ft, but judged considerably easier to build than Paradox and better suited to open-ocean voyages.[46] Weighed directly against Fafnir, Paradox's near-zero draft was preferred for beaching on sandy shallows, a trait Fafnir does not share.[46]

## A French alternative: the Souriceau 4.75m

The **Souriceau 4.75m**, a French design, was weighed as a further alternative to Paradox — judged, on reflection, not worth its own plan cost for a boat that size, a cost-consciousness that told in Paradox's favor instead.[47]

## A Goat Island Skiff/Paradox hybrid (speculative)

Michael Storer's **Goat Island Skiff** — a 16 ft flat-bottomed hull carrying a sail plan broadly similar to Paradox's own, sold through the same Duckworks outlet as Michalak's designs — was floated as the basis for a hybrid: fitted with a Paradox-style cabin and deck to keep the weather off, an idea raised only in passing rather than pursued in earnest.[41]

## Where the plans are sold: Duckworks Magazine

Duckworks Magazine (`duckworksmagazine.com`) sells Michalak's plans alongside John Welsford's and others', and was the outlet that published the Robbsboat design discussed above; its publisher himself competed in a Frolic2 in the WaterTribe Everglades Challenge.[42]
