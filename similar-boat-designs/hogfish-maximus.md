# "Hogfish Maximus"

**"Hogfish Maximus"** is a roughly 38 ft flat-bottomed, shoal-draft cruising sharpie designed and built by **Chris Morejohn**, described in the Paradox archive as "basically a 38 foot version of Paradox"[1] and best known for crossing the Atlantic in 2004 with Paradox-style [chine runners](/chine-runners/index.md) doing the work of lateral resistance. It is the largest and most-travelled of the boats treated here as kin to Paradox — a full liveaboard hull carrying the same runner-on-a-flat-bottom idea to ocean-crossing scale. Its designer holds that, despite the resemblance, it is a separate design of his own rather than a scaled-up Paradox (see ["Modified Sharpie," not a scaled-up Paradox](#modified-sharpie-not-a-scaled-up-paradox) below).

## The Hogfish family

Chris Morejohn, a Florida Keys boatbuilder and liveaboard cruiser, built a family of large flat-bottomed sharpies and is remembered as "the creator of several large liveaboard sized sharpies as well as some revolutionary flats boats"[2]:

- **"Hogfish Lips"** — a 32 ft sharpie, the boat the Morejohns were living aboard when they were met cruising the Bahamas in 1994 (see [Connection to the Paradox circle](#connection-to-the-paradox-circle) below)[2].
- **"Hogfish Maximus"** — the larger, roughly 38 ft hull, "basically a 38 foot version of Paradox," and the boat that crossed the Atlantic in 2004[1][2].
- **"Jubilee"** — a 40 ft version of the same "Hogfish" design, advertised for sale in 2005 and later photographed on a travelift with its narrow chine runners clearly visible; like "Hogfish Maximus," it combines chine runners with a centerboard[3][4][5].

All three carry **"Paradox"-style chine runners**, the feature that most visibly links the family to Matt Layden's boat[3][4].

A 2009 exchange, prompted by a newcomer's question about who designed "Hogfish" and "Jubilee," added photographs of the chine runners and rudder assembly on the 40 ft "Jubilee" to the group's files section and confirmed the designer as **Chris Morejohn** — see [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) for his own boats and background.[6][7][8]

The same exchange added a fourth name and specification set to the family, not fully reconciled with the account above: an unqualified **"Hogfish"**, launched in 1998 to sail the Florida Keys and reported by 2009 relocated to Portland, Oregon under a new owner, is described as just over 30 ft with a 7 ft beam, drawing 2 ft with her swinging centerboard up and about 6 ft with it down, carrying roughly a ton or more of internal ballast, and ketch-rigged with a leg-o'-mutton mainsail and a jib flown from a bowsprit — whether she carries chine runners at all went unremembered. Whether this "Hogfish" is the same hull as "Hogfish Lips," the 32 ft sharpie the Morejohns were already living aboard by 1994, or a distinct, fourth boat in the family, is not resolved: a 1998 launch date would postdate "Hogfish Lips" already being lived aboard, so if the two names refer to one hull, one of the two dates given in the record is wrong. The same exchange settled **"Hogfish Maximus"**'s own rig, an open question since 2004: she carries chine runners, a forward daggerboard, and a conventional sloop rig. No plans were ever offered for sale for "Hogfish," "Hogfish Maximus," or "Jubilee" — all three hulls were built once and sold outright — and a request for a smaller, 6-8 meter version of the design went undrawn; Chris Morejohn is also credited with designing some commercially successful, mass-produced fishing boats, with no further detail on record.[9]

A follow-up firsthand account resolved the unremembered detail above: the unqualified "Hogfish" does carry chine runners, external chine logs running her full length, and was built very heavily — in the same "NORTHE" ("no other route thought heavy enough") tradition already recorded for [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) — and sailed by Chris Morejohn as far south as the Virgin Islands. Dave Bolduc has himself sailed aboard her, in the Bahamas in a stiff 30-plus-knot breeze and 8 ft seas that she handled admirably, and he and Mindy have long been tempted to buy her whenever she has come on the market, though living far inland they have no real need for a liveaboard boat. Dave Bolduc separately showed Chris Morejohn Matt Layden's own sketches for a 23 ft hull, which Chris judged he could frame up in about two weeks. See [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) for his broader boatbuilding career and his continuing contact with the archive's Glen Maxwell.[10]

## A prospective "Loose Moose 3," and a smaller "Hogfish 18"

In March 2009 a longtime Bolger-boat owner, posting as "Bob" from the Bolger-focused **Loose Moose** blogs (`boatbits.blogspot.com` / `fishingundersail.blogspot.com`), introduced himself to the list directly. Having lived aboard Phil Bolger's "Jessie Cooper" for nearly five years — cruising her across the Bay of Biscay to Spain and on to Germany — he went on to a longer Bolger-designed successor, **"Loose Moose 2"** (an AS39), which was later lost; by 2009, aboard a CAL 34, he was in discussion with Chris Morejohn about a third boat, **"Loose Moose 3,"** to be a "Hogfish Maximus" of his own, having recently visited Morejohn in St Martin and spent time aboard his own HM. He had come to the list originally while researching roller-reefing balanced-lug sails for a planned balanced-lug schooner, having mistaken it for an unrelated group.[11]

Welcomed to the list, he was asked whether a smaller, trailerable Hogfish-type hull — roughly 20 ft, schooner-rigged with roller-reefing lug sails, water ballast, chine runners, and a forward centerboard, along the lines of the Bolger "St Valery" design — might interest him; Bolger's own remarks, relayed secondhand, held that the prevailing economic climate was likely to revive interest in smaller trailerable boats generally.[12] In reply, he judged St Valery "a work of art" but short on accommodation for full-time liveaboard use and no fan of its slot-top cabin, and mentioned in passing that Chris Morejohn also has a **"Hogfish 18"** design "in his back pocket" — a smaller hull, "really sweet," with no further detail or build reported.[13] Asked to hear more, and whether "Hogfish Minimus" might be the right name for it, no further detail on "Hogfish 18" surfaced on record.[14]

## Chine runners at ocean scale — the 2004 Atlantic crossing

In May–June 2004 "Hogfish Maximus" crossed the Atlantic from Florida to **Portugal** by way of **Bermuda and the Azores**, three months allowed for the passage[1]. A mid-crossing report from **Horta, in the Azores** gave the first firsthand account of a chine-runner hull at ocean-crossing scale: the passage never rolled or sank, the leg to Bermuda ran nearly 14 days for 1,070 miles and the leg on to the Azores almost 18 days for 1,890 miles, and — the point of interest for Paradox — **the chine runners worked well even at this larger size, with the daggerboard needed for under 48 hours of the whole passage** (only when hard on the wind), the boat otherwise going to weather fine without it[15]. Loaded to her waterline with a family's tools, spares, and nearly a year's food, she was judged to pound no more than some fin-keeled boats and to roll markedly less than a Hans Christian 38 sailed on the same points of sail[15][16]. The crossing left a settled view that a **21 to 25 ft version of Paradox** would suit ocean voyaging well[15].

For the full passage account, the fuller return verdict, and the list's replies, see [Glen C. Maxwell — Matt Layden eyewitness record](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md#an-atlantic-crossing-aboard-hogfish-maximus), and [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) for how this firsthand ocean-crossing data bears on Paradox's own coastal-versus-offshore classification.

## Chine runners plus a centerboard

The one clear design difference from Paradox — which relies on chine runners alone — is that both "Hogfish Maximus" and "Jubilee" **combine chine runners with a centerboard**[5]. The narrow "wing" runners proved more effective than expected and did not create excessive turbulence even running with the board fully raised; a centerboard is reckoned to become worth adding alongside the runners at around a 25 ft waterline, depending partly on how much offshore sailing is planned[5]. Morejohn's runners are also **not simply scaled-up Paradox runners**: they work from the flow at the chine/hull junction rather than [Matt Layden's lifting-body approach](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md), ride wetter because they do not extend as far aft, and are less aerodynamically shaped and narrower than a runner scaled directly from a Paradox would be[17]. The full runner-sizing discussion is at [how chine runners work — narrow "wing" runners on a Chris Morejohn sharpie, and a centerboard-plus-runner combination](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#narrow-wing-runners-on-a-chris-morejohn-sharpie-and-a-centerboard-plus-runner-combination).

A further view holds that a boat carrying as large a rudder as these do also needs a **bow centerboard** to maintain headway and balance — a point made about both "Hogfish Maximus" and "Jubilee" and paralleled in a *Boats With An Open Mind* write-up on Bolger's Dart Dinghy and Cartopper designs; the same bow appendage, or even a bow rudder, was floated as a possible aid when docking a 40 ft hull under power, though neither point is confirmed against these boats specifically.[6]

## "Modified Sharpie," not a scaled-up Paradox

One design point is on the record directly from the boat's designer: although "Hogfish Maximus" resembles what an enlarged Paradox would look like, it is **not a scaled-up Paradox but a separate design, arrived at through a separate design process**[16]. A firm line is also drawn between these hulls and **traditional sharpies** — which have low sheer and a low center of gravity and sail differently — with the term **"Modified Sharpie"** coined to mark the distinction[16]. For where that distinction sits within the wider question of Paradox's own sharpie ancestry, see [sharpie lineage — whether the scaled-up family still counts as sharpies](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-the-scaled-up-family-still-counts-as-sharpies).

A 2009 exchange, prompted by an unrelated, far larger scaled-up Paradox live-aboard project, recalled "Hogfish" again as a 32 ft chine-runner sailboat worth comparing against — matching "Hogfish Lips" rather than the larger "Hogfish Maximus" — though the thread did not develop the comparison further; see [design/larger-paradox.md — reactions at scale](/design/larger-paradox.md#reactions-at-scale-rudder-and-sail-balance-mast-handling-and-structural-cautions).[18]

A separate February 2009 mention, in the unrelated "vertically challenged" thread, speculated that "Hogfish" — seen only by name on the Bolducs' own website — might be a still-larger boat built on a lot of Paradox's own ideas, and suggested a fellow correspondent look into contacting her owner directly; the guess was never confirmed or corrected on record, but matches the already-documented "Hogfish Maximus"/"Hogfish Lips" family above.[19] A name and rough specification for an unqualified "Hogfish" did surface the following month (see [the Hogfish family](#the-hogfish-family) above) — closer in size to "Hogfish Lips" than to "Hogfish Maximus," though whether it is the same hull as either remains unresolved.

## Connection to the Paradox circle

The link between the Hogfish sharpies and Paradox is not only one of hull shape. Chris Morejohn belongs to the wider circle of small-boat builders around [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) and the [Bolducs](/people/bolduc/dave.md): the Morejohns were met living aboard "Hogfish Lips" during a **1994 joint cruise to the Bahamas made in tandem with Matt Layden**[2]. For the designer's own biography, his other boats and stories (including the well-known photograph of him at the helm of a Paradox on Exuma Sound), and his place in that circle, see [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md).

The Bolducs' recurring temptation to buy into the family, recorded above from 2009, is on record three years earlier. Interviewed in July 2006, they described having seen "a boat called Hogfish that's kind of like a bigger version of our boat" — a **32-footer** — during the previous year, and having weighed buying her, before the arithmetic of a larger boat closed the question: dockage to pay, sails too big, "everything's large." It is the earliest dated instance of that pull, and it names the **32 ft** hull rather than the 38 ft "Hogfish Maximus" — matching "Hogfish Lips," or the unqualified "Hogfish" whose relationship to her remains unresolved above.[20]
