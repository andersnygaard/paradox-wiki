# Lucky Town 7

**"Lucky Town 7"** is a 14 ft trailerable microcruiser designed, built and sailed by her own designer, **William H. Longyard** of Winston-Salem, North Carolina, who later wrote the small-boat-voyaging survey *A Speck on the Sea*. He spent roughly ten years on her design, dissatisfied with the production "mini cruising" boats then on the market and setting out to show what could be done with a 14 ft hull.[1][2] She shares Paradox's central idea of a boat sailed and managed entirely from below, worked out differently: a lifting keel in place of chine runners, and a single polytarp junk sail in place of a balanced lug.[1] Plans were later made available through Duckworks for a nominal charge.[1]

## Design and specifications

| Dimension | Lucky Town 7 |
|---|---|
| Length overall (LOA) | 14 ft |
| Waterline length (LWL) | 13 ft 9 in |
| Beam | 6 ft |
| Sail area | 120 sq ft |
| Lifting keel | 100-265 lb, given as 246 lb by an outside account |
| Draught | 10 in (keel up) / 33 in (keel down) |
| Auxiliary power | 2-6 hp, in practice a 4 hp diesel |
| Displacement | 680 lb |
| Accommodation | Sitting headroom for two, room for a six-footer to stretch out |

She carries a flush toilet and holding tank, with provision made for a shower not yet fitted as of a 2009 account.[2] Her single-cabin layout shares one idea with [Paradox](/paradox.md), sailed and managed entirely from below, while substituting a lifting keel for Paradox's chine runners.[1]

## Rig, power and equipment

Her sail is a single polytarp junk rig, costing about $100 to make and reefed entirely from the cabin, which Longyard rated far friendlier to handle than any rig he had previously used, gaff and gunter included.[2] The rudder is an aluminum kick-up design, and the mast steps on a spring-loaded tabernacle that can be raised or lowered on the water instantly, letting her launch quickly once trailered to the water's edge.[1][2] A 4 hp diesel in the aft compartment charges two marine batteries, which in turn power a 55 lb-thrust electric trolling motor, used judiciously; she also carries an air compressor, a radio bank and a solar panel.[2]

## Motivation and reception

Longyard's own account of why he built her: dissatisfied with the production boats available for what he called "mini cruising," he set out to design and build his own, "to show what could be done with 14 feet."[2] An outside assessment from a fellow builder rated her "high on my list of potential candidates" among boats he might build himself, praising her numerous features "seldom found in production boats of equivalent size."[1]
