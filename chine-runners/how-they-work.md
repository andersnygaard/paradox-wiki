# How chine runners work

The [Paradox](/paradox.md) has **no centerboard or daggerboard**. The lateral resistance that lets it sail to windward instead of sliding sideways comes from **chine runners** — in one compact functional definition, "a wooden foil shape which extends horizontally outward from the chine of the hull," where "the combination of the **air foil shape, the heel of the hull and a large rudder** provide lateral resistance without the need for a centerboard."[1] From firsthand inspection they are "small **two-inch wide horizontal winglets** sticking out from the chines for about **a third of the boat's length**"[2] (see [windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)). They are not a modification layered onto a keel: Paradox was **designed from the start** to use them, and they, the heeled hull, and the big [rudder](/components/rudder.md) together supply **all** of the boat's lateral resistance — there is no separate keel option in the design to compare against.[3]

## Three sources of leeway resistance, not one

The sources converge on the same model: windward ability is a **combination of three cooperating elements**, not a single foil. The boat's leeway resistance "comes from a combination of three sources, **the chinerunners, the lifting-body-shaped hull, and the large rudder**."[2] Matt Layden's own account is the same: the chine runner lets his sharpies "sort of 'fly' upwind through the water **with the aid of a big rudder to reduce leeway**."[4] This is why the chine runners cannot be understood, or judged, in isolation from the hull's heel and the rudder.

## The mechanism: two competing explanations — deflector and foil

There are **two overlapping but genuinely different explanations** of *why* a small horizontal winglet resists leeway so effectively. The design's own longevity has not settled which framing is correct; both explanations follow.

### The deflector view (Newton's third law)

One primary-source hypothesis is that **chine runners work as deflectors, not as hydrofoils**. Deflectors are concave surfaces that redirect what strikes them; by Newton's third law, **the more water the chine runner deflects to leeward, the harder the boat is pushed to windward**. When the boat heels, the hull side together with the runners presents a large lateral area to the flow. On this account the **Bernoulli effect plays no significant role** — unlike an ordinary ballast keel, which works as a lifting foil — and that "may explain the chinerunners' remarkable efficiency and why they are so misunderstood."[5]

This matches an independently given **disc-vs-cup analogy**: a flat disc held square to a moving fluid feels a force as the fluid strikes it and stops, while a hemispherical cup (like half a tennis ball) held the same way feels **roughly twice that force**, because the fluid entering the cup is *deflected back out* rather than merely slowed — the same principle used in a rotating-cup anemometer. The chine runners, on this view, work more like the cup than the disc.[6]

### The hydrodynamic-fence / foil view

Matt Layden's own stated mechanism, given at the 2019 Cedar Key gathering, casts the runner as a **hydrodynamic fence** "with **low pressure on one side and high pressure on the other**," which enables the hull to fly upwind.[4] The "**air foil shape**" definition is in the same family. This pressure-differential framing is closer to how a foil is normally described, and stands in tension with the "Bernoulli plays no significant role" claim — the sources agree completely on *that* the runners work and *that* they need the heeled hull and big rudder, but not on the fluid-dynamic detail of *how*.

Both camps agree the concept is **subtle and easily underestimated**. The runners "are not just add-ons": preventing leeway with them, "like most successful fluid-flow devices — including the bumble bee — is a sophisticated concept," and one that has "been subject to little research and experiment," which is why so few designers have adopted it despite its being in plain sight for a quarter-century.

An independent sail trial, ["Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md), approached this debate from the opposite direction: scepticism that a boat would sail to windward at all on a bare flat bottom, with no keel, centreboard or leeboard, is the design's universal first reaction, and testing that scepticism directly was the trial's declared purpose. Reaction at the sailing club ran sceptical enough that the club president stood a **rescue boat** by for the launch of ["Little Jim"](/boats/little-jim.md) at Chew Valley Lake in October 2006. The result told against the trial's own starting assumption: she made good about **45 degrees to the wind** at close to her **4.5-knot** hull speed. On the question this section exists to answer, the trial reports that **debate continues** over *why* she points so well without a keel — crediting the **chine runners** with a big part of it, and separately noting a theory that the **curvature of the bottom rocker** also helps create lift, a theory that lands closer to the lifting-body/foil explanation above than to the pure deflector account — without adjudicating between the two: "whatever it is, it works." Its own closing judgment reframes the debate rather than settling it: the design's greatest hurdle is not actually going to windward as well as a keelboat, but **willingness to accept that something so different from previously-held belief can work** at all. What this account adds to the ownership and design testimony collected elsewhere in the archive is its evidential character — an outside, sceptical trial conducted under club-level scrutiny, rather than testimony from an interested party.[7]

## A UK owner's sine-wave hypothesis, and a measured heel/wind/displacement optimum

A third, non-designer explanation of the mechanism comes from a UK owner who built and sailed a Paradox for years, offered explicitly as a personal theory rather than a settled account — and it should be read that way, as an **owner's hypothesis**, not as established fact alongside the designer's own testimony above. On this account, the chine runners **"destroy the conventional sine wave through which the boat moves and replaces it with smaller ones,"** so that resistance to forward motion is reduced; when heeled, the leeward side becomes "an effective foil keel," with internal ballast carried to windward performing the function a hiking crew would on a dinghy.[8] The same owner separately credits the runners with **damping roll** when running and at anchor, alongside their leeway-resisting role.[9]

That hypothesis comes bundled with the fullest **measured** heel/wind/displacement optimum on record from any owner, restated twice a few months apart in close but not identical terms: at an angle of heel of about **14 degrees**, in roughly **10 knots of wind (Force 3)**, with the boat suitably ballasted so the windward chine runner is "just breaking the surface of the water," the transom (apart from a smidgen) is clear of the water — read as the boat's optimum sailing trim.[9] A later, more precise restatement of the same figure, on a boat of **640 kg (1,410 lb) displacement**, gives the angle as **14.5 degrees** measured by inclinometer, again at 10 knots (Force 3), again with the windward chine runner skimming the surface as "her optimum" condition; the same restatement adds that with the stem submerged to its planned few inches of depth, the transom presents no drag, which is described as the optimum upwind trim generally.[10] A later handover note to a new owner restates the same finding in operational terms: **"She sails best to windward when the windward chine runner is just clear of the water. You'll need a Force 3 for this."**[11]

An early, generic description of the runners from a prospective builder before he had built his own boat is consistent with, but adds nothing beyond, the fuller accounts above: "narrow fore and aft keels fitted horizontally on both sides of the boat" that, as the boat heels, "help prevent excessive leeway when sailing to windward," and let the boat sail in very shallow water.[12]

A contrast from outside the Paradox family sharpens what the runners specifically add: the West Mersea Duck Punt, a long, lean, open racing punt steered with a short paddle and carrying **no leeboards or keel at all**, gets its own lateral resistance from **hull shape alone** — "very much as per Matt Layden's Paradox, except there are no chine runners" — offered as a real-world example of the same hull-shape principle working, to a lesser degree, without the runners added on top of it.[13] A swing-keel, 26 ft Glen-L-based sharpie offers a further real-world comparison at shoal draft: sailed with the keel retracted (an 11 in draft), she tracks by the vee her hard chine forms in the water when heeled, with no chine runners fitted at all.[14]

## The designer's own account (2007): a lifting-body system

The fullest first-person statement of how the whole system works comes from Matt Layden himself, in a 2007 *Small Craft Advisor* interview. He is emphatic that the chine runners are only **"the most noticed part of the system I've been working on to get leeway resistance and control in sailboats, without need for a centerboard or keel"** — narrow, horizontally protruding ridges running along the middle third of the chines on some of his sharpie hulls, not a foil that does the job on its own. He explicitly corrects the **"minor internet mythology … that chine runners, in themselves, *replace* a centerboard or other conventional foil":** "That's not the case, they're really a fairly minor part of an integrated system." This is the designer's own confirmation of the three-sources model above.[15]

In that system the equivalent of a conventional midship foil is **the hull itself, shaped as a "lifting body"** — a form Layden first met in 1970s *Aviation Week* and *Popular Science* articles on experimental NASA aircraft. Sailing at an angle of attack to the flow (that is, making leeway), the hull develops **high pressure on one side and low pressure on the other, exactly as a wing does**, and the resulting sideways lift resists leeway. This lifting-body hull carries **"half or less of the total leeway load"**; the remainder is taken by the **[rudder](/components/rudder.md)**, sized "as deep and efficient as practical" with "a conventional foil section for good lift across a good range of angles."[15]

Against that hull the **chine runner acts as an aerodynamic "fence"** that separates the high- from the low-pressure side and minimises flow across the chine (its Bolger-derived origin is traced under [origin and evolution](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md)). The hull **"would work decently without them,"** but the **"rough sailing and towing model experiments show a noticeable improvement in pointing and handling with the runners added."** Matt Layden also shapes the chine and bottom rocker to take advantage of the **tip vortex** shed off the end of any lifting surface, which he believes helps reduce pressure on the low-pressure side.[15]

Because the lifting body and the rudder share the leeway load in something like a **50-50, 40-60, or even 30-70 ratio**, the boat's centre of lateral resistance sits **farther aft** than in a conventional sailboat — which in turn wants the **rig placed well aft**, "as you see in my designs" (see [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md)). Matt Layden's own framing therefore sits squarely in the **lifting-body / pressure-differential (foil) family** — the same explanation he would give more briefly at the 2019 Cedar Key gathering (the "hydrodynamic fence" with "low pressure on one side and high pressure on the other," above) — and gives that view its primary-source authority. It stands in acknowledged tension with the "Bernoulli plays no significant role" deflector hypothesis, even though the sources agree completely *that* the runners work and *that* they need the heeled hull and a big rudder.[15]

A 2008 field report added an anecdotal, if imprecise, data point in the same vein: sailing partially reefed in a forecast Force 5-7, "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md#a-partial-reef-account-in-a-force-5-7-and-the-hulls-way-after-masting-down-september-2008)" held over 5 knots on about 2 ft of unrolled luff, and kept moving at 2 knots even with the mast lowered flat and no sail at all, to pass under a bridge — credited, by way of Sven Yrvind's own lifting-body claims for this hull type, to the hull's shape working together with a heavy displacement. Strictly, the lifting-body mechanism above concerns leeway resistance rather than forward glide, so a hull that keeps way on under bare poles is better read as evidence of low drag and heavy displacement than as an independent confirmation of the lifting-body effect specifically — it is included here as the field anecdote it was.[16]

## An earlier designer's quote (2005), posted directly to the list

Two years before the 2007 *Small Craft Advisor* interview, the same description reached the list directly, in the designer's own words: the chine runners, or **winglets**, are **small horizontally protruding fins at the chines amidship** that help the hull function as a **"lifting body"** to resist leeway. Matt Layden himself has said a **centerboard or other retractable vertical fin would do the job better**, but professes "a mental allergy to moving parts and complication." The runners are **easy to build** and **protect the hull rather than weakening it**, as a centerboard trunk would; and they take up **no space in the interior**, always a concern in so small a cruiser. This 2005 statement independently corroborates the fuller 2007 account above — the lifting-body framing, the deliberate trade against a centerboard, and the no-moving-parts philosophy are all already present here, two years earlier.[17]

## Why heavy displacement and heel are essential

A chine runner only works if it is **kept well submerged**, which is why the design depends on the boat's heavy displacement and its behaviour when heeled:

- **Draft grows sharply with heel.** Paradox's draft goes from **9 inches upright to approximately 23 inches at 30° of heel** — the only quantified heeled-draft figure on record — driving the lower runner deep into solid water.[18]
- **The lower runner acts like a shallow fin keel.** Because Paradox carries relatively heavy displacement for her size and her hull has rocker, the heeled runner behaves "much as a **shallow fin keel** would in a v-bottomed boat": the upwind chine clears the water while the lower chine does the work.[19]
- **It needs enough weight aboard.** Performance depends on carrying **enough weight to submerge the lower chine runner when heeled**; an under-loaded boat will not point.[20]
- **Forward speed itself adds lift.** Beyond heel and displacement, a further owner's restatement ties leeway resistance directly to boat speed: higher forward speed is credited with generating more lift from the hull's own lifting-body shape, further cutting leeway — the same mechanism working harder as the boat goes faster.[109] Maintaining good forward speed in the first place is itself necessary for the chine runners to be effective at all.[110]

The requirement reduces to a **geometric one**: the boat must be heavy enough to sink deep enough, and the **beam-to-draft ratio has to fall within a narrow range**. Paradox at **beam 1.23 m, draft 0.23 m** and YRVIND ½ at **beam 1.3 m, draft 0.22 m** are boats deliberately kept inside that window. The payoff is "shallow draft with no moving parts," plus a hull that (like a catamaran) "quickly moves a lot of buoyancy to leeward for a small angle of heel," a large flat interior floor, and easy construction — "almost too good to be true."[20]

The same requirement has been restated independently from the sail-design trade, expressly on Matt Layden's own authority: against a good deal of debate about how effective the runners are, the designer himself points out that they work **only in conjunction with a hull that is deep in the water** — without that depth, the runners are held to be not effective at all. The reading advanced alongside it treats Paradox as applying a traditional principle rather than departing from one: on a traditional deep-hulled yacht the hull itself does most of a modern keel's work, the keel serving as a "stub" that increases the hull's depth and acts "like a 'fence'" to extend what a naval architect would call its span — most of the lift coming from the forebody rather than from area aft. A Falmouth Quay Punt, a working lighter with a comparatively boxy midsection, is offered as the traditional parallel, and Matt Layden's achievement framed as applying that same principle to a boxy, relatively light, much shallower hull.[21]

That general payoff has a concrete, quantified expression: with no centreboard to retract and the rudder raised to the horizontal, Paradox can sail on all points of sailing in under 1 ft of water — a capability that depends specifically on the rudder being raised, not on the hull's draft alone.[22] It sits in useful tension with the heeled-draft figure above: heeled over for windward work, the same boat draws far more than a foot to keep the lower chine runner submerged, while sailed with the rudder up she can cross water under a foot deep on any point of sail.

## Sven Yrvind's seven reasons

The ballasted, **near-vertical** chine runners of the YRVIND ½ — a variant of Matt Layden's horizontal winglets, ~1.2 m long, 10–15 cm deep, each carrying ~40 kg of lead, supplementing a bow centerboard — come with the fullest single enumeration of chine-runner benefits on record, the combined case for the feature:[5]

1. **Leeway resistance by deflection** (the Newtonian mechanism above): heeled, the hull side and runners deflect water to leeward and push the boat up to windward.
2. **Bottom protection.** Like bilge keels, they protect the boat's bottom when it takes the ground at low tide.
3. **Lower ballast.** They let the lead ballast be placed a little lower.
4. **Reduced heeling moment.** They keep the lateral area **high up** near the waterline; just as a high sail-center heels a boat more than a low one, a *low* lateral area would heel the boat more than a high one — so keeping resistance high reduces the heeling moment.
5. **Reduced rolling.** (Paradox's own resistance to rolling is often credited to the chine runners this way, though ballast placement may also play a part — see [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered).)
6. **Less turbulence.** They likely prevent turbulence and vortices forming along the chines, reducing resistance.
7. **A trimaran-like righting effect.** When the boat heels far enough that the windward runner lifts clear of the water, that runner's buoyant volume in effect shifts to the lee side, adding righting moment "kind of like a submerged trimaran that lifts its windward float" — a small effect, but it helps.

"It's the combined advantages of the many small advantages that makes me choose them" — no single one of these is decisive, but together they make the feature worthwhile. Note that reasons 3, 4, and 7 are specific to the **ballasted, near-vertical** adaptation; Paradox's runners are unballasted horizontal winglets.

The runners' bottom-protection role (point 2 above) does not extend to the **topsides**: whether they project far enough to also shield the hull sides from towpath/lock-wall abrasion on extended **UK canal cruising** — closely enough to skip fiberglassing the sides altogether, as considered for [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) — remains open, since the runners' documented protective function is specifically for the **bottom** taking the ground, not the topsides scraping a canal wall (see [epoxy pre-coating technique — a UK builder's cloth-weight questions](/construction/epoxy-coating-technique.md#cloth-weight-canal-abrasion-and-cloth-only-versus-glassed-topsides-questions-from-a-uk-builder) for the fuller thread).[23]

## The waterline question

Under heel the lower chine "effectively increases the waterline length of the boat," producing "a faster, more weatherly boat than it would first appear"[19] — though one specific point needs correcting: the **chine runners themselves do not lengthen the waterline** at any angle of heel or speed, because waterline lengthening is a **bow/stern-overhang** phenomenon — overhangs wetting down under heel or at speed — and Paradox has little net overhang to begin with.[24] Heeled sharpies generally present a longer, narrower hull to the water, though this effect may be less pronounced on Paradox, whose bow and stern usually show clear of the water in photographs.[25] The broader heeled-hull/long-keel analogy still holds, and the runners can be summed up as "sort of like a **Scheel Keel, without the keel**."[24]

Commodore Ralph Munroe's sharpie **Egret** illustrates the overhang principle by contrast: her pronounced after overhang genuinely lengthens her waterline at speed as the bow wave re-crests at the stern and wets the buoyant overhang — a longer waterline that raises speed *potential* without guaranteeing it, since the newly submerged surface is itself a drag penalty (and a transom-hung rudder blade does not count toward waterline length).[26] The real point, restated in sharper form: **Paradox is faster and more weatherly than her static hull shape alone suggests** — a fact owners consistently confirm even as skeptics doubt both her speed and the runners' efficiency from looking at the hull at rest. She would sail faster still with added overhangs and a longer waterline for her beam, but that is not the design's purpose: she is a genuinely efficient sailing package for a one-person skinny-water microcruiser, not a racing machine.[27]

## What the chine runners do *not* do

Heeling itself is **not** countered by the chine runners; it is countered by **hull shape and ballast**. This is the opposite of a keelboat, where the keel *promotes* heeling (the water's force on the immersed keel adds to the sail force) and it is the ballast at the keel's foot or in the bilges that counters it.[3] The same stability picture holds for Paradox: with no heavy ballast keel, her stability comes from a low cruising load and a flat bottom that "a bit like a multihull moves a lot of buoyancy to lee at small angles of heel," while at large angles her righting moment comes from a good height-to-beam ratio and the buoyancy of her superstructure — giving **positive stability up to 165° of heel**, "better than most conventional keel boats."[2] (This independently corroborates the bundle's 165° self-righting figure — see [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).)

## A claimed secondary effect: roll damping when running free

Beyond leeway resistance, the runners are widely credited with a second, distinct effect — **damping roll when running downwind**. The earliest dated firsthand observation of it comes from the December 2001 [Cedar Key to Honeymoon Island sea trial](/boats/zoe.md#sea-trials-route-cedar-key-to-clearwater), and it is notable for having been recorded as a *surprise*. Because the design carries **no boom vang**, the builder had worried throughout construction that the boat would roll her guts out once off the wind. Running free for the first time on that trip at **4.3 kt**, she showed only a very slight and by no means uncomfortable oscillation about the roll axis. Two candidate explanations were offered on the spot and neither was resolved: that the **chine runners were acting as roll dampers**, or that a **balanced lug** rig is simply not prone to inducing roll off the wind.[28]

The archive has never settled which mechanism dominates, and a third candidate has since been advanced: that **athwartship ballast distribution** slows the roll by the radius-of-gyration principle, independently of the runners — see [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md), which notes that the design's well-known resistance to rolling downwind "is often credited to the chine runners" but that ballast placement may contribute too. Roll damping has also been reported at much larger scale, on a roughly 38 ft chine-runner sharpie judged markedly better damped than a Hans Christian 38 (see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md)), which is consistent with a hull-form effect but does not isolate the runners either. Treat the runners-as-roll-dampers explanation as **plausible and repeatedly reported, but not demonstrated against the competing rig and ballast explanations**. A 2008 correspondent, wondering whether a heavier mast would help damp roll, was told no — see [mast — whether a heavier mast reduces rolling](/components/rig/mast.md#whether-a-heavier-mast-reduces-rolling-no) for that answer.

## Runner width, and no published width-versus-leeway trial

Photographs of the runners commonly surprise newcomers expecting something wider: the assumption is often that a functional runner would need to be at least 6 in wide to "dig in" when heeled, yet the CR1/CR2 runners on "Zoë" are much smaller and "stubby" than that — consistent with the **two-inch-wide** figure recorded above.[29] No documented trial quantifying runner width against leeway-resistance efficiency exists; builders' width choices to date rest on following the plans or the eyeballed/scaled judgment of individual builders (see, e.g., fabrication accounts at [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md)), not on a published formula or measured comparison.

## Narrow "wing" runners on a Chris Morejohn sharpie, and a centerboard-plus-runner combination

A 2006 sighting of Chris Morejohn's 40 ft sharpie **"Jubilee"** — a sister hull to "[Hogfish Maximus](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md#an-atlantic-crossing-aboard-hogfish-maximus)," photographed in the group's Hogfish Maximus files folder — raised the runner width question again at a larger scale: her chine runners looked strikingly **narrow** for a 40-footer, prompting the question of whether a hull scaled up from Paradox needs proportionally bigger runners at all. The answer is that the narrow "wing" runners are **more effective than their size suggests** and do not need to be bigger: both "Jubilee" and "Hogfish Maximus" combine chine runners with a **centerboard**, and the combination works well, without excessive turbulence even running downwind with the board fully raised. This is the archive's clearest on-the-record example of a Layden-family hull pairing a **retractable centerboard alongside chine runners** rather than treating them as alternatives. Extrapolating from this, a centerboard becomes worth adding **alongside** chine runners at around a **25 ft waterline**, depending also on how much offshore sailing is planned — with a centerboard additionally useful for trimming a lightly loaded boat, not purely for extra lateral resistance. No formula was offered for exactly how runner size itself should scale with hull size, leaving the [no-published-scaling-formula](#no-published-scaling-formula-and-no-bilgeboardbilge-keel-experiment-on-record) question open at any hull length short of that threshold.[30][31]

A firsthand direct comparison of the two designers' runners, from crewing "Hogfish Maximus" across the Atlantic, settles the point once pressed. Chris Morejohn's runners on "Jubilee" and "Hogfish Maximus" are **"much different" from Matt Layden's**: Morejohn designs from the perspective of the flow at the chine/hull junction itself, rather than from Layden's lifting-body system, and the result is a runner that rides **wetter** than Layden's (confirmed firsthand on the Atlantic crossing) because it does not extend as far aft. Morejohn's runners are also **less aerodynamically shaped**, and narrower than they would be if scaled directly from a Paradox's own proportions — the clearest on-record confirmation that "Jubilee"'s narrow appearance reflects a genuinely different design approach, not merely an error of scale.[32]

The fullest account on record of how the runners' **size and shape changed over time**: on Swamp Thing, Matt Layden's earliest chine-runner boat, they started out **small and square**, and only became larger and more triangular through years of Matt's own trial-and-error sailing and model testing — the shape now seen on Paradox. Some years later Matt built one side of his own Paradox's chine runners noticeably **larger** than the other and sailed the boat for months comparing the stock runner against the enlarged one; he concluded the larger runner's added drag and draft did not improve performance enough to justify the change, and eventually ground the larger runner back down to match the original. This is the fullest on-the-record account of a **controlled runner-size trial**, though it settled a comparison of two specific sizes rather than establishing a general width-versus-leeway formula.[33]

On whether chine runners would help a **dory hull** specifically, the consensus is that they would probably work, though placement would need to shift further amidships (as it already sits on the dory-like Swamp Thing) and the hull would need enough ballast to sit low enough for the runners to be effective.[33] A **sharpie hull suits chine runners far better than a dory hull**, for two compounding reasons rooted in hull geometry:

- **A box hull resists sideways slippage on its own**, before the runners are even added — a property Matt Layden first noticed on Phil Bolger's small box-shaped boats, which also carried exterior chine logs adding further resistance. The closer the angle between the sides and the bottom is to 90°, the stronger this effect; this is likely why hulls with slacker bilges do not use chine runners.[34][35]
- **Paradox's proportionally deep midships hull, combined with heavy rocker equal to the curvature of the sides, lets her immerse far deeper before her gunwales go under** than a "classic" hull shape could manage — creating still more sideways resistance. (See [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md) for the fuller question of what makes a hull like this one a sharpie at all.) A dory of similar size, by contrast, loses most of its hull resistance to slippage because its topsides flare outward, and once enough ballast is added to submerge it deep enough for a keel-equivalent effect, freeboard is largely gone and swamping in a gust becomes a real risk. A dory is also inherently **more tender** to begin with — standing on a dory's gunwale heels it far more than the same test would heel a Paradox — which lets Paradox carry more sail without heeling past the point (just enough to present a "vee" shape underwater) that gives her best performance. Phil Bolger's own words capture the apparent paradox: "What people don't realize is that the shape they don't like is the reason for the performance they do like." For a dory hull on its own terms, a **centerboard or fin keel** is the better solution — Bolger's own later designs mostly abandon chine runners in favor of a centerboard (well forward or off-centered) paired with an oversized, end-plated rudder, the end plate working on the rudder the same way a chine runner works on the hull.[35] Nothing rules out *trying* chine runners on a dory, but no such build or test is recorded.

## A Bolger box-hull-versus-dory-hull design comparison

A published design comparison illustrates the sharpie-versus-dory case above: Phil Bolger's redesign of the **St. Pierre Dory**, given greater freeboard than the type traditionally carries, set alongside Bolger's **"Jesse Cooper,"** a box-hulled sharpie of about the same length and beam and almost the same shape as Paradox. Despite the similar dimensions, the sharpie hull gave its builder-liveaboard considerably more interior room over several years living aboard than the dory hull would have allowed. The comparison also illustrates a limit of the box-hull-without-ballast-depth approach: Jesse Cooper was not ballasted deeply enough to let chine runners work, so she instead carried a large daggerboard (later changed to twin bilgeboards), and needed more ballast than originally specified — some 1,400 lb of concrete and iron in one version sailed offshore. A dory's own best solution remains a fin keel, heavy enough to provide stability low down — illustrated by Annie Hill and Jim Sale's dory-hulled "Badger," which cruised mostly downwind, waiting out unfavorable weather at anchor, and whose owners eventually paid to have a new keel fitted.[36] The comparison does "an excellent job of illustrating how chine runners are best optimized for a heavy, slab-sided boat like Matt's sharpies."[37] "Jesse Cooper" is one of Bolger's "Square Boats" — see a write-up at boatdesign.com and Chapter 12 of Bolger's book *30 Odd Boats* for further reading.[38] See [Glen C. Maxwell — Matt Layden eyewitness record](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md#the-full-return-report-cockpit-size-pounding-and-modified-sharpie-versus-sharpie) for the "Atlantic trip" thread in which the question was asked.

## A 2009 revisit: a chine-runner conversion floated for a Bolger AS-29/"Jesse Cooper" hybrid

A 2009 exchange asked whether Phil Bolger's AS-29 — already covered above as a later refinement of "Jesse Cooper" — could be built with chine runners in place of its own daggerboard, combined with a ketch junk rig. The reply set the idea against Paradox's own success: on a heavily ballasted hull with pronounced bottom rocker, a chine runner traces a curved "half-moon" under heel rather than the straight line it forms on a hull like Paradox's, creating drag rather than clean lateral resistance, and a hull built this way sails well as designed regardless. Jesse Cooper's own heavy, offset daggerboard — a real hazard, risking a serious collision if it struck something solid while fully submerged at speed — was one of the problems the AS-29 was drawn to fix, along with giving the hull a proper place to mount an outboard, which Jesse Cooper lacked; the Advanced Sharpie family (AS-19, AS-29, AS-39) all share the same underlying hull shape.

The case was not closed outright, though: Jesse Cooper and the AS-29 share enough with Paradox — heavy ballast, pronounced rocker, a broadly similar chine shape — that chine runners "might work" and would not be difficult to add, weighed only against the real cost and risk of committing a design this size and expense to an unproven conversion for an uncertain performance gain, and the complication a retrofitted runner could cause the AS-29's own bilge-board slots. The largest hull actually built with working chine runners remains "[Hogfish Maximus](#narrow-wing-runners-on-a-chris-morejohn-sharpie-and-a-centerboard-plus-runner-combination)," which pairs them with a centerboard for exactly this kind of insurance. The general caution drawn from the exchange was that a proven design should not be modified without first understanding every issue the change could raise — the same caution that governs any proposed modification to Paradox herself (see [modifying Paradox](/design/modifying-paradox.md)). A design-purism data point from Bolger's own practice was offered alongside it: when a builder fitted a junk rig to an AS-29 in place of her own designed rig, Bolger held she was no longer properly an AS-29 at all and declined further comment on her; that builder later sold the boat, and her next owner has reportedly considered converting her again, into a trimaran.

No trial of chine runners on a Jesse Cooper/AS-29 hull, or of a Paradox/Bolger-AS-29 hybrid generally, has ever been built or reported — the idea remains speculative. One proposed way to test it cheaply without commitment closed the exchange: glue candidate runners on, then simply grind or cut them back off again if they don't help, since doing so "doesn't seem like it need interfere with the use of the boat in its intended form."[39][40][41][42][43][44]

A further exchange the following day pressed the same idea toward a concrete goal: gluing candidate runners onto an existing bilgeboard-equipped Bolger hull specifically so she could sail with her boards raised in shallow water, the same result Paradox achieves with no board at all.[45] The reply grounded the "half-moon" objection in quantified rocker figures: a 26 ft modified Glen-L sharpie carries only about 4 in of rocker at its center of buoyancy — close to flat, like Paradox — against roughly 24 in on a typical Bolger hull, which is why Bolger's own designs generally rely on leeboards instead, why the Advanced Sharpie family uses twin centerboards, and why that same Glen-L sharpie's own swing keel tracks well even fully retracted.[46]

Asked whether simply lowering the AS-29's own twin bilgeboards partway would reproduce a chine runner's shallow-water effect,[47] the answer distinguished the two mechanisms: a swing-keel sharpie like the Glen-L above is sailed with her keel mostly cranked up, but on any hard-chine hull the chine itself forms a keel-like vee in the water once heeled, which is why that same boat tracks fine with the keel fully retracted. One theory offered for why Matt Layden turned to chine runners at all held that Paradox and Little Cruiser, being so light for their length, simply do not sit as low in the water as a heavier boat would — so the runners exist to extend the chine downward by roughly another 4 in, deep enough to bite.[48]

A more specific version of the glue-on-and-grind-off test was floated for a 28 ft Bolger hull: rather than following Paradox's own proportions, the runners could be built much shorter and wider — roughly five feet long by eight to ten inches wide — avoiding the crescent shape a full-length runner would take on a heavily rockered hull, at the cost of some awkwardness alongside a dock or another vessel. Built from foam covered in fiberglass and epoxy and bonded to the hull, runners of this kind would be easy to shape, light, and easy to remove or modify for a test.[49]

A closer look at the geometry qualified the standing "half-moon" objection. Where a hull's side and bottom meet at a consistent right angle and the two surfaces' curves match, as on the AS-29, the chine — though itself curved fore-and-aft — sweeps through a single flat plane, the way a wide, rounded rudder tip is curved yet still lies in one plane; a runner built along a chine like that would present a straight face to the water regardless of how long or deep it was made, not a crescent. Paradox's own chine, by the same reasoning, is likely not planar in that sense, meaning a runner following it exactly would come out curved even at Paradox's own shallow rocker — while the AS-29's much deeper rocker, precisely because its chine is planar, could in principle produce a straighter runner than Paradox's own, the reverse of what rocker depth alone would suggest. Whether chine runners would actually sail well on an AS-29 was left open regardless of the geometry.[50]

A further comparison judged the Bolger sharpie and Paradox close enough in kind — "basically the same thing" — that chine runners should perform on an AS-29 or a similar Bolger "square boat" about as well as they do on Paradox, differing mainly around the edges; two concrete hull differences were named, a raised bow clear of the water on Bolger's designs against a bow riding close to the surface on Paradox and many other sharpies, and Paradox's rocker peaking aft. Few larger, similarly shoal-draft square-hulled boats exist ready-made to buy instead of building one, leaving something like a MacGregor 26 or a Hunter motorsailer as close to the only options.[51]

A further practical note on the AS-29's own twin boards observed they need lowering only for windward work: raised off the wind, they cut wetted-surface drag and let the boat sail faster — a moving-parts answer to the same leeway problem chine runners solve with none.[52]

Continuing the same exchange days later, a further theory floated the ideal hull shape for chine runners in the first place: a sharpie with a **rectangular cross-section amidships and considerable rocker** — "Jesse Cooper" offered again as the exemplar — while a rounder-bilged design like Bolger's own **"St Valery"** was judged unlikely to work for the purpose, its bilges being rounded off rather than chined.[53]

## A dory-hulled catamaran design using chine runners

A different designer's use of chine runners on a **dory-sided hull** is worth noting: **Jeff Gilbert**, designer of the "Gumboots" catamaran, was trying chine runners on one of that design's two dory-sided hulls (drawn under the working name "Gumboot+Mono").[54] The concept graphically illustrates just how hard it would be to keep such a hull upright, reinforcing the case above that a sharpie hull suits the runners far better than a dory hull does — while a designer might still reasonably want to try it.[55] Gilbert's smaller camping-catamaran design "Hot Chili" is a separate, published example of his catamaran work, with no direct Paradox connection[56]; several of Gilbert's designs (four, by one account) had been built, and he ran his own dedicated Yahoo group[57], with the "Gumboots" design and his regular design column published as Duckworks Magazine features.[58]

## Why speed itself reduces leeway, and a shallow-draft safety case for no keel at all

Beyond the deflector-versus-foil mechanism, the runners' effect compounds with the boat's own forward motion. As the heeled hull's slab side meets resistance from the water, that resistance is forced downward and, on meeting the chine runner, deflected aft — adding a forward push rather than pure drag, so the faster the boat moves the less leeway it experiences. The same principle applies, in reduced form, to a conventional keel; a keel sits more directly under the hull and is less exposed to this downward deflection, so it benefits from the effect less than a chine runner does.[59]

Having no keel hanging below the hull also pays off in exactly the situations a keel is meant to guard against. Caught off a lee shore and lying ahull or reefed down, a chine-runner boat is less likely to be knocked down, since with nothing protruding below she can simply slide sideways instead of tripping over a fixed appendage. Washed up over a reef, she is also less likely to strike it directly, and can often wash all the way onto a beach with little damage short of genuinely treacherous rock; a boat like this can in principle just be hunkered down in until the waves carry her high enough to walk away, where a deeper-draft boat would still be getting bashed apart offshore. Shallow draft of this kind is why lifeboats are traditionally built shallow too.[59]

## The chine-runner curve: cosmetic fabrication choice or hydrodynamic feature?

A build laying up the hull bottom in **sheet metal** rather than plywood raised a fabrication-driven version of the shape question above: does the chine runner's **upward curve at the outer edge** (the bottom's own rocker, carried out into the runner) serve a genuine hydrodynamic purpose, or is it incidental to how the runner is normally built in plywood?[60] The question matters practically for metal construction, since a sheet-metal bottom does not bend to the same curve as readily as **18 mm plywood** does.[61] No builder on record could confirm a hydrodynamic function for the curve specifically (as opposed to the runner's overall presence and outward dimension, [established above](#the-designers-own-account-2007-a-lifting-body-system)): a remark attributed to Matt Layden holds that the runner's **outward dimension matters more than its exact shape**, and two independent guesses — that the curve helps the hull act like a **sled when beaching**, and that it protects the **edge of the fiberglass roving** — were offered without corroboration.[62][63][64] A separate builder considering a stainless-steel bottom raised the same curve problem, without a bending solution of his own.[65] On at least one plywood-built Paradox the upward curve was reportedly **absent entirely**, without any noticed difference in performance, suggesting the curve is not essential to the runners' function even if some benefit cannot be ruled out.[66] Fabricating the curve in metal was suggested via a hardwood pattern (beating or heat-forming the sheet metal around it) or, for thicker stock that will not bend, grinding the shape directly;[62][67] the risk of a gap opening at the chine if a stiff sheet cannot be bent to the bottom's curvature at all was also raised, unresolved for the sheet-metal build in question.[61] See [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md) for the fullest composite/metal-adjacent Paradox build on record, though that boat's bottom is glassfibre-over-foam rather than sheet metal.

A period-literature parallel surfaced once the question widened: Matt Layden's own *Beachcruising and Coastal Camping* records that 1930s Key West sponging dinghies carried a roughly **4-inch-radius "knuckle-V"** on their chine protrusions — a **non-trip chine** — and that a cruising sailboat built with one would surf sideways in a breaking sea rather than tripping over, as an ordinary hard-chine boat would; the same book records that Matt's own next boat after Swamp Thing's capsize while surfing sideways carried **gently radiused chines**.[68] This is the closest thing on record to an explicit prior link between a radiused chine edge and resistance to tripping while surfing sideways, though it was offered as a historical parallel rather than a settled explanation of the Paradox runner's own curve, and it left the specific "upward versus downward convexity" question (below) unanswered for the metal-bottom build in question.

Pressed on which way the curve should face, a sharper version of the puzzle emerged: on the full-scale drawings, the chine runner's outer edge shows a **"downward convexity"** curve of about a **15 mm radius** below the runner — and whether an unintuitive "**upward** convexity" (the opposite curve) would resist a sideways-sliding trip better, reasoning that the sharp upper angle of the drawn ("downward") curve looked more likely to catch than shed a sideways slide.[69][70] The question could not be resolved either way; the sled analogy above assumes the drawn curve behaves like a beaching sled's rocker regardless of which way it is understood to face, while another view simply held that hydrodynamics can defeat naive geometric intuition. Practical fabrication answers still stood regardless: cutting the runners from **solid hardwood and gluing them on** afterward, rather than attempting to bend sheet metal to the compound curve at all, was offered as a straightforward alternative construction path — the approach several plywood builders already use (see [construction technique](/chine-runners/construction.md)).[71] The sheet-metal build, working in **2 mm bronze** (chosen over stainless steel to double as a lightning-protection ground plate, weighing some **70 kg** and glued to the bottom with an elastic epoxy filler — the NM (Nils Malmgren) brand's elastic filler product — rather than mechanical fasteners — see [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md) and [chine runner construction — bronze versus steel](/chine-runners/construction.md#bronze-versus-steel-if-a-builder-insists-on-a-shoe)), found the 15 mm bend very difficult to form in two directions at once even with a neighbor's mechanical-shop help, leaving the question of whether the curve could be reproduced in metal at all still open.[69][72] Once the question was read as being about the hull bottom's overall fore-and-aft curvature, it was clarified to concern specifically the **chine-runner profile's own radius** as drawn in the plans' chine-runner section view, not the bottom's rocker generally — the bottommost layer shown in that section view is fibreglass on the stock plywood design, corresponding to the bronze sheet.[73]

**Resolution:** by October 2004, the bronze-bottomed build had settled the question the practical way rather than the theoretical one — the drawn curvature was excluded entirely and the bronze sheet simply cut straight, with no ill effect and the bottom's bronze surface, if nothing else, attractively "shiny."[74] This is the closest thing on record to a real-world resolution of the multi-week curve debate: a Paradox bottom built with the chine-runner curve omitted altogether performs indistinguishably in practical use, corroborating the earlier report of a plywood-built Paradox missing the curve with no noticed difference (above), and reinforcing that the curve is a refinement rather than a functional requirement of the chine-runner system.

## Whether a flat bottom is essential to the mechanism

A further question about the runners' underlying requirements is whether Paradox's **flat bottom** specifically is essential to the chine runners' success, rather than the runners working on any hull shape. Two historical comparisons bear on it rather than a direct answer: Wharram catamaran builders once tried a similar horizontal-winglet technique, calling them "**vortex generators**," which met a skeptical reception — though the Wharram cat hull (narrow V-shaped) is about as different from Paradox's flat-bottomed shape as could be imagined, so the lukewarm reception of that unrelated hull form does not itself settle the question for a flat-bottomed boat. A related catamaran fitting, marketed as an "anti-vortex panel," raises the same heel-dependency point more directly: since a cat sails flat and upright rather than heeling, such a panel cannot work the way a Paradox chine runner does, whose own effectiveness specifically depends on heel to submerge the lower runner (see [why heavy displacement and heel are essential](#why-heavy-displacement-and-heel-are-essential) above).[75] A period precedent for a genuinely flat-bottomed, fast sailing hull also bears on it: Nathaniel Herreshoff's catamaran **Amaryllis** reportedly had flat-bottomed hulls resembling plywood mixing troughs, and sailed fast enough (under gaff rig) that cats were subsequently banned from competing against monohulls in the racing of her day — evidence that a flat bottom need not be a performance liability in itself, independent of whether it specifically aids chine-runner effectiveness. No documented trial isolating the flat-bottom variable for chine runners answers the question directly.[76]

## Adapting chine runners to a different hull, in place of a leeboard

Simplifying Jim Michalak's **"Jewelbox Jr"** design by fitting Paradox-style chine runners in place of its leeboard draws two cautions rather than encouragement. The first points to **heel angle** as the likely reason chine runners work on Paradox at all: Paradox is narrow enough to heel substantially under sail, which lets the runners act more like a leeboard or side-mounted centerboard — a condition the wider, flatter Jewelbox Jr might not reproduce, making twin fixed fin keels a more promising substitute for that hull instead. The second is a further mismatch: Paradox carries **much less rocker in the bow** than the Jewelbox Jr, so a technique proven on one hull may simply not transfer to the other. Neither amounts to a tested verdict, only a caution that chine runners are not a hull-agnostic fitting.[77][78][79]

A far lighter hull raises the same transplant question in a starker form: converting a **Mirror Dinghy** — at roughly 150 lb, a small fraction of Paradox's loaded displacement — from a daggerboard to chine runners for open-boat beach-camping cruising.[80] Since no consensus exists even for Paradox herself on how much of her own windward ability is the runners specifically versus the heeled hull and rudder together, no firm answer is available for a hull this much lighter and differently proportioned; the practical advice given was to test rather than reason it through — mock up chine runners in sheet polystyrene foam, stick them on with double-sided tape, and see whether they tear off or hold, with a swinging centerboard recommended as the fallback if they under-deliver, since a swinging board is more forgiving of beaching than a fixed one.[81]

## Jim Michalak's "Robbsboat," and a critique of his view of chine runners

Jim Michalak's **"Robbsboat"** is an easier-to-build Paradox knock-off, covered in one of his own design studies, but it resembles Paradox only superficially: Michalak evidently does not believe chine runners actually work, and theorizes that Matt Layden is primarily a downwind sailor willing to spend weeks in port waiting for the right wind. That theory does not match the record — [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md)'s "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" sails well to windward, a fact Glen went to some trouble to document (see [windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)). Paradox is judged a blind spot in an otherwise admired portfolio, compared to Phil Bolger's catamarans, which ignore the accumulated wisdom of multihull designers and are, as a consequence, not among his better designs — every designer has such blind spots. Michalak is credited with having already taken the leeboard from an ineffective nuisance to a pretty-good foil, prompting the further question of how far he might advance horizontal foils generally if he could accept that they work. A separate, lightly built stitch-and-glue Paradox — built lighter than the plans specify — was reported as still fun to sail, and preferred over the Robbsboat precisely because its hull shape is the designer's own proven one, even under-built, rather than a knock-off.[82]

A further, later critique aims at the chine runners specifically rather than at Robbsboat's own design philosophy: a chine-runner hull, on this view, simply cannot out-point a proper daggerboard — the one real complaint set against an otherwise-declared love of Paradox from a builder who has owned a Robbsboat, an Enigma clone, and a Paradox-based hybrid design of his own.[83]

## Analogies and speculation

A handful of further ideas about chine runners have been floated over the years without ever being built or tested. Gathered together here rather than scattered as separate asides, they add intuition but not settled fact.

<a id="a-snowboardski-edge-wear-analogy-speculative"></a>
- **A ski/snowboard-edge analogy.** If a chine runner behaves hydrodynamically the way a ski edge behaves on snow, wear should concentrate near the runner's fore-and-aft tips rather than along its middle — suggesting a runner shaped like a long, subtle, mirrored hourglass (narrower at the tips) might be more efficient than a constant-width winglet. No tapered or hourglass-profile runner has been built or tested.[84]
<a id="a-kayak-chine-analogy-for-the-deflector-view"></a>
- **A kayak-chine analogy for the deflector view.** Leaning a hard-chine kayak digs its curved chine into the water and steers the boat toward that side. By the same logic, a heeled Paradox's flat side holds water against the bottom rather than letting it spill over the top — much as Phil Bolger's bottom-plated rudders are shaped to let water "stick" to the blade's lower edge — giving the whole side steering/lateral-resistance value, with the runner's upper angle catching the water. On this reasoning, sailed upright in conditions that could trip her, a Paradox should present the runner's rounded lower radius to the flow rather than its sharper upper edge, letting water slide underneath rather than catch on top. Real questions remain unresolved: whether the runner is fully immersed or only partly in the water when the boat skids sideways down a wave face; whether flow along each face is laminar or turbulent and whether curvature affects the resulting pressure difference at all; and whether the ordinary hull-tripping mechanism — the water's surface layer moving faster than the water beneath it — even applies to a hull that might simply flow along with the surface water instead of resisting it, which would make the runner's curve moot in exactly the condition it is meant to help with.[85][86]
<a id="a-bolt-on-hydrofoil-fin-idea-considered-and-dropped"></a>
- **A bolt-on hydrofoil fin, considered and dropped.** A small bolt-on hydrofoil (roughly 100 sq in or less), mounted on threaded inserts so it could be unscrewed and plugged when not wanted, was proposed for motoring speed and efficiency in an emergency rather than for sailing — a genuinely effective sailing foil being conceded impractical given the added weight and limited space aboard a boat this size. It drew no development beyond a round of general hydrofoil reference links shared for background reading. This is a distinct proposal from the [chine runners](#the-mechanism-two-competing-explanations-deflector-and-foil) themselves, which already provide the boat's lateral resistance and are not hydrofoils in this lift-generating sense.[87][88][89]
<a id="placement-on-a-water-ballasted-box-keel-hull-an-outside-question"></a>
- **Where runners would belong on a water-ballasted box-keel hull.** For a hull form unrelated to Paradox's sharpie shape — a water-ballasted boat with a box keel — whether chine runners belong at the hull's own chines or instead at the sides of the box keel is an open question that hinges on which of the deflector/foil theories above actually governs their effect: under one theory placement would not matter between the two locations, under the other neither location would work well, since a box keel introduces a second pair of vertical surfaces with no equivalent on a Paradox-family hull.[90][91]
<a id="no-published-scaling-formula-and-no-bilgeboardbilge-keel-experiment-on-record"></a>
- **No scaling formula, and no bilgeboard/bilge-keel experiment on record.** No formula for scaling chine-runner size to a larger hull has ever been attributed to Matt Layden — how much bigger a runner would need to be on a hull roughly 75% larger than Paradox on the same basic shape (as with Phil Bolger's Martha Jane) remains unanswered, given that effectiveness also depends on proper hull depth and adequate rudder area. Whether Matt Layden ever tried bilgeboards or bilge keels, as used on a number of British and Dutch small craft, as an alternative to chine runners or leeboards/centerboards is likewise unrecorded.[92]

## A rocker/crescent misconception, and the multihull question restated

Because the chine runners cover only about a third of the boat's length rather than the full chine, they do not trace the crescent shape the hull's rocker would otherwise put through a full-length runner — worth stating plainly, since the rocker can look like a problem to anyone assuming the runners run bow to stern.[93] The rudder's own role in the system is also easy to underrate from the chine runners alone: the 50-50/40-60/30-70 lifting-body-to-rudder load split has been on record since the designer's 2007 account above, but it is a detail that reading about the runners in isolation can miss.[94] On multihulls, chine runners are judged unlikely to work at all — consistent with the anti-vortex-panel distinction already drawn above — though vortex generators specifically may fare better.[94]

## A December 2009 debate: the Triloboat 16's negative result, and Matt's own explanation restated

Whether chine runners would help a lighter, easily-transported hull — a PDRacer or a Robbsboat — was pressed again from a fresh angle: the case against rests on the same displacement/rocker requirement already established [above](#why-heavy-displacement-and-heel-are-essential), read as running counter to a light, easily-transported design's own goals, with both Matt Layden and Jim Michalak held to already understand as much. The *Small Craft Advisor* interview extract already read on Sven Yrvind's website that May (see [above](#a-rockercrescent-misconception-and-the-multihull-question-restated)) was reported to have since been taken down from the site.[95]

A real-world test bears on the PDRacer/light-hull question directly: builders of the **Triloboat 16** fitted chine runners of their own and judged them ineffective, in the designer's own secondhand-relayed suspicion because they sat too close to the surface — not deep enough to grip; the article was pinpointed to the May 2009 archive on Sven Yrvind's site, and a further intuition held that a submerged, gripping bow may matter to the whole system working, which a boxy hull cannot provide.[96]

Applied specifically to Robbsboat, that verdict was disputed rather than accepted: her hull sits as deep in the water as Paradox's own when properly loaded, so chine runners should work on her regardless of whether her bow drags or flies at rest — a dragging bow is held to matter only for tacking speed in light air (a shorter waterline tacks faster in ghosting conditions, at the cost of pounding once there is wind enough to matter), and the Enigma (both versions), Robbsboat, AS-19, and AS-29 all share the same Bolger "flying bow." What is held to matter for the runners themselves is the rocker's peak position: on Paradox, Enigma, and the Enigma 460 it sits slightly aft of center, tuned to where the runners do their work, whereas Robbsboat's own symmetrical rocker could put her runners a little too far forward by comparison — offered as opinion rather than settled fact.[97] (A Robbsboat build with chine runners specifically intended in place of a leeboard is already under construction — see [Paradox compared to other small-boat designs](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-phil-bolger-redesigns).)

A further reply agreed chine runners are simply the wrong fitting for a PDR — "not the right boat" — and noted the point is largely moot regardless, since the class itself prohibits horizontal foils. The consensus reached was that chine runners work, but only with help from the hull providing some lateral resistance of its own and from an oversized rudder, which in turn requires the boat's center of effort to sit farther aft than normal practice — a restatement, from a different angle, of the lifting-body/rudder load-sharing split already on record [above](#the-designers-own-account-2007-a-lifting-body-system).[98]

The Triloboat verdict was pressed further on the mechanism rather than the depth: from memory, the boat's midsection is genuinely flat, giving her chine runners no curvature at all to work with, and she would not tack on chine runners alone, riding with her bow well clear of the water; by contrast, most Enigma 360 photographs show her bow touching the water, while the Enigma 460 and Swamp Thing more often show it flying. A Robbsboat or a similar Bolger sharpie could probably be trimmed to work with chine runners, but at the risk of a chain of unintended consequences elsewhere in the design — a waterline shorter than optimal, a center of lateral resistance out of place — since Layden, Bolger, and Michalak are all held to understand these trade-offs better than an outside commentator can.[99]

The debate's practical close came by relaying Matt Layden's own explanation directly: the lateral resistance comes from the heeled hull acting as a **lifting body**, with the chine runners serving only as "the icing on the cake" — so a hull that lacks the right underwater profile under sail gains little from adding runners, however well they are built.[100] This restates, from the builder side, the designer's own 2007 lifting-body account [above](#the-designers-own-account-2007-a-lifting-body-system), and closes the exchange on the same footing: chine runners amplify a hull that is already doing most of the work, rather than substituting for one that is not.

A closing reply traced the same bow-foil/aft-rudder configuration further back: Sven Yrvind's own latest boat carries a correspondingly larger rudder still, alongside a planned small bow centerboard to aid windward performance — a configuration Phil Bolger first tried on his **Dart Dinghy** (based on a Caribbean dugout canoe and the English Yorkshire coble) and later extended in his **Cartopper** and **Camper 640** designs, all light-displacement boats pairing a small bow foil with a large aft rudder.[101]

The debate continued past that point into a fresh question: how does the heel-dependent mechanism square with video evidence of two owners sailing with hulls kept nearly upright? One possibility raised is that Paradox's own heavy displacement and rocker may let her sail this way without needing to heel much at all. Set against that, Bolger- and Michalak-style light sharpies work on the opposite principle, built to "sail over the water and not through it": bow and stern ride swept clear of the water for a short, upright, low-wetted-surface waterline in light air, and only drop to skim the water — lengthening the waterline as the lee chine immerses and the weather chine lifts clear, lowering wetted surface — once heeled 20 to 30 degrees in a proper breeze. Jim Michalak has described this "split personality" of his own sharpies, taught to him by Phil Bolger, in his own words: designed to "meet the waves with the bottom and not pushing through them with the sides," holding a short, bolt-upright waterline for quick manoeuvring in light air but needing real wind to heel over, lengthen the waterline, and "meet the waves with the edge of her chine like a V bottomed boat." On this reasoning, a hull built to that lighter-displacement principle suits smooth inland water rather than the depth chine runners need to grip.[102]

A firsthand hedge answered the "why so upright" question from the owner's side instead: any slab-sided hull moves forward far more easily than sideways, so a Paradox sailing upright is most likely doing so in light wind, being slippery enough through the water to be driven along by quite little of it. Leeway in that condition has never been measured directly, but would not be surprising if it proved considerable; beating to windward in such light air would itself be unusual, since a yuloh would ordinarily prove more effective than trying to sail to windward in it.[103]

The same exchange extended the [Hogfish Maximus and Jubilee comparison](#narrow-wing-runners-on-a-chris-morejohn-sharpie-and-a-centerboard-plus-runner-combination) into a design-tradeoff theory of its own: both of Chris Morejohn's larger sharpies pair their chine runners with a centerboard as well as a large rudder, raising the question of why Paradox carries neither a centerboard nor a daggerboard alongside her own runners and rudder. One answer offered is that Matt Layden may have traded away a little extra leeway in light winds for the simplicity, reliability, and interior space a centerboard trunk would otherwise cost.[104]

The thread also carried a running update on Sven Yrvind's own boat-in-progress, continuing the bow-foil/aft-rudder configuration already on record above: word reached the list that he had abandoned his bow board altogether and was instead planning to use his oversized rudder itself like a mizzen sail.[105] That drew a mix of admiration and unease. Losing all lateral resistance from a submerged rudder while surfing down a large wave, with only the raised portion of the rudder held above the wind to keep the bow pointed into it, raised a real worry about maintaining steerage at the bottom of a trough and broaching while climbing back up the next one — set against the appeal of a mizzen doing the same job as an "air rudder," already reported of some Bolger Micro owners who steer mainly by trimming the mizzen and rarely touch the tiller, one of whom regularly backs into a mooring under mizzen alone with no engine; a Jim Michalak "Eisbox" plan's own small, lead-weighted centerboard in a self-draining bow well, raised and lowered by a single line led back to the helm, was offered as a simpler-seeming alternative to whatever daggerboard Sven Yrvind had chosen instead.[106] A fuller explanation followed: the idea is a mizzen sail mounted on the rudderpost itself, working like an arrow's fletching, trimmed by a sheet led to the tiller — not aimed at beating to windward at all, but at weathercocking the boat to hold her nearly head-to-wind while still making some headway. An earlier idea of building the rudder itself from a catamaran sail rather than fitting a separate cloth sail to the rudderpost had been tried and abandoned unexplained; the bow-board designs had likewise all been dropped in favor of experimenting with canting-mast options instead.[107] The canting-mast connection resolved a separate open question of long standing: a solid mizzen or vane working in conjunction with the rudder, rather than a canting mast valued for a stability argument of its own, was recognized as the likely explanation for why a Yrvind design under construction, referred to in the exchange as "Bris 4.1," carries canting masts.[108]
