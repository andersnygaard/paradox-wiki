# Chine runners — origin and evolution

The chine runner is [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own invention, and the single feature that most defines the [Paradox](/paradox.md) and its sister designs. Its history is traced by three converging firsthand sources spanning four decades of sailing and studying the boats. Seen from outside the design world, the runners are simply "longitudinal narrow ‘wings’ that stick out horizontally from the chines" and "act in some mysterious way to minimize leeway when the boat sails to windward" — read as the mark of a free-thinker willing to try new ideas rather than follow traditional practice.[1]

## From fouling leeboards to the first runners

After a severe knockdown off Maine, Matt abandoned the fouling leeboards of his first boat, [Terrapin](/matt-layden/terrapin.md), and on the 1985 [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) **first used the tapered external chine log he came to call the "chine runner."** The runners had "the added benefit of not picking up lobster-pot lines or seaweed" — the failing that had plagued the leeboards.[2] Swamp Thing was "the **first boat to have chinerunners**," with "other boats based on the same concept" being **Paradox and Enigma**.[3]

## The conceptual origin: Bolger's chine battens and the NASA lifting body

The runner's *physical* debut on Swamp Thing has a distinct *intellectual* origin, which Matt Layden set out in his own words in a 2007 *Small Craft Advisor* interview. Two unrelated sources fed into it. The hull form the runner serves — a "lifting body" that produces sideways lift from a pressure difference across the heeled hull — Layden traces to **1970s *Aviation Week* and *Popular Science* articles about experimental NASA aircraft**[4]. The runner itself he traces to **Phil Bolger**: "some seemingly random comments by Phil Bolger about a couple of his sharpies with external chine battens also got me thinking about what aerodynamic engineers sometimes call a **'fence,'** to separate the high from the low pressure and minimize flow across the chine, **and that evolved into my current chine runners**."[4] (See [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md) for the fuller question of Paradox's own place in the sharpie type this Bolger-derived origin sits inside.) The fuller mechanism is described under [how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md); the point here is that the feature grew from a Bolger chine-batten hint married to an aircraft lifting-body concept, not from foil practice in yacht design.

A secondhand elaboration of the same origin, offered as personal belief rather than firsthand testimony, adds a more specific narrative: that Matt Layden first met the effect sailing a small Bolger design with chine logs installed externally, and noticed they cancelled some of the leeway once her leeboard — one that could only be locked fully down or removed, with no way to raise it partway in shallow water — was taken off; shortening those logs and reshaping them wider, more streamlined, and aligned with the water flow is offered as the step that turned a chine batten into a chine runner. The same account restates a boxy hull's own resistance to leeway, likening the runners to end plates on the keel that hull effectively forms — the identical argument, in near-identical words, that the same correspondent had already put on record five years earlier (see [how they work — narrow "wing" runners on a Chris Morejohn sharpie](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#narrow-wing-runners-on-a-chris-morejohn-sharpie-and-a-centerboard-plus-runner-combination)) — offered here as a supplement to, not a replacement for, Matt Layden's own account above.[5]

Layden is also candid about the feature's **narrow niche and uncertain prospects**. Its place, he says, "is in moderate to heavy cruisers that haul a lot of weight" — "not so great for the usual light beamy racer/cruiser or trailersailer" — and he is unassuming about how far it will spread: "Sven is kind, but I'm not sure chine runners will go far." He developed and validated the runners through **"rough sailing and towing model experiments,"** consistent with the scale-model tank-testing noted elsewhere in his design method.[4] His own modesty is part of why the concept remained obscure (see below).

## Earlier parallel work: Castles (1976) and the Wharram catamarans

Matt Layden's account above is of **how he arrived at the feature**, and nothing in the record contradicts it. But the runner was not without precedent, and the clearest statement of that precedent comes from outside the Paradox community entirely — the editor of the Amateur Yacht Research Society's research journal, introducing Sven Yrvind's 2008 feature:

> Similar devices were used by **Prof. Walter Castles back in 1976** (AYRS booklet #83A) and they have been added with some success on a few **Wharram catamarans** since then; but they have had **little visibility on monohulls** until Matt Layden fitted [them] to his Swamp Thing … and then used [them] on a number of other small boats culminating with Paradox (1993) and Enigma (2005).[15]

Two things are worth separating here. The claim is that **similar devices** existed — not that Layden took the idea from them; his own stated path runs through Bolger's chine battens and the NASA lifting body, and this bundle records no evidence he knew of Castles' work. What the editorial establishes is that the *class* of device had been tried before, chiefly on **multihulls**, and that Layden's distinct contribution was making it work on a **monohull cruiser** and proving it in sustained sailing — "with these boats he has cruised and raced along the Eastern Seaboard of the USA, proving the seaworthiness of his chine runners."[15]

Castles' own supporting work is described as **lost**: his programs on "cusped vortex generators" were no longer to be had, leaving only the equations in AYRS booklet #83B and an open appeal for anyone able to reconstruct them.[15] That loss is part of why the concept had no worked-out theory to inherit — which bears directly on the state of knowledge described at [how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md).

The same editorial adds an outside technical judgment on **runner shape** that no source inside the community offers. Suspecting that, "like the design of winglets on keels and aircraft, there are significant subtleties in making efficient chine runners," the editor observed that builders had converged on a single crude solution — "most people have seemed to have used **flat plates of plywood**, more-or-less streamlined, and more-or-less **delta shaped**" — and that "though these seem to work, one wonders whether they are as good as they could be," closing with an open call for anyone who knew better to write in.[15] It is an outside reader's confirmation of exactly the gap the builders themselves kept circling (see [how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) and [construction](/chine-runners/construction.md)); no answer to the appeal is on record here.

## Matt Layden's own chine-runner drawing for another designer's boat (2007)

The chine runner is also documented in **Layden's own hand, drawn to dimensions, outside his own boats**. When Sven Yrvind's 16-foot ocean boat was published in 2008, Yrvind thanked Matt for "doing the drawings and artwork," and the feature carries eight dimensioned hand drawings initialled **ML** and dated **2007** — among them one titled **"Yrvind 5.0m / Chine Runner,"** giving the runner in **plan and profile** with stations along the hull, and a bulkhead section that labels the chine runners on the outboard corners of the bottom.[16] Yrvind's boat is a foam-sandwich cat schooner, not a Paradox, so the drawings are evidence about Layden's *practice at that date* rather than about Paradox's own runners — but they are the fullest published drawings by the designer this bundle records, and they show him specifying the feature for a hull half again Paradox's displacement. (For that boat, see [Sven Yrvind — voyages and boats](/people/yrvind/voyages-and-boats.md#the-16-footer-matt-layden-drew-2007).)

## An outside estimate of the development period

One owner's own estimate, offered without a cited source and not corroborated elsewhere in the record, puts the runners' development at **"a period of eleven years"** — read here as a rough impression rather than a dated fact, since it sits alongside, but does not resolve, the conception/first-build dating already established below.[6] The same owner's own definition of the feature, arrived at independently before he had sailed one himself, calls the runners **"wings"** that "act in some mysterious way to minimize leeway when the boat sails to windward," and reads their presence as evidence of the designer's own character: **"They indicate that he is a free-thinker; a person not caught up with restrictive traditional design. He is a pragmatic person very willing to try new ideas."**[1]

## Dating: concept 1982, first boat 1985

The two dates commonly attached to the chine runner **reconcile through a single witness**. The concept was **"created by Matt in 1982"** while **Swamp Thing, the first boat to carry it, was built in 1985**. The gap is therefore best read as **conception (1982) versus first build (1985)**, not a contradiction. (The same 1982 date recurs across sources; the first chine-runner *boat* is dated to 1985. All are consistent under the conception-vs-build reading.[7][3])

The 1982 conception date is also **fixed in edited print in 2008**, in the same words, by the same witness — "the chine runner concept, created by Matt in 1982" — which dates the claim itself rather than merely repeating it.[16]

A **third date** sits slightly outside this scheme: an editor's note in that same 2008 issue puts Swamp Thing's runners at **1986** rather than 1985.[15] It is a secondhand summary written to introduce someone else's article, against two independent accounts giving 1985 — one of them Yrvind's own detailed year-by-year listing[3] — so **1985 remains the better-supported date**, and the 1986 figure is recorded here as a minor variant rather than a rival reading.

## Which boats use runners, and which use a centerboard

The split across Matt's designs is explicit: **Paradox, GJAC, and Swamp Thing use chine runners**, while [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) uses a **bow-mounted centerboard**; the later [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) and [Elusion](/matt-layden/elusion.md) **return to chine runners**.[8] Matt went back to chine runners for Paradox after concluding Little Cruiser's centerboard "did not seem to improve windward performance sufficiently to merit its construction."[2]

A firsthand comparison sharpens the practical verdict: sailing Paradox (chine runners) alongside Little Cruiser (centerboard), "we cannot tell which boat can point higher… both methods work equally well on Matt's designs." The chine runner's distinct edge is **really shallow water**, "where a centerboard can't be lowered sufficiently to provide adequate lateral resistance" — the runner keeps working where a board cannot.[9] A further, hedged secondhand report adds a caveat to that comparison rather than resolving it: Little Cruiser reportedly performed worse than Paradox in shallow water once her centerboard had to be raised, yet pointed higher than Paradox once back in deeper water — recalled rather than sourced firsthand, and left standing in tension with the "cannot tell which boat can point higher" verdict above.[14]

The difference between Swamp Thing's and Paradox's draft — 5 cm — is one "most people would not notice, [but] Matt did," and reflects Matt's exacting standard for what counts as genuinely shallow draft (water "not above your knee," ~35 cm). Enigma's competitive credentials underline the concept: in **March 2006 Matt sailed the 80 kg Enigma 1,200 miles around Florida in the WaterTribe Ultimate Challenge, finishing first in her class and third overall.**[10][10]

## The Enigma refinement

The runner was **tuned, not frozen**. In 2008 Swamp Thing's runners were rebuilt **about ¼″ wider and ⅛″ thicker (~25% wider)** at Matt's suggestion to try to improve her windward performance, and Enigma's runners are described as **"shorter and wider"** than Paradox's.[11] A second account instead describes the Enigma runners as **higher-aspect foils** of a "shorter and **deeper** shape especially created for this lightweight boat," which "Layden reports … seem to be **an improvement over Paradox's runners**."[7] The two secondary accounts **disagree on the cross-section** ("wider" vs. "deeper") for the very same boat, so the exact shape change is left unreconciled — but both agree Enigma's runners were a deliberate, reportedly successful refinement.

## A forward chine extension on Paradox, tried and not recommended

Not every attempted refinement of the underlying chine shape succeeded. In September 2001 [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) tried **extending the chine forward** on [Paradox](/paradox.md) itself, to see whether it improved the boat's overall performance (a photo of the modification was posted to the group's files area)[12]. After **several trial sails** the change made **very little difference**, and Matt Layden did **not recommend** it — a documented negative result from a design avenue the originator himself tried and set aside.[13][12]

The same message records Matt weighing a **"Little Cruiser style" rudder** for Paradox as a next step, since the two boats' rudders differ in balance point — a rudder-design thread carried forward at [rudder — why the rudder was built large, and the trade-offs of its size](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size).[13]

A month later, a far more rigorous follow-up put real numbers to the same underlying question of whether the chine shape could be improved: see [chine runners and windward performance — Matt Layden's quantified sea trial (2001)](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#matt-laydens-quantified-sea-trial-2001), which extended the port chine runner 16–18 in forward and measured VMG on a triangular course, finding any improvement fell within the margin of error.

## Sven Yrvind's verdict: the innovation of the century

The chine runner has its highest external endorsement from an ocean voyager and designer of radically small boats — offered from direct experience rather than theory. After sailing Paradox and studying it for a decade (see [windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)), the conclusion was: **"I am now convinced that the chinerunner concept, created by Matt in 1982, is the greatest innovation for small cruising boats in the last century"**[10] — restated elsewhere as "the most important innovation for small boats during the 20th century."[3]

The reason **so few designers have adopted it** despite its being in plain sight for a quarter-century is equally clear: Matt "does not try to make his voice heard above the din from the egos in our conservative, self-centered society to promote his idea," and "the principles behind it are complex and subtle, and have been subject to little research and experiment."[10]

## Why the idea has not transferred: a 2016 outside test

A second, more prosaic reason surfaced nine years later, from a professional design shop that seriously considered adopting the runners and worked out what it would take. **B&B Yacht Designs**, looking in March 2016 for more windward ability from their Core Sound 17 Mk3, weighed retrofitting chine runners against lengthening the centerboard — chine runners being, on the face of it, "a relatively easy modification." Two obstacles emerged, and neither is about promotion or obscurity.[17]

**The runners are not a bolt-on; the hull has to suit them.** Matt Layden's hulls are **flat-bottomed with vertical sides**, and carry the runners **horizontally** at the chine. A V-bottomed hull with flared topsides has no such geometry, and would need the runners set at some angle **below** horizontal, found by trial.[17] An independent designer who had already run his own tests across several hull forms gave the empirical version of the same point: hulls "with a lot of chine bury and/or bottom rocker" respond well, "while flatter run plane hulls didn't respond nearly as well" — and on some planing hulls the runners cut leeward skid a little while "suffer[ing] a lot of additional drag once the wind was abeam or aft."[18]

That is a sharper account of the limited uptake than the promotion argument above, and it is consistent with the rest of this page: the runner works as part of a system — heavy displacement, a deeply immersed chine, a heeled lifting-body hull, a large rudder (see [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md)) — and a hull that lacks the system gains little by adding the visible part of it. What the same shop found when it *did* sail a Layden hull, and how it proposed to measure the difference, is at [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#an-outside-shops-chine-runner-test-programme-2016).

## Yrvind's own boats, and where he parts ways

The concept was also adopted directly elsewhere. The **YRVIND.COM (2008)** was "a boat inspired by Matt's chinerunner series,"[10] and the later YRVIND ½ used a ballasted, near-vertical variant of the runners (see [how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md)). There is **one place that adoption now diverges** from Matt: for long ocean passages a **variable lateral area** — something like a centerboard or leeboard — is preferred, because sailing downwind it gives one more way to control the boat, at the cost of added complication. The considered summary draws the boundary cleanly: **"Coastal sailing like Matt does, chinerunners is fine."**[10] The feature is optimal for exactly the shallow-water coastal cruising the Paradox is built for.
