# Chine runners and windward performance

Because the [Paradox](/paradox.md) resists leeway with [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) rather than a centerboard or keel, newcomers and skeptics alike routinely ask whether the boat can really work to windward. It can, on an unusually strong evidence base for so small a boat: a **quantified controlled sea trial** by the designer, an **independent month-long firsthand test**, and a **hard real-world cruising confirmation**, all pointing the same way.[1][2][3]

## Matt Layden's quantified sea trial (2001)

In October 2001 designer [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) ran a **quantified sea trial** — the most rigorous, data-driven performance test of the design on record — comparing the as-built chine runners against a modified port-side runner.[1]

**The modification.** Matt **extended the port chine runner forward by about 16–18 inches**, leaving the starboard runner as originally built, to see whether the change improved windward performance.[1]

**Test method.** Matt sailed Paradox on a stretch of the **Indian River** chosen for practically no current, uniformly deep water, and little traffic. He laid out a **triangular course** with legs over a mile long, using charted channel markers and a point of land, cross-checked with compass bearings and timed runs. He ran **3 circuits**, timing the downwind leg as a control on changing wind strength as well as the two upwind legs, and at each leg's end timed any offset to windward/leeward of the mark to calculate **distance made good to windward**. He deliberately **dipped the standing lug sail to leeward at each tack** (not his normal practice) to cancel rig-asymmetry error between tacks. Wind direction was estimated as half the total angle between the two upwind legs, since compass bearings on the wind were not accurate enough to be meaningful.[1]

Matt candidly noted the method's limits: mark positions were not GPS/survey verified; run-to-run fluctuations ran "several percent" per leg; a rain squall disturbed the last two legs for ~30 minutes; and he put the margin of error at **at least 1/10 knot**, remarking that doing meaningfully better "would probably require a towing tank."[1]

**Results (average of 3 runs per leg):**

| Leg | VMG | Angle to wind | VMGW |
|-----|-----|----------------|------|
| Downwind | 4.53 kt | — | — |
| Starboard tack | 3.63 kt | 51° | 2.29 kt |
| Port tack (modified runner) | 3.49 kt | 51° | 2.21 kt |

(VMG = velocity made good along the course; VMGW = velocity made good to windward.)[1]

**Conclusions.**

- The modified port runner showed **at most a slight improvement**, but the difference (2.21 vs. 2.29 kt VMGW) was **within the margin of error**, so it could not be said for sure it helped.[1]
- The **as-built chine runners are "not dismally bad"** to windward, though "may be susceptible to improvement."[1]
- In numbers, this confirmed what had already been observed informally: **Paradox sails to windward passably well without a centerboard.**[1]
- In the rare cases Paradox has sailed near other small shoal-draft cruisers, it has "usually sailed right past them, upwind or downwind" — attributable not to Paradox being unusually fast, but to other small shoal cruisers generally not being fast either.[1]

This trial sits alongside Matt's separate, unsuccessful [forward chine-extension experiment](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md#a-forward-chine-extension-on-paradox-tried-and-not-recommended) and his "Little Cruiser style" rudder consideration — see [rudder](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size) — as part of the designer's ongoing empirical iteration on the boat's underwater shape.

## Sven Yrvind's month sailing Paradox (1997)

The strongest **independent** endorsement comes from a Swedish long-distance microcruiser designer who came to Paradox as a skeptic. Sent drawings of the boat, he doubted it had "any means of preventing leeway" until a closer look revealed the chine runners — and even then he questioned whether they would be enough. A sailor who had spent a winter in the Bahamas alongside Matt, and confirmed the runners worked very well, put the skeptic in touch with Matt directly; Matt said Paradox could sail to windward better than that sailor's own, slightly bigger centerboard boat, and invited him to see for himself.[2]

In **October 1997** the skeptic **sailed Paradox for a month**, deliberately testing the runners to windward, and found that Paradox "certainly sailed as close to the wind as my boats, **probably much better**." He returned to sail with Matt again in **2003, 2006, and 2007**, about a month each time.[2] The verdict: the chine runner "certainly is one of the smartest inventions in the history of yachting, simple and cheap, easy to build, and it does allow the boat to sail to windward **in very shallow water**."[2] (For the fuller account and the "greatest innovation of the century" judgment, see [origin and evolution](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md).)

## Glen Maxwell's Cumberland Sound confirmation (2002)

Over a year after Matt's own trial, a builder added a **real-world** confirmation. Sailing "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" up the **Cumberland Sound** (St. Mary's River entrance, GA) against **12 kt winds and a foul current**, the boat short-tacked for almost **five hours** to reach anchorage, averaging **3.6 kt** over **18 nm**, tacking through as tight as **90°** on occasion (though favoring **110°** to keep boat speed up against the chop): "To all those skeptics of the chine runners I can now attest to their performance."[3]

This is a sustained, hard-conditions data point from an owner's actual cruising use — complementing Matt's short controlled trial with a longer, harder test. A **mast-heel play problem** (see [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md)) may have cost some windward performance during this same session, leaving open the possibility the numbers *understate* the runners' true potential once that fix is made.

## Dave Bolduc's 1997 published windward validation

The earliest **dated, published** windward-performance verdict on record predates Matt's own 2001 quantified sea trial above by four years. Answering a reader question in the *Messing About In Boats* construction series, Dave Bolduc reported that after sailing Paradox against Little Cruiser (which has a centreboard) for over two months, he did not find enough difference in the windward performance between the two boats to suggest altering Paradox — and recommended building to the plans before modifying them.[4] This is the same firsthand Paradox-versus-Little-Cruiser comparison given in undated form from microcruising.com below (see [pointing angles owners report](#pointing-angles-owners-report)); the two accounts are complementary rather than duplicates, with this 1997 magazine account the earlier and explicitly dated version of the verdict.

## Pointing angles owners report

Newcomers unfamiliar with the design regularly ask essentially the same question — not expecting J-boat pointing, but wanting to know whether the chine runners allow reasonable windward progress and whether anyone can roughly quantify how high she points.[5] There is no single numeric pointing answer beyond Matt's trial above, but the firsthand reports are consistent:

- A Paradox has been seen sailing alongside Seapearl dinghies and **pointing as high as they did**: "The chine runners work !!" (A **Small Craft Advisor (SCA)** article covers the boat's pointing ability in more depth.)[6]
- Sailing "Zoë"'s re-cut sail (see [sail — the Dabbler re-cut](/components/rig/sail.md#the-dabbler-re-cut-mark-ii-sail-a-decisive-improvement)), upwind performance is **very good**: Paradox **will tack through 90°**, though tacking through a wider **100°** sails noticeably faster. "You just can't beat those chine runners for doing the job they do relative to the convenience they provide. Try em, you'll like em."[7]
- Pointing depends on the **whole design working together**: the boat needs **enough weight aboard to submerge the lower chine runner when heeled**, the **cut of the sail** matters greatly, and a lug sail demands different handling skill from a conventional sloop — every builder who persists through the learning curve ends up pleased.[8]

The `microcruising` group is a further source: the owners of [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) sail alongside Matt in his own Paradox and are consistently impressed by her capability. Their own firsthand comparison is the sharpest verdict on record: sailing Paradox (chine runners) alongside Little Cruiser (centerboard) they "cannot tell which boat can point higher… both methods work equally well on Matt's designs," with the chine runner's distinct edge being **really shallow water**.[9] (See [origin and evolution](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md).)

A further owner's rule of thumb, restated across several seasons on the same boat, gives a specific point of sail at which the runners are working best: **"She sails best to windward when the windward chine runner is just clear of the water. You'll need a Force 3 for this."**[10] See [how chine runners work — a UK owner's sine-wave hypothesis, and a measured heel/wind/displacement optimum](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-uk-owners-sine-wave-hypothesis-and-a-measured-heelwinddisplacement-optimum) for the fuller, quantified version of the same owner's finding (about 14–14.5° of heel, 10 kt/Force 3, at a displacement of 640 kg/1,410 lb).

A newer owner's first extended cruise adds a further, if less quantified, firsthand impression: struck by how the boat ghosts along in winds too light to ripple the water, then gives wonderful bursts of speed to windward, right up to hull speed. See [boats/ironsides.md — a first cruise](/boats/ironsides.md#a-first-cruise-four-days-in-south-puget-sound-july-2009) for the fuller cruise account.[11]

## Build accuracy, heel, and a firsthand comparison of two owners' windward touch

Chine runners built exactly to plan make a real, quantifiable difference to windward performance; departing from the plans' shape or dimensions costs performance, and about **fifteen degrees** of heel is judged to make for better progress on the wind — consistent with the measured heel/wind/displacement optimum recorded elsewhere (see [how chine runners work — a UK owner's sine-wave hypothesis, and a measured heel/wind/displacement optimum](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-uk-owners-sine-wave-hypothesis-and-a-measured-heelwinddisplacement-optimum)).[16] A firsthand comparison between two owners sailing alongside each other, one in "Little Jim" and one in "Faith," found a striking gap in how much windward performance a sailor can extract from the same design: one owner's sail sometimes looked as if it were set for reaching rather than beating, and yet the boat was still going well to windward — a difference in touch as much as in equipment.[16]

## An outside shop's chine-runner test programme (2016)

Everything above is evidence gathered by the designer or by owners of his boats. In **March 2016** the question was taken up from outside the family, by **B&B Yacht Designs** — a working North Carolina design shop with no stake in Matt Layden's designs — after their own Core Sound 17 Mk3 proved less weatherly than wanted in that year's Everglades Challenge. Chine runners were considered as an alternative to lengthening the centerboard, on the grounds that Layden "used those with no boards and they seemed pretty effective."[17]

The shop's proposed **method** is the most rigorous test design on record after Matt Layden's own 2001 trial: **hot-glue a single runner to one side of the boat only**, sail it, and compare **course made good on the GPS against the compass heading on each tack** — the asymmetry making the runner's contribution directly readable from the difference between the two tacks.[18] The refinement they identified matters for anyone transferring the idea: Layden's hulls are flat-bottomed with vertical sides and carry the runners **horizontally**, whereas a V-bottomed hull with flared topsides would need them **angled below horizontal**.[18]

Three findings came out of the episode, none of them from a Paradox:

- **A measured tacking angle on an [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md).** Sailing a chine-runner Enigma brought to the shop that month, the designer put her at **about 120° between tacks** — his own boat making about 100° in 10 knots the next day. He was explicit that the figure was a "best guess," taken without instruments from inside an unfamiliar boat in strong wind. It sits below the [90–110° owners report for stock Paradox](#pointing-angles-owners-report), and should be read as a rough outside sighting rather than as a competing measurement.[19] The fuller account of that sail, which ended in a capsize, is at [Enigma — an outside designer sails an Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md#an-outside-designer-sails-an-enigma-and-capsizes-her-march-2016).

- **The drag trade-off, stated as a testable proposition.** Chine runners "may add some drag downwind if they are always submerged," and will add some upwind too — but "the reduction in leeway may reduce lift induced drag so much that overall drag upwind is reduced." The three possible outcomes were named separately: pointing higher, sailing faster, or both. The metric to watch is **velocity made good to windward**, tested with and without runners on the **same day** to hold wind and sea constant.[20] This is the same VMG-to-windward measure Matt Layden used in his own 2001 trial [above](#matt-laydens-quantified-sea-trial-2001), reached independently.

- **The runners are hull-form dependent.** An independent designer reported his own testing across several hulls: forms with a lot of chine bury and bottom rocker respond well, flatter planing hulls much less. That finding, and what it implies about why the idea has spread so little, is at [origin and evolution — why the idea has not transferred](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md#why-the-idea-has-not-transferred-a-2016-outside-test).

## A recurring skeptic's question: does British water sail differently?

A distinct, recurring form of the same underlying doubt asks not about the chine runners specifically but about regional sailing conditions: whether a hull form and rig that work well in American, Australian, or New Zealand waters can really perform to windward the same way in "British conditions." The direct answer, on the record, is that there is no reason to expect a difference — sailing conditions are not seriously different enough between those places to change a boat's windward performance, and the durable explanation for why conventional (rather than radical-looking) designs still dominate the secondhand and new-boat markets is advertising, salesmanship, and self-perception rather than any real performance gap.[12] See [secondhand Paradox market — build vs. buy secondhand: a UK newcomer's cost-benefit hesitation](/operations/secondhand-market.md#build-vs-buy-secondhand-a-uk-newcomers-cost-benefit-hesitation) for the fuller exchange this question arose from, including a design-history theory on why flat-bottomed sharpies suit American waters (wide planks from bigger trees) against the English Channel's traditional narrow, deep, "plank on edge" hull form.

The chine runners' unfamiliar look continues to draw the same skeptical question from newcomers: whether so radical-looking a leeway-resisting mechanism can really sail to windward at all, and whether the boat's storage arrangement ("gizmos") is more complex to live with than it looks.[13] The consistent answer is that the runners are simpler in practice than they appear and the design's windward performance is well attested by the evidence above. A related recurring question — whether Paradox suffers more leeway than a centerboard catboat sailing upwind, or whether "the chine runners [are] magic"[14] — gets a characteristically terse answer: **"They're magic!"**, pointing to "Zoë" wake photographs (labeled "wake1" and "wake2" in the group's photo files) as a windward-performance illustration.[15] The boat's enclosed layout — whether Paradox could be built with a more open cockpit, or whether other chine-runner designs exist — is addressed on [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#why-the-cabin-is-enclosed-a-convertible-car-analogy).

## A 2017 balanced verdict: adequate for coastal cruising, no match for a modern dinghy

A further, more measured 2017 verdict weighed the chine runners' windward performance against both a deep keel and a modern racing dinghy, rather than against nothing: performance is not in doubt, though it falls short of a deep keel — Paradox points quite well despite its unconventional sail, but carries a lot of leeway since the runners simply lack the area, and tacks slowly through a wide turning circle. As a coastal cruising boat for shallow water this works very well; against a modern racing dinghy it is, by the same reckoning, poor. The same exchange traced the Chinese lug's own per-batten sheeting lines ("sheetlets") to the historical "vang" used on Thames barges — a control that fell out of general use on small cruising yachts once paid hands became too expensive to keep it rigged, but which the more competent barge skippers still use to markedly improve a spritsail's windward performance the same way. Full exchange: [junk rig — a 2017 objection: is the underlying windward question even settled?](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-2017-objection-is-the-underlying-windward-question-even-settled).[21]
