# John Gilbert

**John Gilbert** is a sailor who moved to **Nanaimo, British Columbia, Canada**, on the Pacific coast, after earlier living in **Thunder Bay, Ontario**, where he once meant to build a Paradox before the move changed his plans.[1][2] Around the same time he proposed a PVC-pipe toilet, with a screw lid and a coffee filter, for a boat with no installed head, biodegradable and simple to bury ashore.[2] He has cruised British Columbia's tidal coastal waters without an engine for years and built the yulohs for two of his own, non-Paradox cruising boats.[3][4] He never built a Paradox himself. He reasoned about the design from physics and first principles, working through ballast and hull construction, scaling, rig classification, sculling, self-steering, cabin heating, auxiliary power and the loads a hull and rudder carry.

## Ballast, rig and hull construction

Lead runs a little over eleven times the density of water, against roughly thirteen times for gold and twenty-one for platinum, and scrap wheel-balancing weights from tire shops are a cheap source, melted into pigs.[5] Ballast spread across the beam, as Paradox's is, slows her roll rather than her pitch, by the same radius-of-gyration principle a figure skater uses pulling the arms in to speed a spin.[6] A stripped Paradox was put at 550 to 650 lb, close to the practical limit for car-topping.[7] Full account: [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives), [ballast placement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) and [car-topping conversion ideas](/design/ideas/car-topping-conversion-idea.md#a-stripped-paradoxs-own-car-topping-weight-and-roof-rack-loading-technique).

The rig reads as a variant of a standing lug, its tack close to but not on the mast, and its reefing and furling system, slackening the halyard and hauling in the reefing line, earned the nickname "refurling."[8] Full account: [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md#balanced-lug-standing-lug-or-dipping-lug).

The sheer clamp's notch near the stern clears the steering-line lead blocks.[9] A heavier structure is usually more seaworthy than a lighter one built to the same design: modern composite technique can build strong and light, but it is more critical to get right and shows wear from beaching and years of use faster than a stouter structure, so genuinely light weight suits a car-topper, an easy tow or a racer more than a cruiser.[10] Full account: [sheer strakes](/components/sheer-strakes.md#sheer-clamp-notch-near-the-stern) and [hull scantlings](/design/hull-scantlings.md#the-case-for-over-construction-field-evidence-hazards-and-a-counter-argument).

Casting lead directly into the chine runners, or bolting on a steel ballast wing, only works near the bow or stern without adding draft; elsewhere it adds drag and loses ballast value to the buoyancy of whatever is bolted on. Running lead in pieces along the outside centerline, as on Nathanael Herreshoff's sharpie Meadowlark, was proposed as an alternative without departing from the stock arrangement.[11] Full account: [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#a-2007-revisiting-and-the-case-against-a-bolt-on-steel-wing).

## Scaling and an enlarged Paradox

Displacement increases with the cube of a scale factor while stability rises only to the fourth power, so a hull can normally be stretched fifteen to twenty percent before the result is honestly a new design rather than a bigger Paradox; Matt Layden's and Dave and Mindy Bolduc's own Little Cruiser stands as the precedent for that different, larger design.[12] A worked 20 percent scale-up, 16 ft 6.42 in by 4 ft 11.3 in by 10.8 in and about 2,310 lb, used a butt-block joint in place of the plans' scarfed side-panel overlap, a shortcut a real builder later adopted to add about eight inches, concentrated in the cabin.[13][14] Full account: [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#further-scaling-cautions-stability-an-existing-39-ft-bolger-hull-and-the-fourth-power-rule-restated), [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-2009-coda-two-further-declared-attempts-a-towing-weight-fix-and-the-enigma-460-alternative) and [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#why-paradox-is-fundamentally-a-solo-boat-and-one-builders-case-for-a-removable-cabin-instead).

## Sculling and the yuloh

He traced the yuloh's origin to Chinese junks and sampans.[15] He read its proper total curve as 7 to 10 degrees, confirmed by building the yulohs for two of his own, non-Paradox displacement cruising boats years earlier, both of which worked well.[4] Full account: [yuloh and stowed-rig retention](/components/yuloh.md#blade-camber-the-ford-nomenclature-and-pivot-socket-dimensions) and [building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket).

## Self-steering

A weighted-shaft self-steering method held a sailing model's heading well once the rig was balanced, varying only slightly even in gusty conditions.[16] A lug-rigged ("angle sail") boat is unlikely ever to self-steer well on sheet-to-tiller gear, for want of the positive feedback, a reliable balance of forces restoring course through changes of wind and sea, that the method needs to work.[17] Full account: [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md#a-pendulum-weight-self-steering-idea-and-its-limits) and [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md#a-2015-revival-of-interest-in-sheet-to-tiller-steering).

## Cabin heating

A "Little Cod" wood-burning stove aboard a 21 ft cruising sailboat ran too hot at first, until the firebox fire was kept small and fed one stick at a time every 5 to 10 minutes, and five weeks living aboard through a Vancouver November and December passed in reasonable comfort on that technique.[18] Carbon monoxide from a candle-and-diffuser heater carries a real risk, calling for a CO monitor, or at minimum a colour-changing oxygen-level test card, alongside good ventilation and an exhaust path.[19] Full account: [cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-charcoal-or-kerosene-can-heater-and-the-transom-cook-stove-alternative) and [cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-carbon-monoxide-caution-for-candle-and-diffuser-heaters).

## Auxiliary power: a firsthand case for adapting to tide and wind

Years of engine-free cruising in complex British Columbia coastal waters showed what genuinely no-engine sailing demands: in light or no wind, the ordinary time-distance-destination calculation stops working, calling instead for moving toward a shallow, exposed bit of shore to wait out a tide change, or getting under way at 3 a.m. to catch a favourable one, a real change in the pace of life that rewards with better rest once found.[3] In a place with genuine current running past it, going without an engine severely restricts a sailor's ability to use the water at all, unless the boat itself is purpose-built for human power.[20] British Columbia coastal knowledge extends to its buoyage: the international lateral standard attempted decades earlier never fully displaced North America's older system, and on the British Columbia coast the "red, right, return" rule applies specifically to the direction of the flood tide, which is not always obvious among the islands.[21] Full account: [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#adapting-the-pace-of-cruising-to-tide-and-wind-in-genuinely-engine-free-conditions), [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-qualifier-when-local-current-itself-calls-for-an-engine) and [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md).

## Assessing Paradox from outside

The forces on a rudder are bounded by the boat's own scale, and a roughly 1,400 lb boat is a small, light one whose loads can never be huge; those forces are best described as moments, force multiplied by distance, a function of the hull's own size and weight together with the rudder's area and how far it sits from the boat's turning centre.[22][23] Matt Layden got Paradox's own balance about right more by educated guess than by calculation: the Enigma 3.6 and 4.6 both needed their masts raked aft considerably to get balance, done only after initial sailing trials.[24] The inaugural Race to Alaska drew a verdict of Paradox "way outclassed" by the leaders, capable of finishing the course but much more slowly, since few boats manage to go to windward in near-gale winds over the course's several hundred miles.[25] The sharpest practical difference between Paradox and most other microcruiser designs is open-water capability itself: most such designs suit only sheltered or nearly-sheltered waters, built as open or partially open boats, where Paradox is not.[26] Full account: [rudder](/components/rudder.md#rudder-vulnerability-and-how-much-load-it-carries-relative-to-the-hull), [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#the-plans-ca-label-and-an-unresolved-centroid-offset-2012), [Race to Alaska](/merits/race-to-alaska.md#a-2015-mid-race-assessment-how-would-paradox-have-fared) and [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2011-coda-a-request-for-a-fuller-rough-water-account).
