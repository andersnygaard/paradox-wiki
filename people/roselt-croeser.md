# Roselt Croeser

**Roselt Croeser** is a Paradox builder in **Western Australia** and a member of the **Amateur Boat Builders' Association**, the Perth home-boatbuilding club. He built the Paradox "**[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)**" from **early 2013 to the end of 2016**, and presented the build and its shakedown cruise to Shark Bay at the association's **April 2021** meeting, the write-up of which is the archive's sole source on him.[1][2]

He appears on the mailing list itself eight times: twice before or right at the start of the build — replying to a fellow owner's September 2012 question about junk-rigging a Paradox, he revealed he had already converted a different boat to junk rig and planned to fit one to his own, still-unbuilt Paradox too — a plan carried out four months later, when construction of what became Planktos got under way (see [below](#a-2012-mailing-list-exchange-months-before-the-build)); and again the following March, offering a fellow newcomer a stretching idea and general encouragement (see [below](#a-march-2013-mailing-list-exchange-a-stretching-idea-and-general-encouragement)) — five times more, mid-build: confirming a fellow Western Australian owner's coastal voyages in August 2014 (see [below](#an-august-2014-exchange-confirming-a-fellow-owners-voyages)), explaining a bulkhead's curved side that November (see [below](#a-november-2014-exchange-the-bulkhead-side-curve)), relaying that same owner's own account of recovering from a 2009 capsize the following March (see [below](#a-march-2015-exchange-relaying-geoff-daviss-own-account-of-the-2009-capsize)), the same day applying an outside stability checklist to Paradox and passing on that owner's own theory of the capsize's cause (see [below](#a-further-exchange-the-same-day-a-stability-checklist-and-a-theory-of-the-capsize)), and, four weeks later, sharing a jig for lining up the rudder's fittings (see [below](#an-april-2015-exchange-lining-up-the-rudder-fittings)) — and once more a year on, giving a firsthand rationale for the junk rig conversion and a build-status update (see [below](#a-2016-mailing-list-exchange-the-twist-problem-and-a-build-status)).[7][8] Otherwise his build ran years after the list era the archive is mostly drawn from, worked up from the plans and — on his own assessment — from the builders' group behind them, which he credited alongside the quality of the plans in judging Paradox "the easiest type of boat to build."[3]

## Approach

The build was run **alongside owner-building a house**, moved through **four different locations**, and was finished deliberately to a **working boat** standard: "very strong, very simple," using whatever was already to hand.[2] That frugality is what makes the build notable — the mast was **repurposed from a sunken boat** and the sail came from an **SS18**, holding the whole build to about **$2500** in materials.[2][3]

He also **converted the boat from the stock lug rig to a junk rig**, making Planktos the only junk-rigged Paradox in the archive built by *converting* the stock lug rather than designing the rig from scratch — the other built-and-sailed junk-rig Paradox on record, [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s [THU](/boats/thu.md), was purpose-designed.[1] His verdict on the conversion, and his full account of the build, costs, and how she sails, are recorded at [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md).

## The November 2016 launch

Planktos was launched in **November 2016**, reported to the mailing list in real time — a contemporary record that predates, and corroborates, the 2021 retrospective above. Full account at [Planktos — launch](/boats/planktos.md#launch-november-2016).[16]

## On the group: a sailing life and a builder's counsel

Croeser later joined the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) as "Waldo Cruiser," where his posts round out both the sailing of "[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)" and his settled view of how to build one. He sailed her out of Perth — overnight rendezvous at the north end of **Garden Island**, day-sails from **Mangles Bay** — and reported her real numbers, sustained speeds to 5.5 knots and 80-nautical-mile overnight runs, all drawn out at [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md).[4]

His building advice is consistently that of a pragmatist who has finished a boat and sailed it hard: the Paradox is "one of the easiest boats you can build," best approached without anxiety about a perfect finish because "epoxy can fill the gaps"; construction-grade plywood is fine (and bends more easily than marine grade); he used **12 mm** for the bottom because 18 mm "was too heavy to carry," judging it "bullet proof" once glassed to the plans; and he skips foam insulation entirely, having had moisture build up and rot behind it before, lashing in empty plastic containers for buoyancy instead.[5] He also gave prospective builders a plain cost breakdown, the sail and trailer being the big items and the plywood the least of it.[6] He sold Planktos in July 2023 to **Kevin Williamson**, who kept her junk rig and later extended her mast, renaming her **"Skye"**; Croeser has stayed in the group cheering her on under her new name.[4]

## A 2012 mailing-list exchange, months before the build

Replying to [Alberto Callejon](/people/alberto-callejon.md)'s question whether anyone had sailed a Paradox under a junk rig, Croeser answered no, but that he had already converted an eighteen-foot fibreglass production boat to junk rig himself and planned to fit one to his own Paradox too, confident there are "many different ways to rig a junk rig" and that it could be adapted to suit. The exchange predates his own build by about four months, and the plan stated here is exactly what Planktos became: the archive's only Paradox converted from the stock lug to a junk rig.[7]

## A March 2013 mailing-list exchange: a stretching idea and general encouragement

Replying to a newcomer's plan to enlarge his own Paradox by roughly 20%, Croeser offered a distinct scaling idea from the ones already canvassed on the thread — keep the existing frame spacing and simply stretch the side panels longer, rather than widen the bays between them — alongside general encouragement: experimenting is what boatbuilding is all about, and Matt Layden himself only arrived at the Paradox design after quite a few earlier boats, so there was no reason not to continue that journey. The exchange came either just before or just as his own Planktos build got under way (see [Approach](#approach)).[8]

## An August 2014 exchange: confirming a fellow owner's voyages

Replying on a "Longer Cruises" thread, Croeser confirmed a fellow Western Australian Paradox owner's coastal voyages were genuine, adding that the owner had made a further round trip to the Abrolhos Islands and back to Perth the previous year. Full account at [Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md#a-distance-and-duration-recorded-for-the-perth-to-shark-bay-passage-and-an-abrolhos-round-trip-dated-to-2013).[9]

## A November 2014 exchange: the bulkhead side curve

Replying on a "Bulkhead side curvature" thread, Croeser gave the plainest answer on record for why a bulkhead's sides carry a slight convex curve: the hull's side panels are themselves curved in the third dimension, and the bulkhead's sides simply follow that shape — "no other reason." Fuller exchange at [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight).[10]

## A March 2015 exchange: relaying Geoff Davis's own account of the 2009 capsize

Replying on a "New Member now capsize" thread that had turned to a reported 2009 capsize of [Geoff Davis](/boats/small-fry.md)'s "Small Fry" off Fremantle, Croeser said he had been in contact with Davis for some years and that Davis had told him about the incident personally, leaving the telling of the story itself to Davis; what Croeser understood was that Davis recovered the boat, repaired a hole in the hull's side, and built a new mast, before going on to further long coastal voyages — to the Abrolhos Islands and back to Perth in 2013, and to Shark Bay in 2014. He also mentioned having previously owned a Vertue, "Yacabba," since sold, that Davis had sailed aboard with him, and judged Paradox shares most of a Vertue's attributes on a smaller scale. Full account of the capsize itself at [Small Fry — an unconfirmed 2009 report](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source).[11]

The Vertue comparison drew a dissenting reply from a fellow owner, who judged the two designs "very dissimilar" beyond their difference in size.[12]

## A further exchange, the same day: a stability checklist and a theory of the capsize

Still on the same thread, Croeser shared an outside naval-architecture article applying a standard seaworthiness checklist to Paradox — not overly beamy, heavy for her size, a favourable prismatic coefficient, a moderate ballast ratio, a relatively heavy rig damping her roll, and a good ratio of hull above to below the water — and added that the mast's own buoyancy when submerged adds further to her ultimate stability. Drawing on his own experience building a free-standing mast and checking Paradox's mast design with the same calculations, he judged it strong, and considered but declined a mast tabernacle in favour of the stock design's simplicity. Relaying Geoff Davis's own belief that the rudder had given way first in the 2009 capsize, he added that any Paradox's rudder is worth building strong given how much force passes through it. Full account at [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources), [rig — mast](/components/rig/mast.md#a-further-2015-idea-a-keel-mounted-built-in-tabernacle), and [Small Fry — an unconfirmed 2009 report](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source).[13]

## An April 2015 exchange: lining up the rudder fittings

Croeser shared a dummy jig for lining up his rudder's top and bottom fittings without guesswork — two wooden off-cuts and a length of stainless-steel rod sighting the true line between them — along with a caution that the rudder blade must be cut back exactly as the plan shows, or it will strike the hull as the rudder swings to starboard. Full technique at [rudder — lining up the top and bottom fittings](/components/rudder.md#lining-up-the-top-and-bottom-rudder-fittings-with-a-dummy-jig).[14]

## A 2016 mailing-list exchange: the twist problem, and a build status

Resurfacing on a "ballast" thread a year after the rudder-jig exchange above, Croeser gave the fullest firsthand rationale on record for his own conversion: an earlier eighteen-foot fibreglass keelboat, already converted to junk rig, had convinced him his Paradox would carry one too, since some lug sails twist so much that only part of the sail actually works — a fault the junk rig's design cures, and the reason, in his view, for the rig's simplicity as much as its effectiveness. He signed off "Planktos - not finished after 6 years," a duration that, read literally, would put the build's start around 2010 — two to three years earlier than the "early 2013" start point the 2021 ABBA newsletter later gave (see [Approach](#approach)); the discrepancy is unresolved and may be an approximate, rounded figure rather than a precise one. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#further-sheeting-fixes-a-trimarans-cockpit-only-rig-and-the-cost-case-for-conversion) and [boats/planktos.md](/boats/planktos.md#the-junk-rig).[15]

## A 2017 mailing-list exchange: furling friction, a later photo answer, and a Bermuda-rig critique

Replying to a fellow owner's furling-friction question the following year, gave the fullest account on record of the junk-rig conversion's actual trade-off: more rope and about fifteen minutes' extra rigging time at launch, set against never again having any trouble raising or lowering the sail. The two costs he named were social rather than mechanical — other people's unfamiliarity with, and negative attitude toward, the junk rig, and a stowed sail bundle in the lazyjacks bulkier and less tidy against the cabin than a neatly furled lug sail, a possible issue in extreme conditions he hoped to avoid but could not guarantee.[17] A same-day follow-up asked whether the conversion had kept the original sail's shape or been reworked toward a standard junk-rig plan.[18] Days later gave the sail's own dimensions: built from a leftover junk sail with its top panel cut off, leaving four panels at about the plan sail's own area; reefs to three panels around 12-15 kt and to two around 20 kt, with the sheeting system making the sail's twist easier to control — one of his original motivations for the conversion.[19] A further request to see a photo of the rig was answered two days later by pointing to existing pictures in the "Planktos" photo album, with more promised on the next sail out to show specifically how the junk rig looks and works.[20] Full furling-friction discussion: [components/rig/furling.md — a 2017 recurrence, and three further independent fixes](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2017-recurrence-and-three-further-independent-fixes).

On the same thread's turn to a Bermuda-rig question, judged the idea unfavorably: putting a Bermuda rig on Paradox would combine all of that rig's drawbacks with none of its benefits, since sailing one well needs a boom vang, a tight forestay, and all the extra gear needed to reef it properly — better left to the racing crowd — and running downwind, the mainsail would simply blanket the jib.[21] Full account: [boats/thor.md — a second Bermuda-rig temptation, and a windward-performance account](/boats/thor.md#a-second-bermuda-rig-temptation-and-a-windward-performance-account-june-2017).

## A further 2017 exchange: performance compared across three boats, and a windvane once tried

The following month, gave the fullest firsthand performance account on record for a junk-rigged Paradox, comparing her against a Vertue owned for seven years and an eighteen-foot fibreglass fin-keeler also converted to junk rig, all sailed in the same bay under the same conditions; full account at [Planktos — how she sails](/boats/planktos.md#how-she-sails).[22] Restated his own motivation for the conversion and a sheeting/balance technique worked out through experiment; full account at [Planktos — the junk rig](/boats/planktos.md#the-junk-rig).[23][24] Asked by a newcomer for a sketch of his windvane rig, revealed he had in fact built one for the boat — undersized, and set aside once the boat was finished, in favor of steering by feel; full account at [self-steering — a 2017 build from the Wharram sketch, tried and set aside](/operations/self-steering.md#a-2017-build-from-the-wharram-sketch-tried-and-set-aside).[25]

## A further 2017 exchange: a wintery shakedown, irons, and a rough jibe

The following month gave a rougher, more testing account than the mid-summer sails already recorded above: launching against an onshore wind with no working oar, then sailing out through moored boats into building waves and 15-knot gusts, getting stuck in irons on one tack and taking a rough jibe that knocked the boat well over before tacking technique settled down; running off downwind, the boat surfed to 5 knots, fast enough that hand-holding the sheets beat working them as a cleated rope. Full account, including the junk-rig sheeting arrangement the accompanying photographs showed: [Planktos — how she sails](/boats/planktos.md#how-she-sails).[26]

## A further 2017 exchange: bay sailing near home, and a pointing comparison against a McGregor 26

Replying that October on the mailing list's "Trailer" thread, still confined to bay sailing close to home while finishing odd jobs left over from the build, gave an early, favourable verdict on the junk rig — more lines to manage than a lug sail, but never a furling problem, simply letting the halyard go. Sailing in 10-15 knots covered 14 nautical miles in 4.5 hours; pointed lower than the jib-and-main crowd but kept a reasonable pace with a McGregor 26 to windward even as it pointed higher, helped by a shallow draft that reached water right up to where the sea grass scratched the hull's bottom, "no one else goes there." Judged the other sailors always thought he was having more fun than they were — and agreed. Full account at [Planktos — how she sails](/boats/planktos.md#how-she-sails).[27]

## A 2018 exchange: the value of the view through the windows

Replying to a recurring question about fitting a plexiglass view dome into the hatch, judged the view from inside the boat very good and very necessary — but through the windows rather than the hatch, since the sail obscures the hatch-top view, especially to leeward. Steers from inside looking out through the windows often, valuing the shelter from sun and weather as much as the view itself, plus the sound of the water along the hull. See [components/hatch.md — the plexiglass-dome question recurs](/components/hatch.md#the-plexiglass-dome-question-recurs-2018).[28]

## An August 2018 exchange: general safety practice

Replying on a "Stability" thread that had turned to safety at sea, gave a round-up of his own practice: the protected steering position and the ability to drop a junk rig in seconds both count as safety features in their own right; positive buoyancy comes from tied-in plastic containers rather than foam, which he judged too complicated for his own skill level; and closed with a general maxim — stay out of tight situations, keep open water around the boat, and plan ahead. He also restated, in brief, the already-recorded 2009 rolling and repair of the other Western Australian Paradox (see [above](#a-march-2015-exchange-relaying-geoff-daviss-own-account-of-the-2009-capsize)). Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#the-enclosed-steering-position-and-general-seamanship-maxims).[29]

## A September 2018 exchange: vouching for Small Fry's sale

When [Geoff Davis](/boats/small-fry.md) listed "Small Fry" for sale on the mailing list, Croeser vouched for her quality and seaworthiness as a longtime friend of Davis's, having toured the boat himself while still contemplating his own build and sailed a short distance in her since; judged her definitely set up to be very secure, and to have completed some very extensive trips. Full record of the listing itself at [Small Fry — the 2018 sale listing](/boats/small-fry.md#the-2018-sale-listing-construction-electrical-system-and-price).[30] Once the sale closed in 2020, Croeser welcomed her new owner as a fellow Perth-area Paradox sailor and offered to meet up; full account at [Small Fry — Ownership](/boats/small-fry.md#ownership).[32]

## A 2019 exchange: a Force 6 rescue

Uploaded a written account of being towed in from about 12 nautical miles offshore in Force 6 conditions, framing it as showing the design's true limit is the skipper's own endurance and mental state rather than a fixed number: "The Paradox certainly looks after its skipper." Full exchange, including a corroborating reply from a fellow owner, at [design/blue-water-suitability.md — a 2019 test case](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2019-test-case-a-force-6-rescue-and-skipper-endurance-as-the-real-limit).[31] The exchange ran on for over a week, with follow-up questions drawing out the rudder's own strain under tow, his use of heaving-to, and — separately — Planktos's departures from the plans in her cabin structure and her flotation and water arrangements; full detail at [Planktos — a Force 6 rescue](/boats/planktos.md#a-force-6-rescue-2019) and [Planktos — departures from the plans](/boats/planktos.md#departures-from-the-plans-the-cabin-structure-and-flotation-without-built-in-tanks).
