# Roselt Croeser

**Roselt Croeser** is a Paradox builder in **Western Australia** and a member of the **Amateur Boat Builders' Association**, the Perth home-boatbuilding club. He built the Paradox "**[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)**" from **early 2013 to the end of 2016**, and presented the build and its shakedown cruise to Shark Bay at the association's **April 2021** meeting, the write-up of which is the archive's sole source on him.[1][2]

He appears on the mailing list itself seven times: twice before or right at the start of the build — replying to a fellow owner's September 2012 question about junk-rigging a Paradox, he revealed he had already converted a different boat to junk rig and planned to fit one to his own, still-unbuilt Paradox too — a plan carried out four months later, when construction of what became Planktos got under way (see [below](#a-2012-mailing-list-exchange-months-before-the-build)); and again the following March, offering a fellow newcomer a stretching idea and general encouragement (see [below](#a-march-2013-mailing-list-exchange-a-stretching-idea-and-general-encouragement)) — and five times more, mid-build: confirming a fellow Western Australian owner's coastal voyages in August 2014 (see [below](#an-august-2014-exchange-confirming-a-fellow-owners-voyages)), explaining a bulkhead's curved side that November (see [below](#a-november-2014-exchange-the-bulkhead-side-curve)), relaying that same owner's own account of recovering from a 2009 capsize the following March (see [below](#a-march-2015-exchange-relaying-geoff-daviss-own-account-of-the-2009-capsize)), the same day applying an outside stability checklist to Paradox and passing on that owner's own theory of the capsize's cause (see [below](#a-further-exchange-the-same-day-a-stability-checklist-and-a-theory-of-the-capsize)), and, four weeks later, sharing a jig for lining up the rudder's fittings (see [below](#an-april-2015-exchange-lining-up-the-rudder-fittings)).[7][8] Otherwise his build ran years after the list era the archive is mostly drawn from, worked up from the plans and — on his own assessment — from the builders' group behind them, which he credited alongside the quality of the plans in judging Paradox "the easiest type of boat to build."[3]

## Approach

The build was run **alongside owner-building a house**, moved through **four different locations**, and was finished deliberately to a **working boat** standard: "very strong, very simple," using whatever was already to hand.[2] That frugality is what makes the build notable — the mast was **repurposed from a sunken boat** and the sail came from an **SS18**, holding the whole build to about **$2500** in materials.[2][3]

He also **converted the boat from the stock lug rig to a junk rig**, making Planktos the only junk-rigged Paradox in the archive built by *converting* the stock lug rather than designing the rig from scratch — the other built-and-sailed junk-rig Paradox on record, [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s [THU](/boats/thu.md), was purpose-designed.[1] His verdict on the conversion, and his full account of the build, costs, and how she sails, are recorded at [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md).

## On the group: a sailing life and a builder's counsel

Croeser later joined the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) as "Waldo Cruiser," where his posts round out both the sailing of "[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)" and his settled view of how to build one. He sailed her out of Perth — overnight rendezvous at the north end of **Garden Island**, day-sails from **Mangles Bay** — and reported her real numbers, sustained speeds to 5.5 knots and 80-nautical-mile overnight runs, all drawn out at [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md).[4]

His building advice is consistently that of a pragmatist who has finished a boat and sailed it hard: the Paradox is "one of the easiest boats you can build," best approached without anxiety about a perfect finish because "epoxy can fill the gaps"; construction-grade plywood is fine (and bends more easily than marine grade); he used **12 mm** for the bottom because 18 mm "was too heavy to carry," judging it "bullet proof" once glassed to the plans; and he skips foam insulation entirely, having had moisture build up and rot behind it before, lashing in empty plastic containers for buoyancy instead.[5] He also gave prospective builders a plain cost breakdown, the sail and trailer being the big items and the plywood the least of it.[6] He sold Planktos in July 2023 to **Kevin Williamson**, who kept her junk rig and later extended her mast, renaming her **"Skye"**; Croeser has stayed in the group cheering her on under her new name.[4]

## A 2012 mailing-list exchange, months before the build

Replying to [Alberto Callejon](/people/alberto-callejon.md)'s question whether anyone had sailed a Paradox under a junk rig, Croeser answered no, but that he had already converted an eighteen-foot fibreglass production boat to junk rig himself and planned to fit one to his own Paradox too, confident there are "many different ways to rig a junk rig" and that it could be adapted to suit. The exchange predates his own build by about four months, and the plan stated here is exactly what Planktos became: the archive's only Paradox converted from the stock lug to a junk rig.[7]

## A March 2013 mailing-list exchange: a stretching idea and general encouragement

Replying to a newcomer's plan to enlarge his own Paradox by roughly 20%, Croeser offered a distinct scaling idea from the ones already canvassed on the thread — keep the existing frame spacing and simply stretch the side panels longer, rather than widen the bays between them — alongside general encouragement: experimenting is what boatbuilding is all about, and Matt Layden himself only arrived at the Paradox design after quite a few earlier boats, so there was no reason not to continue that journey. The exchange came either just before or just as his own Planktos build got under way (see [Approach](#approach)).[8]

## An August 2014 exchange: confirming a fellow owner's voyages

Replying on a "Longer Cruises" thread, Croeser confirmed a fellow Western Australian Paradox owner's coastal voyages were genuine, adding that the owner had made a further round trip to the Abrolhos Islands and back to Perth the previous year. Full account at [Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md#a-distance-and-duration-recorded-for-the-perth-to-shark-bay-passage-and-an-abrolhos-round-trip-dated-to-2013).[9]

## A November 2014 exchange: the bulkhead side curve

Replying on a "Bulkhead side curvature" thread, Croeser gave the plainest answer on record for why a bulkhead's sides carry a slight convex curve: the hull's side panels are themselves curved in the third dimension, and the bulkhead's sides simply follow that shape — "no other reason." Fuller exchange at [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight).[10]

## A March 2015 exchange: relaying Geoff Davis's own account of the 2009 capsize

Replying on a "New Member now capsize" thread that had turned to a reported 2009 capsize of [Geoff Davis](/boats/small-fry.md)'s "Small Fry" off Fremantle, Croeser said he had been in contact with Davis for some years and that Davis had told him about the incident personally, leaving the telling of the story itself to Davis; what Croeser understood was that Davis recovered the boat, repaired a hole in the hull's side, and built a new mast, before going on to further long coastal voyages — to the Abrolhos Islands and back to Perth in 2013, and to Shark Bay in 2014. He also mentioned having previously owned a Vertue, "Yacabba," since sold, that Davis had sailed aboard with him, and judged Paradox shares most of a Vertue's attributes on a smaller scale. Full account of the capsize itself at [Small Fry — an unconfirmed 2009 report](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source).[11]

The Vertue comparison drew a dissenting reply from a fellow owner, who judged the two designs "very dissimilar" beyond their difference in size.[12]

## A further exchange, the same day: a stability checklist and a theory of the capsize

Still on the same thread, Croeser shared an outside naval-architecture article applying a standard seaworthiness checklist to Paradox — not overly beamy, heavy for her size, a favourable prismatic coefficient, a moderate ballast ratio, a relatively heavy rig damping her roll, and a good ratio of hull above to below the water — and added that the mast's own buoyancy when submerged adds further to her ultimate stability. Drawing on his own experience building a free-standing mast and checking Paradox's mast design with the same calculations, he judged it strong, and considered but declined a mast tabernacle in favour of the stock design's simplicity. Relaying Geoff Davis's own belief that the rudder had given way first in the 2009 capsize, he added that any Paradox's rudder is worth building strong given how much force passes through it. Full account at [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources), [rig — mast](/components/rig/mast.md#a-further-2015-idea-a-keel-mounted-built-in-tabernacle), and [Small Fry — an unconfirmed 2009 report](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source).[13]

## An April 2015 exchange: lining up the rudder fittings

Croeser shared a dummy jig for lining up his rudder's top and bottom fittings without guesswork — two wooden off-cuts and a length of stainless-steel rod sighting the true line between them — along with a caution that the rudder blade must be cut back exactly as the plan shows, or it will strike the hull as the rudder swings to starboard. Full technique at [rudder — lining up the top and bottom fittings](/components/rudder.md#lining-up-the-top-and-bottom-rudder-fittings-with-a-dummy-jig).[14]

## Earlier projects

Planktos was not his first boat. The association's newsletter refers readers to its **June–July 2014** and **April–May 2015** issues for his past projects.[1] Neither issue is held in the archive, so nothing further about those boats is on record.
