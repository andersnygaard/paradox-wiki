# John Jamieson

**John Jamieson** lives near **Kalispell, Montana**, about seventeen miles west of town in a log cabin in the woods, and describes himself as thirty-five years a shipwright, having built and repaired "dozens and dozens (probably hundreds)" of boats.[1][2] Before that he served as First Mate and Relief Captain aboard the schooner **Zodiac**, a 160 ft vessel drawing 16 ft and working out of Seattle in winter and Bellingham the rest of the year, too large, he found, for the small coves and beaches of Puget Sound, the San Juans and the Canadian Gulf Islands that a boat as small as Paradox could actually reach.[3] He answered building questions with the authority of his trade between 2007 and 2009, mentioning once that he intended to start a Paradox of his own, possibly the enlarged version, once his own shop in Montana was finished; no start or progress on one is on record.[3]

## Disambiguation

He used two different accounts three years apart: `schoonertrash` in 2007, then `jjamieson2` from 2008 on. The two are treated as one person on a two-signal basis: the name is unchanged across both, and the 2008 account's own account of his career aboard the schooner Zodiac corroborates the questions sent under the 2007 handle.[3]

## Thirty-five years a shipwright

His professional judgment was sought out directly more than once. Asked whether cheaper AC exterior plywood could stand in for marine grade, he answered from decades of repair work rather than theory: he had never traced a structural failure to AC grade's wood or glue, the real difference from marine ply being appearance, a rougher face, fewer plies, with only 3/8 in AC judged too thin, at three plies, for some jobs; plywood strength, in his experience, comes as much from ply count as from the quality of the wood or glue.[2] Asked for the stock hull's dry weight against a 20% scale-up's own figures, he offered roughly 450 lb, hedged with a self-deprecating "I don't know, maybe I made that part up."[4] On a separate scaled-up-hull question he wondered, out of curiosity, why a skeg version had been chosen over the standard rudder, a question that went unanswered.[5] Encouraging a hesitant first-time builder worried by a long list of unfamiliar procedures, he recalled teaching an evening wooden-boat-repair class in Juneau, Alaska, where one student bought his first piece of wood, took it home and still had not made the first cut a year later, the board hidden under his bed the whole time out of fear of getting started; his own advice was to get all the advice available and jump in regardless, since "its only a piece of wood, or a jug of epoxy not brain surgery."[6] He offered, unprompted, to copy his own construction manual for anyone who wanted one, asking nothing in return beyond a beer sometime.[7]

## A newcomer's questions, then an old hand's answers

Jamieson's own early questions, in 2007, are a newcomer's: whether any existing self-steering gear could be adapted to a Paradox, what peel ply is and where to buy it in the US, how to make or modify an epoxy pump, and whether a bronze rudder gudgeon could be cast in place of the plans' glass-filled one.[8][9][10][11] By the time he reappeared in 2008 he had become one of the people other builders turned to. Skeptical of a fellow builder's plan to sail a finished Paradox to Hawaii, he questioned the goal itself rather than the boat's capability: building her, learning to sail, learning navigation and surviving a first crossing to nearby Catalina Island were difficulty enough without adding an ocean passage, and he judged almost anyone who builds a boat without first owning one or two beforehand at least somewhat disappointed with the choice, though he found the enthusiasm refreshing regardless.[12] His fullest technical contribution is the plain woodworking definition of a **dado** that closed out a rudder-blade question on Rodney Cannon's own build: a groove sawn or routered into a piece of wood, usually to a set width so as to accept a mating piece of the same width, most commonly cut with a dado blade on a table saw or a router fitted with a straight bit, "Watch your fingers..."[13] He also proposed a shorter, wider test chine-runner for a scaled-up 28 ft Bolger hybrid design, built from foam skinned in glass and epoxy so it would stay light, easy to shape and easy to remove or modify if it did not work,[14] and asked, when a considerably larger relative of the design surfaced, who had actually drawn it.[15] Congratulating Stephen Yahn on Ironsides' own launch drew nothing more than plain good wishes: "Congratulations Stephen!!" and, separately, "Nice job. Beautiful boat!"[16][17]
