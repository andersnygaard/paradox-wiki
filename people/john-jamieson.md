# John Jamieson

**John Jamieson** lives in **northwest Montana**, in a log cabin in the woods.[1] He describes himself as thirty-five years a shipwright who has built and repaired "dozens and dozens (probably hundreds)" of boats.[2] He served before that as First Mate and Relief Captain aboard the schooner **Zodiac**, a 160 ft vessel drawing 16 ft that worked out of Seattle and Bellingham, too large, he found, for the small coves and beaches of Puget Sound, the San Juans and the Canadian Gulf Islands that a boat as small as Paradox can reach.[3] He intended to build a Paradox himself, perhaps the enlarged version, once his own shop in Montana was finished.[3] His trade gave him a shipwright's judgment on plywood grades and joinery, and he held that building one is more a matter of persistence than skill.

## A shipwright's judgment

Marine grade and AC exterior plywood differ mainly in appearance, a rougher face and fewer plies, rather than in strength: across thirty-five years of repair work he never traced a structural failure to AC grade's own wood or glue, though the thinnest common AC stock, 3/8 in at three plies, he judged too weak for some jobs, since a plywood's strength comes as much from its ply count as from the quality of its wood or glue.[2] A dado, the joint the plans call for at the tank dividers, is a groove sawn or routed to a set width so a mating piece fits into it, usually cut with a dado blade on a table saw or a router fitted with a straight bit.[4] Full account: [Plywood grades](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2009-recurrence-doubling-thinner-sheets-instead-of-a-single-thick-panel-and-marine-versus-ac-grade-from-a-working-shipwrights-experience) and [Hull assembly sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#bulkhead-construction-details-not-spelled-out-on-the-plans).

## Building within reach

He held that most of building a boat is simply sticking with it long enough that the process does not outlast the enthusiasm for it, since none of the procedures are as complicated as they look before they are attempted and none of the wood involved costs very much.[5] Teaching an evening wooden-boat-repair class in Juneau, Alaska, he once watched a student buy his first piece of wood, take it home and still not have made a first cut a year later, the board hidden under his bed the whole time out of fear of getting started.[5] "Its only a piece of wood, or a jug of epoxy not brain surgery," was how he summed it up.[5] He also kept copies of his own construction manual on hand to give away, asking nothing beyond a beer sometime.[6] Full account: [Paradox as a first boat](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#an-itemized-list-of-first-timer-worries-and-why-mistakes-are-recoverable) and [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md#an-october-2009-recurrence-an-unmet-request-and-an-informal-alternative-to-the-manual).
