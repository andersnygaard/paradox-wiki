# Don Elliott

**Don Elliott** (also spelled "Elliot"), of Tomah, Wisconsin, was one of the earliest Paradox builders and, as of mid-2001, one of only two people known to have a sailing Paradox, the other being [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own boat.[1] He built his own heavily built Paradox, "[The Edge](/boats/edge.md)," authored the sixteen-part **"Building the Paradox"** construction guide that builders still buy years after his death, and served as a primary technical resource for the plans, insisting on strict fidelity to them as drawn. Away from Paradox he also drew a design of his own, the unbuilt micro-sailboat "[Illusion](/similar-boat-designs/don-elliotts-illusion.md)." He died on 2 December 2008, after a lengthy battle with cancer.[2][3]

## What he built and authored

Don's **"Building the Paradox"** series ran as a sixteen-installment feature in *Messing About In Boats* (vol. 14 nos. 9-24, September 1996 to May 1997), Parts 1-15 plus a Conclusion, and included a valued jig for building the coach roof.[1] For years the guide was sold only directly from Don. A 2003 print run of the booklet had some copies missing a chapter, and Don offered replacement pages directly to affected buyers.[4][5] See [Building the Paradox articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) and [the series' construction content](/references/building-paradox-series.md).

Beyond the technical articles, Don wrote a two-part essay, **"A Story, a True One,"** analyzing why Matt Layden's Paradox, the smallest, stubbiest and only fully enclosed boat in the fleet, beat lighter, faster kayaks, dinghies and multihulls over the 304-mile 2003 Everglades Challenge, crediting a weatherproof, sail-from-within cabin (letting its skipper rest and hold a course in any weather without exposure to cold, spray or rain) as the decisive advantage over open, human- or wind-powered competitors, and concluding that the design's unconventional concept had been proven sound. He allowed a fellow builder to republish the essay years later on condition it not be edited.[6]

His own boat went formally unnamed for roughly two years, despite references to "Elliott's Paradox," until a UK builder's website photos led to the name **The Edge**. See [The Edge](/boats/edge.md#the-name) for the full record.

## Contributions and technical positions

- **Plans fidelity.** Don was a firm advocate of building to Matt Layden's plans to the letter, citing Sheet 1's specification of bronze ring nails as a permanent backup to the glue joint and urging that any deviation be cleared with Matt first.[7] See [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md).
- **Weight and ballast.** He supplied a full loaded-weight and ballast breakdown, his own lead-installation technique and photographs, and later a dedicated illustrated article on the ballast "pig" layout, sizing and floor-barrier detail.[8][9][10] See [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#the-ballast-pig-layout-in-full-don-elliott).
- **Panel layout.** He coached builders through the tight bulkhead-#2, transom and side-panel nesting, and estimated a Paradox should take about six months to build.[11] See [panel layout](/construction/panel-layout.md).
- **Hull assembly sequence.** He detailed pulling a hull together after the bulkheads are up: stem first, tank top and floor as one early-positioned piece, a hot wet blanket to soften side panels, dry-fitting to bevel joints.[12] See [hull assembly sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md).
- **Tacking technique.** He gave a detailed tacking-technique checklist, close-hauled tacking only, timing to wave crests, gentle rudder easing (no more than 30 degrees either side), downhaul and perpendicularity checks and a paddle in very light wind, holding that there was nothing wrong with Paradox's lateral plane or rudder size.[13][14] See [rudder and chine experiments](/components/rudder.md).
- **Against enlarging Paradox.** He argued against scaling the boat up: her width is fixed by the standard 4 ft plywood sheet, lengthening alone alters the displacement distribution, and a true enlargement demands a complete redesign with fresh calculations, model testing and revised scantlings, concluding a substantially enlarged boat would no longer be a Paradox in the designer's sense.[15] See [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).
- **Little Cruiser cutaway offer.** He offered to produce a free complete cutaway drawing of [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s interior for a planned book, given enough photos, sketches and dimensions, noting his understanding that no plans or drawings were ever made for Little Cruiser.[16]
- **Exterior-vs-marine plywood advice.** Answering a new builder's plywood question, he confirmed a good-quality Douglas fir exterior ply (not white pine) will serve structurally, holding the same glue as marine grade, but recommended marine grade for the side hull panels and cautioned against any deck plywood with thin outer veneers.[17] See [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-new-builders-exterior-vs-marine-choice-and-why-marine-usually-wins-on-labor).
- **Foam flotation over plywood.** He held that it was safe to bond foam flotation over epoxy-coated interior fir plywood, citing reassurances from System Three and West System, and planned a follow-up installation article.[18] See [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md).
- **Epoxy mixing.** He sharply disputed a round-vs-square mixing-container argument, holding that the great majority of bad epoxy mixes trace to measurement, temperature and attention lapses rather than container shape, and recommended a square plastic tub.[19] See [mixing container shape and stir time](/construction/epoxy-coating-technique.md#mixing-container-shape-and-stir-time).
- **Water tank storage alternative.** For a builder settling on fresh water rather than self-flooding ballast, he suggested building the tanks as drawn but with open, oval-shaped hatch lids in place of the sealed covers, so the space can double as dry stowage.[20][21] See [provisioning and water](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md#self-flooding-sea-water-ballast-tanks-feasible-but-risky).
- **A homemade hand sander.** A 15 in sander fitted with a 3 in (75 mm) wide grit pad made from the abrasive belt of a handheld belt sander, described in his "Building the Paradox" booklet and later used by another UK builder shaping a boom. See [builder's toolkit](/construction/builders-toolkit.md).[22]

## A separate, unbuilt design: "Illusion"

Before his death Don was working on a design of his own, entirely separate from Paradox: a 3 metre micro-sailboat named "Illusion," never completed. See [Don Elliott's "Illusion"](/similar-boat-designs/don-elliotts-illusion.md) for the design, her lineage and her reception.

## Founding and running his own companion group

Beyond the wider Paradox community, Don founded and moderated his own companion group on 16 November 2003, restricting discussion strictly to the Paradox as designed by Matt Layden, with no changes discussed, a policy strict enough that other correspondents warned newcomers about it before they joined.[23][24]

A 2011 recollection, offered in the course of a debate over starting a rival Facebook group, held Don's group as the very first Paradox group, strict enough to see a good many members expelled for disagreeing with him, and credited it with prompting the wider community's own group to form as a freer environment for dissenters, a claim not otherwise corroborated: the wider community's own July 2001 founding is credited elsewhere to Glen Maxwell, with Don already an active early contributor. See [references/mailing-list.md](/references/mailing-list.md#sibling-and-successor-groups).[25] A further, independent account in 2015 supports the expulsion claim specifically: a different correspondent recalled being banned from Don's group himself after proposing design changes, met with a response amounting to no deviation from the designer's wishes, and judged the group certainly controlled, if lacking the soul and diversity of the wider community that followed.[26]

More than a year after Don's death, a newcomer asking whether the wider community kept any FAQ file, covering junk rig, scantlings, stitch-and-glue construction and water ballast, was told that a good deal of the same information survived in Don's own group, worth checking in its own right and not least for his drawings, though how much longer it would stay online was itself uncertain.[27][28] A separate reply proposed downloading and storing all of Don's files before they were lost for good, the same preservation worry raised when the closure below was first announced.[29]

In June 2010 Don's own group closed outright. Spam had accumulated there over the years, since partly cleaned up by his son; Wanda sought volunteer moderators for it but, lacking the time or heart to keep the search going, gave notice the group would close regardless, while reaffirming that Don's articles and build manuals would remain available for purchase and that the family was considering a website of its own for his material.[30] Before the shutdown, a suggestion went round to download and repost its photographs and files elsewhere so the material would not be lost; preserving a closing group's whole content, it turned out, needed more than an ordinary member could manage, since only a moderator could capture everything across, and the correspondent who offered still could not have done it himself.[39][40]

## After his death

Don's death was announced directly to the community within days, in his widow Wanda Elliott's own words: he had continued to run his instructional-materials business almost to the end, and she intended to keep making his construction articles available for purchase, though her own limited boat-building knowledge meant she could not answer technical questions in his place; ordering procedures would continue as normal, with some initial delay, and she asked that orders be withheld until after 1 January 2009. The family planned to keep Don's own Paradox and sail her with the grandchildren, closing with the thought that Don had "sailed off into a new adventure."[3]

The news drew a personal note of condolence from [Alberto Callejon](/people/alberto-callejon.md), the Patagonian builder of "[Cachivache](/boats/cachivache.md)," who credited "the care and aid received from Don" with having helped him build his own boat.[31] Word of the death also reached at least one Paradox owner independently, over a month later, who remembered him simply as "a gifted artist" who "helped many builders understand the building process for the Paradox."[32] In 2011, confirming a new buyer's payment for the Paradox plans had arrived, [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md) offered his own assessment of Don's guides: their building techniques were mostly standard rather than unconventional, their real value lying in Don's illustrations and detailed instructions; he called Don's death a real loss to the Paradox design and to small boats generally, and regretted that Don had spent some of his energy arguing online with people who saw things differently.[33]

Demand for the manual outlived him by years. Ordering moved to Wanda, first by check or money order and later by PayPal, with the price settling near $18 by 2013; when a reply was slow, as it sometimes was in the family's early years running the business alone, she readily explained it, once simply that she had been busy with her grandchildren and had missed a payment notice.[34] Those who obtained a copy considered it worth the wait: one called it a terrific value even for a reader building nothing like a Paradox, since it presents several methods and techniques usable on other shop projects,[35] and a builder working through a multi-year build in Hawaii leaned on it at every phase, finding its drawings of the boat in use a continuing source of inspiration; full build account at [people/dave-skaife.md](/people/dave-skaife.md#building-the-boat-in-hawaii).[36] See [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md#a-further-august-2013-recurrence-encouragement-and-confirmation-the-manual-remains-available) for the fuller record of the manual's continuing demand.

Eight years after Don's death, "The Edge" was offered for sale in 2017, sound overall, needing only minor toe-rail and rudder attention, priced at $2,800 OBO with a trailer and some cruising gear included, and sold within about two weeks to **[Deric](/people/frederic-tallman.md)**, of New York State, who had bought Don's plans some five years earlier without ever starting to build, choosing instead to keep sailing an existing Herreshoff-designed cat ketch. He towed the finished boat home across five states from Wisconsin, and recalled Don as prolific well beyond Paradox: a catamaran, a houseboat, a wooden kayak kept by his son and an unbuilt interest in foiling boats besides his cartoons and other guides.[37][38] Full record of the sale and Deric's restoration work at [boats/edge.md: sold to Deric, and restoration begins (2017)](/boats/edge.md#sold-to-deric-and-restoration-begins-2017).
