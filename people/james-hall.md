# James Hall

**James "Jim" Hall** was an English sailor from **Dorset**. He had already built a 21 foot yacht of his own and sailed her single-handed to distant places before he bought **"[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"** from her builder, [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md), in 2008.[1][2][3] He sailed her out of Poole Harbour and along England's South West coast for eight years, mostly alone and usually on passages of about ten days, until his death in 2016. He was open that he had bought Faith rather than built her, sailing under false colours, as he put it, among a list of owners who mostly had built their own.[4] Full build and sailing record: [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md).

## Before Paradox

About thirty years before he bought Faith, Hall owned and sailed a **YM Goosander 23**.[5] The 21 foot yacht he built and sailed alone to distant places belongs to the same period: by the time he came to Paradox ownership he was an accomplished, self-reliant sailor rather than a newcomer to small boats.[1]

## Sailing Faith, mostly alone

Hall's pattern of cruising set him apart from the other UK owners he knew: rather than day sails or short weekends, he favoured long solo passages, typically around ten days at a stretch, and had managed four such cruises in a single year by 2012, in his own words "'Nobby No Mates' - that's me - an anti social loner....."[6] He knew [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) well enough to cross paths with him while sailing, but had, as of that year, still not met [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md) or seen his boat in person.[6] A twelve-day cruise that same summer took Faith through the Solent and Chichester Harbour, launched and recovered at Ridge Wharf; one hard beat back to Poole from Oxey Lake against a Force 5-6 westerly sent him looking afterward for a better-shaped replacement sail.[7][8]

He timed his launches around the tide rather than his own convenience: the boatyard he used only allowed its slipway and storage compound to be reached while staff were on site, so he would launch onto the dry slipway itself while the tide was still out, secure the car and trailer in the compound before the water returned and be away over the horizon by the time the yard locked up for the night, the same technique, for a different reason, that Alastair Law used habitually aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)."[9]

He ran a 2.3 hp Honda outboard, kept as light as possible since he judged extra weight aft undesirable, and rerouted the mainsheet across the aft deck to keep it clear of the engine.[10] Between the sail, that outboard and a yuloh he reshaped with slightly more twist than Bill Serjeant had originally given it, Faith gave him three independent ways to make way, and after he fitted a new, higher-peaked sail he rarely needed the engine at all.[11] He had no firsthand experience of her construction to offer on chine-log technique, not having built the boat himself, but he judged Serjeant had made an excellent job of her.[12]

## Keeping an older boat going

Faith came to him without foam flotation at all, a choice Serjeant had made deliberately while building her, trading the buoyancy for storage and living space, and Hall improvised his own emergency buoyancy in its place: the forward compartment, awkward to reach for gear storage anyway, packed with empty plastic screw-top bottles; the stern end carrying hollow plastic fenders, a part-full water can, a home-made portable toilet and snap-lidded dry-food containers for buoyancy that varied with how much food and water happened to be aboard. Years of accumulated gear under the cabin sole, a 15 kg take-apart storm anchor and 15 m of chain, tools, spares, a Honda outboard, a large battery, had made the boat steadily heavier despite his own wish to stop adding to it, and he fitted a manual bilge pump under the sole as a result.[13] He questioned the published buoyancy figures rather than take them on trust, working from Faith's own known displacement to judge how much trapped air a swamped hull would actually need to stay afloat, and concluded a more modest scheme was the realistic one: enough foam to keep her from sinking outright, accepting that a large enough hole would defeat self-recovery regardless.[14][15]

A serious crack turned up at both ends of the trailer's axle beam in 2011, traced to the suspension units' near total lack of springing under load, a ride harsh enough to scatter the boat's contents around the cabin.[2] Rather than patch it, he built a replacement trailer around a rear axle salvaged from a scrapped van, mostly from steel angle to avoid trapped, rusting sea water, with a tipping bed that lets the boat run off the rollers under gravity even on a level, dry surface, finished and running by December 2011.[16]

He kept a charcoal heater built from copper pipe for cold weather cruising, supplemented on occasion by an unvented kerosene pressure lantern for extra warmth on short spells with the hatch cracked to vent the fumes.[17][18] He treated the heater as more habit than necessity, joking that Faith was already "fully insulated by the junk all around the inside."[19] In 2015 he epoxied a strip of stainless steel to the stem's curved lower section to protect it from wear.[20]

## Running her singlehanded

He fitted an electric tiller pilot rather than build a wind vane: an outboard already hung weight off the stern, a vane would have needed to lift clear for shallow water just as the rudder did and the standard rig carries no headsail for a sail-actuated downwind system.[21] Tiller-fixed self-steering held a course well enough to windward until the wind itself shifted, but running before a big following sea defeated the tiller pilot's own correction, struggling enough that he expected a wind vane would do better.[22] His settled practice was to reef early rather than push the gear, switching to hand steering for a while when the autopilot began to hunt and keeping its duty cycle down to a fifth of the time or less to spare the battery, charged through the long summer days by a solar panel.[21]

## A cruising blog

From 2014 he kept a personal blog, `jimtimes.wordpress.com`, recording his own cruises around England's coast.[23]
