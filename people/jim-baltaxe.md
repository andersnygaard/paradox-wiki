# Jim Baltaxe

**Jim Baltaxe** is from **Wellington, New Zealand**. In 2008 he ordered a set of stock Paradox plans, which reached him within about two weeks despite the distance.[1] He is known for two things: a composite-construction and chine-runner method drawn from a vacuum-infusion workshop he attended, and ideas on safety, lighting, propulsion and rigging.

## Composite construction and chine runners

A vacuum-infusion workshop run by Derek Kelsall lays vinylester resin over a foam core to produce completely fair, flat panels that can then be bent into shape once cured, a process he calls "Magic" next to ordinary hand-laid glasswork. He favors vinylester over epoxy generally for its lower risk of skin sensitization, despite a stronger, more unpleasant odour and the same handling precautions. The workshop left him weighing a foam-and-glass Paradox rather than plywood, purely for the challenge and the practice, before he chose the stock plywood plans instead.[2] Full account: [Epoxy brands and chemistry](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#a-compositevacuum-infusion-workshop-and-a-speculative-foamglass-paradox).

A chine runner's core suits closed-cell foam, blue insulation foam or PVC foam, better than solid timber blocks: since the runner is not structural and is sheathed in glass regardless of the core material, foam bends into shape even after the hull has already been faired, avoiding the steaming or block-by-block lamination solid timber needs.[3] Cutting the bottom panel slightly wide of the hull sides before fitting it leaves a fair-curved shelf running the hull's length, forming the runner's bottom surface, with the foam core laminated onto the shelf's top, a construction that also answers a grounding-durability concern since the extended bottom sheet itself takes a boat's first impact.[4] Full account: [Chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#a-closed-cell-foam-alternative-and-a-2007-durability-exchange).

## Safety, lighting, propulsion and rigging

A heat source in the cabin, even a candle and diffuser, can generate carbon monoxide, and Paradox's own good ventilation may not be enough to prevent serious health problems from it, an insidious risk since the early symptoms, lethargy and aches, are easily mistaken for a coming flu rather than any obvious alarm.[5] Full account: [Cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-carbon-monoxide-caution-for-candle-and-diffuser-heaters).

A mast light can be an LED made for a garden-umbrella pole, donut-shaped and reasonably waterproof, or a complete solar shed light wired so its charger sits near the deck while the light itself mounts partway up the mast.[6] For propelling a Paradox from inside the cabin, one idea was a Hobie Mirage Drive pedal unit fitted in a well near the second bulkhead, recessed into the hull bottom so it could stay fitted permanently without much added drag and clear of grounding damage, set aside again in favor of the yuloh.[7] A PDRacer video's roller-furling system suggested that Paradox's own halyard could be made continuous with the reefing line to save clutter on so small a boat, an idea already working well aboard [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md).[8] Full account: [Noli](/boats/noli.md#paint-advice-for-a-fellow-uk-builder-and-cabin-sole-matting-and-small-gear-for-noli), [Auxiliary and outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#pedal-power-and-why-a-mirage-drive-does-not-suit-paradox) and [Furling system](/components/rig/furling.md#alastairs-fuller-walkthrough-and-a-continuous-halyardfurling-line-innovation).
