# Nels Arvidson

**Nels Arvidson** is a Canadian design correspondent who wrote to the Paradox community intermittently for nearly fifteen years, from September 2002 to April 2017, weighing in on hull materials, chine-runner mechanics, ballast placement and the case for a larger Paradox. Over two days in March 2003 he sent 8 of the 17 messages the community exchanged about Matt Layden's Everglades Challenge win, 47 percent and more than double the next most active correspondent, and gave the community its own copy of the official results, headed "MATT WINS!," while no rival had yet reached the fourth checkpoint. He later organized a volunteer project to translate Sven Yrvind's book into English, lining up translators chapter by chapter. His own sailing was done in a Bolger "Micro," kept outdoors year-round and sailed for more than a decade, and in his later years he gathered ballast and drew up modifications for a Jim Michalak "Robbsboat." He grew up servicing weather equipment and training radio operators by light plane across the western Hudson Bay coast, and settled on the South Thompson River near Chase, British Columbia.

## Chronicling the 2003 Everglades Challenge

Over 11 and 12 March 2003, Nels carried Matt Layden's Everglades Challenge news to the Paradox community faster than anyone else. He put Layden roughly 7.5 hours ahead of the second-place boat by Checkpoint 4, [1] credited Layden's roller-launching design as the decisive factor letting him sail and sleep underway while paddled competitors had to land to rest and reported that Layden had broken off mid-race to rescue and tow ashore a capsized kayaker. [2] He then gave the community the race's official result, headed "MATT WINS!": Layden finished the 304-mile course at 17:51 on 11 March, with no rival yet past the fourth checkpoint, [3] and summed it up as Layden having "created a class of his own! The microcruiser class." [4] The fuller results table and the field's own checkpoint standings are recorded on [Original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md).

## A correspondent on hull materials, chine runners and rig design

He first wrote to the community in September 2002, hunting down Don Elliott's paid Paradox-building articles, and was pointed instead to Charles Wilson's free plans CD. [5][6]

Hull materials became one of his recurring subjects. In December 2003 he argued against building Paradox in steel at her size, judging her plywood-and-glass construction already tough enough and weighing a stitch-and-glue-plus-bilge-keels alternative alongside a welded-aluminum hull with an inflatable collar. [7][8][9][10][11] He returned to the question in 2008, advising a builder in Patagonia against steel directly: plywood floats and insulates even flooded, where steel does not, and a comparably sized steel design runs bigger and costlier than Paradox. [12] See [aluminum and steel Paradox](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md).

He was also a reliable explainer of how chine runners work, tracing the effect to a box hull's own resistance to sideways slippage and arguing that having no keel below the hull is itself a safety feature off a lee shore, letting a boat slide sideways rather than trip. [13][14][15][16] See [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md).

He repeatedly made the case for a larger Paradox, and in 2008 was the one who confirmed that Matt Layden had approved a 20% scale-up in principle, though of the roughly 40 members who had floated the idea over the years nobody had yet built one and reported back. [17][18][19][20] Weighing his own build the same year, and judging the stock Paradox too small for two aboard, he considered Matt Layden's smaller Enigma as a solo alternative before settling on a Bolger Micro instead. [21] See [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md).

## Organizing the Yrvind book translation, and other community service

He organized a volunteer project to translate Sven Yrvind's book into English, working out a chapter-by-chapter pipeline and lining up translators himself, though the translation work itself was done by others. [22][23][24][25] See [Sven Yrvind's book](/references/yrvind-book.md).

He also proposed distributing the Paradox plans through Duckworks rather than directly through the Bolducs, with a periodic royalty paid to Matt Layden and the Elliott family, reasoning that a faster, more reliable path to the plans would reach more of the builders who wanted them. [26] See [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md).

Recommending reading material to the community, he recalled dropping in unannounced years earlier on Harold "Dynamite" Payson to buy a set of "Cartopper" plans, and being invited to spend the whole afternoon with Payson, his wife and their son. [27]

## A life of small boats: a Bolger Micro, a Robbsboat, and wilderness travel

His own sailing was done in a Bolger "Micro," kept outdoors year-round and sailed for more than twelve years by 2008; her Cetol-finished brightwork, house-paint interior and Brightsides topsides were showing their age by then, and he judged tinted Lexan windows the best protection paint and cushions could get from the sun. [28]

In his later years he took on a second small-boat project entirely apart from Paradox: a Jim Michalak "Robbsboat," for which he collected about 150 lb of vinyl-coated cast-iron gym weights as ballast, weighing firebricks as an alternative. [29] He drew up a fuller set of modifications for her: extending the cabin forward for more support under the lowered sail, more sitting room and more shelter for two; toe rails along the raised deck's outer edges; a system to deploy and retrieve a bow anchor without going forward at all; and, at the design's own recommendation, a standard leeboard that, fully raised, might double as a forward walkway. He weighed a full-length Birdwatcher-style "slot top" as a further alternative, noted that Sven Yrvind's own latest build shared the same no-side-decks arrangement and kept a cheap, easy-to-make polytarp junk rig under consideration, the whole project conceived as an "extend the season canoe" for protected water. [30][31]

Small boats had been a fixture of his life for years before any of this. Apartment and rented housing came with its own storage problems, solved one boat at a time: a solo canoe kept on a car roof rack year-round, a folding Folbot in an apartment's own storage spot, a small dinghy carried and, once, slept in aboard a van, and a rented covered space shared with neighbours building a canoe and, later, an airplane. [32] He had put in years of wilderness canoeing too, 20-25 miles some days, occasionally under an improvised tarp sail. [33]

He closed one debate about carrying a motor by quoting Stan Rogers's "Make and Break Harbour" on a small engine's place aboard an old working boat; his own home water's current ran too swift to paddle against, so his trips there ran downstream rather than up. [34] When a newcomer dreamed of sailing his own Paradox to Hawaii, Nels suggested simply flying out on a seat sale instead, recalling his own Maui holiday of snorkeling trips and a drive up Haleakalā. [35] It was a recommendation earned the hard way: decades of white-knuckle bush flying around the Canadian Arctic, in aircraft no newer than a 1950s DC-3, had left him under no illusions about flying, but weighed against a commercial jet or an LA freeway at rush hour, he allowed even a small-plane crossing might come out ahead. [36] Weighing Paradox against his own Micro late in his correspondence, he judged there was no real controversy over the design among anyone who had actually built, sailed or seen one, only a narrow niche shaped by cruising ground, skill and whether a sailor typically sails alone; his own preference stayed with the Micro's four-person day-sailing capacity, her centerline engine and her cat-yawl rig, without dampening his own stated wish to build a Paradox as well. [37] The wish went back to his first month of correspondence: if he built one, he already meant to simplify it, with "no water tanks and a few other changes." [38]
