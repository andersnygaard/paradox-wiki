# Sybe Starkenburg

**Sybe Starkenburg** is a bookshop owner in **Montagu, South Africa**, who introduced himself to the [Paradox](/paradox.md) mailing list at **67 years old** in May 2017, already building his own boat.[1] Welcomed onto the list with the reminder that "questions build the community,"[7] he thanked the group for its support on "a big project."[8]

## The bulkhead-1 taper, and a compound curve (May 2017)

His first question was why the plans show a 5mm gap between bulkhead 1 and its own outer cleat.[1] The taper exists because the hull narrows toward the bow, so a bulkhead's side must run smaller toward the front to keep a flat surface for gluing against the hull — a fit that only reads correctly viewed from directly above the boat, not as the plans draw it from behind — and the same taper combines with a small offset curve into a genuine compound curve, lofted by springing a batten through the chine, a 5mm centre mark, and the gunwale.[2][3][4] Thanked for the answer.[5][6] Full technique: [construction/panel-layout.md — bulkhead side curves](/construction/panel-layout.md#bulkhead-side-curves).

## Laying out the starboard side, transom, and bulkhead 2 (June 2017)

By June he had begun marking out his own starboard side panel forward, the transom, and bulkhead #2 directly on the plywood, and ran into the same tight fit at that joint the plans have presented builders since 2001: a roughly 35mm overhang at the transom's bottom, and a similar small overhang at bulkhead 2's own butt joint that would not fit the panel as marked.[9][10] Reassured that other Paradox builders had hit the identical geometry without complaint, and advised to double-check his own marking-out before assuming a plan error; a related question of his own — whether deck beams run to the side of the bulkheads or to the cleats — was answered too: both are fastened to the bulkheads, and it makes no difference which one runs further into the corner, since the corner gets notched out regardless.[11] Thanked the answer.[12] A second thought refined it further: the transom's aft edge does need to overhang a little to stay flush at the 50mm forward point, bevelled to the side panel's own curvature, but not by the full 35mm first reported.[13] Full technique and the plans' own tight-fit precedent: [construction/panel-layout.md — tight spots builders hit](/construction/panel-layout.md#tight-spots-builders-hit).

A follow-up question about the transom panel's own height arithmetic ran through several attempts before it resolved: first asking whether the deck beam's 25mm thickness adds directly to the transom's 459mm centerline height,[14] clarified that 25mm is only the beam's own thickness and that the panel's curvature follows the same drop-per-100mm figures used across the bulkheads generally,[15] thanked in turn;[16] a further attempt at the same arithmetic still came up about 42mm short of the expected total, leaving him unsure where his own reasoning had gone wrong.[17] The reasoning was in fact correct — the answering builder's own hull, a 20% scaled-up Paradox with a different cutting pattern, could not be checked directly against it, but he suggested swapping the transom panel with bulkhead #2's own smaller piece, just 451mm at its widest point, which resolved the fit.[18] Confirmed he now knew how to carry on.[19]

## A windvane sketch requested (July 2017)

Asked to see [Roselt Croeser](/people/roselt-croeser.md)'s own windvane steering rig for "[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)" — an exchange that prompted an account of a vane once built and later set aside; full account at [self-steering — a 2017 build from the Wharram sketch, tried and set aside](/operations/self-steering.md#a-2017-build-from-the-wharram-sketch-tried-and-set-aside).[20]

## A cleat-gluing question (July 2017)

Asked which face of a cleat glues to the panel — cleats vary in size, 12 x 19 mm generally and 25 x 30 mm at bulkhead 2 — and was pointed to sheet 4 of the plans, where the larger face is directly visible; found a PDF's on-screen scale harder to measure than a printed sheet, but planned to try anyway. Full technique: [construction/panel-layout.md — cleat gluing orientation](/construction/panel-layout.md#cleat-gluing-orientation-and-calibrating-a-scale-directly-off-the-drawings).[21][22]

## Fitting the sheer strake (April 2018)

By April 2018 he had reached the sheer strake, running into serious trouble fitting it from frame 3 aft to the transom, where it curves sharply downward while also following the hull's own curvature — a genuine compound bend in two directions, and one he had no idea how to approach.[23] Advised to steam the timber into place and hold the shape with plenty of epoxy — the same technique already used on the equally troublesome chine logs, with lamination as the sanctioned fallback if steaming alone proved insufficient; full technique and further firsthand accounts: [construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md — a 2018 recurrence](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md#an-october-2015-recurrence-a-rain-soak-and-heat-gun-result-and-a-steaming-in-a-bag-alternative).[24] Chose to laminate rather than steam.[25]

## Chine-runner fitting order questioned (June 2018)

Noticing from photos that most builders finish the hull's bottom before starting the chine runners, asked whether anything argued against reversing that order — fitting the runners first, the bottom sheets after.[26] Confirmed there was no reason not to.[27] A fuller, worked-out answer followed from a fellow builder who had used a hybrid sequence of his own — full technique at [chine-runners/construction.md — fitting order](/chine-runners/construction.md#fitting-order-blank-before-or-after-the-bottom-panel).[28]

By this stage the hull's side panels, transom, bulkheads, and sheer strakes are assembled and the chine runners are under way; no boat name is yet on record.

## Rudder gluing, and a gudgeon-casting attempt (March-April 2019)

By 2019, work had reached the rudder: asked whether epoxy mixed with cotton flox is right for gluing the rudder plywood, and whether to follow Don Elliott's own build-manual recipe for the gudgeon — epoxy, cotton flox, and chopped-strand glass fibres worked into a putty; see [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — a 2019 recurrence](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#a-2019-recurrence-gluing-generally-and-a-chopped-strandcloth-gudgeon-build) for the reply this drew.[29] A first attempt at casting the gudgeon three weeks later went wrong: mixing 250 ml of epoxy thickened with micro glass fibres and glass-matting fibres in a single container, the mix heated up and bubbled its way out of the mold before curing, at a moderate 21°C outside temperature.[30] Replies converged on two fixes for the exotherm that had bubbled the casting out of its mold: keep the batch smaller and spread it thinner in a flat, open tray rather than a deep container, or pour it in small stages, letting each stage pass its own peak heat before adding the next; full technique: [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — controlling exotherm in a small epoxy casting](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#controlling-exotherm-in-a-small-epoxy-casting-april-2019).[31] Mixed smaller and poured in stages on the next attempt, the gudgeon casting went well; a further question followed about the top pintle's diameter and matching Wicox part numbers, answered from an existing rudder-hardware build rather than confirmed against the commercial part itself — see [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — controlling exotherm in a small epoxy casting](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#controlling-exotherm-in-a-small-epoxy-casting-april-2019) for the answer.[32] A further exchange weeks later turned to how the finished gudgeon should be fastened to the transom — sealant and bolts, following Don Elliott's own build guide, or epoxy directly — with practice on record split between the two and no single method settled; see [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — fastening the finished gudgeon, not casting it](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#a-may-2019-recurrence-fastening-the-finished-gudgeon-not-casting-it).[33] Settled on both together — sealant on the bolts, plus epoxy bonding the gudgeon itself to the transom, reasoning a bonded gudgeon could still be cut free later if it ever needed replacing.[34]

## Deck curvature, and a lead shortage in South Africa (September 2019)

By September 2019, work had reached the deck and the ballast: asked whether the deck plywood needs wetting to curve without splitting, and, separately, since lead was proving hard to source locally, what else might serve as ballast.[35] Told wetting was unnecessary on an already-built hull, and, for the ballast question, offered a plain suggestion of scrap steel bars in place of lead.[36] A third reply agreed the plywood would bend without wetting and added a second ballast alternative: melted-down scrap lead in the form of wheel-balancing weights, obtainable free from a tyre shop that otherwise has to pay to have them taken away.[37]

## The hull finished (October 2019)

Replying to the mailing list's move to groups.io, reported that the hull itself was finished, asking to be carried over to the new group.[38] By April 2020 he had reached the cabin, cutting the cabin deck, and asked whether to enlarge the hatch beyond its plans size of roughly 870mm — answered simply no by an established builder; see [components/hatch.md — sizing the hatch to your own hull](/components/hatch.md#sizing-the-hatch-to-your-own-hull).[39] By August 2020 he had reached the hatch's own hardware, asking why the plans call for carriage bolts on its outer rail rather than screws or nails; see [components/hatch.md — the outer rail's carriage bolts](/components/hatch.md#the-outer-rails-carriage-bolts) for the two answers it drew.[40] No boat name is yet on record.

## The masthead sheave and rope diameters (June 2021)

By June 2021 — about four years into the build — he reached the mast and rigging, asking why the masthead sheave sits at a 60-degree angle and what rope diameters the plans call for on the halyard and sheet.[41] Thanked for a fuller explanation of the sheave's own orientation, and separately asked whether it could be built to a slightly larger diameter to take a heavier halyard — left unanswered on record.[42] Full technical detail: [components/rig/mast.md — why the masthead sheave sits at 60 degrees](/components/rig/mast.md#why-the-masthead-sheave-sits-at-60-degrees-and-the-halyard-sheet-and-reef-line-diameters).

## Window screws (October 2021)

By October 2021 he had reached the windows, asking whether they are fastened with screws or bolts, what size, and what interval to space them at.[43] Full answer, a proven 19-year-old fastening spec: [construction/window-glazing-materials.md — a proven window-fastening screw size](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#a-proven-window-fastening-screw-size-2021).

## A celestial navigation app (December 2021)

By December 2021 — describing himself as a Paradox builder "almost completed," and a retired mariner and navigator — he built a **StarFinder app for Android**, a tool for finding stars and calculating a line of position, with the sun, moon, and planets also included, and asked for volunteers to beta-test it ahead of a Google Play release.[44]
