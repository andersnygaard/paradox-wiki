# Nickolas Bell

**Nickolas Bell** is an **Australian** builder and sailor, active from the mid-2010s into January 2025. He built a heavily laminated [Paradox](/paradox.md), **"[Thor](/boats/thor.md),"** in South Australia, launched her on the Murray River in June 2015, and sailed her hard for years before selling her on: "I had a paradox, I sold it after years of adventure."[1] The buyer, in February 2022, was **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)**, who renamed her **"Pamela B."**[2][3] Bell is known on the record for a fastening method that puts no screw into raw wood, for sailing Thor hard enough to know exactly where and why a Paradox broaches and for what he did after selling her: a tiny home-built pontoon houseboat, ridden **1,550 km down the Murray** in **79 days** from **Yarrawonga**, steered only by an idling motor, a trip he judges a Paradox could have made just as well, the shallows if anything favouring the smaller boat.[1]

## A fastening method: nothing driven into raw wood

Bell's hull went together dry: screwed, not glued, and "measured and measured" until he was satisfied it was symmetrical: "It took me forever." Only then was it glued, and days later every screw came back out, its hole backfilled with a wooden dowel, so no metal fastener was left in the wood to heat and crack the ply as it cured. Any screw a fitting genuinely needed later went into a hole drilled oversize and backfilled with resin and powder, driven into the cured resin rather than raw wood: "the long way around but it never leads to cracks."[4]

The core of it, a temporary screw pulled once the glue cures, is [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md)'s own idea, first recorded in 2001. Bell gives the fullest step-by-step account of the technique on record, and the wooden-dowel backfill and the two-stage resin-plug method for a later screw are his own additions to it, not recorded from anyone else. His advice to newer builders is of a piece with the whole approach: "remember it's about sailing time too, build to a standard that makes it very strong, the finish is acceptable and get sailing!"[5] He is just as quick with a compliment for someone else's build, telling a fellow builder plainly: "I've built one, and your looks perfect."[5a] The same jig-and-clamp discipline crossed over from the boat: laminating beams for the deck and cabin taught him a trick he later reused just for the fun of it, building a café chair the same way from lumber-yard offcuts and leftover resin.[6]

Bell owns more than one boat. Alongside "Thor," a second mast belongs to a Stevensons Cruiser, and its earlier rigging once jammed the sail locked fast up while motoring in toward a marina, the blocks bound tight by rope pressure after the sail had been laced too tight; forcing the anchor over the side to slow his approach, he narrowly avoided a collision with a large, expensive cruiser, then climbed the mast "like a monkey on a slippery pole" and hung from the sail until it came free, bruised, sore and thoroughly embarrassed. Staggering the throat and peak halyard blocks so they angle apart fixed it for good; the rig now looks "awful," but it does not bind.[7]

The Stevensons Cruiser itself is a hard-chined, flat, wide catboat with no weighted keel, his first build and by far the easiest of his boats, kept mainly to hone building skills. She sails well enough in fair weather to be enjoyable but proved a lakes-and-rivers craft rather than an offshore one, taking on spray and riding uncomfortably when he took her to sea despite her own built-in flotation, and she is "no adventure machine" next to a well-built Paradox.[8] He has put the count of his boats at four in one telling and three in another, a discrepancy left as it stands.[8][9]

## Sailing Thor hard

Bell's sailing testimony is unusually specific about the boat's limits. Running in a seaway she will broach: "In a running sea with waves on a windy day mine has broached too. You need to concentrate when surfing down a wave it's very likely." The boat stays dry inside and the ballast keeps her stable, "fun and a challenge" so long as he is not too tired, in which case his rule is to reef hard and set a course well off the wind. A fouled sail wrapped round the mast "looks like an ice cream cone" and comes undone by rattling the mainsheet.[10] His verdict on the flat bottom offshore is blunt: "The bottom slaps hard, real hard in a big sea," which is why his own was built as thick as it was.[11]

He has sailed 23 nautical miles in a day and calls it tiring, judging that anyone contemplating being out of sight of land for long needs sea experience, Paradox experience, stamina and, above all, a chosen weather window (see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2018-test-case-370-nautical-miles-of-open-pacific-to-lord-howe-island) for the fuller passage-fitness exchange this fed into).[12] In years of sailing he never shipped any water through the tiller hole.[13]

Night sailing worries him, having only attempted it twice: the second time the hull was shrouded in phosphorescence, a dim blue light appearing wherever the water broke or splashed. He carries a very big compass with a light built in, the biggest the store had, reasoning that sailing without one at all, as he once did, was at best foolish; the size also means he can still read it without his reading glasses.[14]

Bell gave three separate firsthand knockdown accounts on "Thor": a mild one in February 2016, sailed to the point of a knockdown in about 25 knots of wind and back upright with no crew action needed;[15] a violent, accidental one over a reef in shallow water, hatch open and the outboard idling, sail buried, pushed sideways for five to ten seconds before righting "with a vengeance"; and a deliberate one at a local regatta, rolled to almost right angles under full sail and abruptly upright again, alongside a technical exchange on chine-runner and mast-buoyancy mechanics during a knockdown.[16][17] Full accounts: [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-deliberate-capsize-test-and-mast-buoyancy-versus-ballast-in-a-knockdown-august-2018) and [design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#firsthand-knockdown-accounts).

Off Wardang Island, releasing the tiller entirely once sent the boat spinning in a continuous turn rather than holding any course; a pod of dolphins followed regardless, tracking her for fifteen minutes into the shallows.[18]

## After Thor: a pontoon houseboat down the Murray

After selling "Thor," Bell built a tiny pontoon houseboat and rode it the length of the Murray. Launched from Yarrawonga, she carried him **1,550 km** downstream over **79 days** with the motor at idle for steering only. He judges a Paradox could have made the same trip just as well, the shallows if anything favouring the smaller boat: "I had a paradox, I sold it after years of adventure, then made a pontoon houseboat, a tiny one, travelled the length of the Murray, 1550 km downstream, a paradox could do that, even the shallows would be easier for a paradox."[1] The houseboat project was already under way before the sale, attention shifting to repainting the Stevensons Cruiser and building the new boat while "Thor" was still his.[19]

## Selling Thor to Mark Brothers

Bell followed **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)**'s refurbishment and relaunch of "Thor" under her new name with a warmth that reads as a previous owner's rather than a bystander's: "That's what she was made for, I'm overwhelmed to see the boat back at sea, I'm almost in tears, well done Mark. She won't let you down."[20] Brothers's own account of buying a seven-year-old, secondhand Paradox in South Australia, one of about four then in the country, and towing her partway to meet the seller at the New South Wales border at Mildura, corroborates Bell's in every particular without either man naming the other.[3] Full account of her second life: [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#sold-across-the-country-and-renamed-pamela-b-2022) and [Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md).
