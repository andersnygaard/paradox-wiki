# Nickolas Bell

**Nickolas Bell** is an **Australian** who **built a [Paradox](/paradox.md) and sailed her for years** before selling her on — "I had a paradox, I sold it after years of adventure."[1][2] His boat was built heavily: a **bottom about 25 mm thick** in all, **20 mm of marine plywood** under "coat after coat of resin in double-bias heavy fibreglass cloth," and West System epoxy throughout the inside in so many coats that he used the inside of the hull as the place to dry his rollers and brushes.[3][4] After selling her he built a **tiny pontoon houseboat** and took it **1,550 km down the Murray**, launching from **Yarrawonga** and drifting downstream over **79 days** with the motor at idle for steering only — a trip he says a Paradox could have made just as well, the shallows being easier for one.[2]

## Building: no metal left in the hull

Bell's fastening method is the archive's most thorough answer to the problem of screws in thin plywood. He assembled the hull **dry, screwed together**, and "measured and measured" until he was satisfied it was symmetrical — "It took me forever" — then glued it, and **days later took every screw out and backfilled the holes with wooden dowels**, so there is **no metal in the hull to heat up and crack the ply**. Where a screw is genuinely needed later he **oversize-drills the hole, fills it with resin and powder, and drives the screw into the cured resin** — "the long way around but it never leads to cracks."[4]

On epoxy technique he is against brute force: clamping hard "is not the idea, if you squeeze too hard on SOME joints is counterproductive," and **end grain especially should be coated, left a while, and recoated** so enough resin bonds rather than being squeezed out.[5] For laminated parts made in a batch he adds the detail that matters for symmetry — **clamp each one at exactly the same spot every time** and they will come out matching; otherwise expect to sand them to shape.[6] He also cast his **rudder gudgeon in a cut-out mould**, building it up in glass cloth and micro filler, sanding and shaping it after it released; several years on it was still in good shape.[7]

## Sailing her hard

Bell's sailing testimony is unusually specific about the boat's limits. He has sailed **23 nautical miles in a day** and calls it tiring, judging that anyone contemplating being out of sight of land for long would need **sea experience, Paradox experience, stamina, and above all a chosen weather window**.[8] Running in a seaway she **will broach**: "In a running sea with waves on a windy day mine has broached too. You need to concentrate when surfing down a wave it's very likely." The boat stays dry inside, the ballast keeps her stable, and it is "fun and a challenge" — unless you are tired, in which case his rule is to **reef hard and set a course well off the wind**.[9] The **furling gear** he furls by rhythm rather than force: lift the sail on the halyard, furl a little, lift again, and repeat, which "made the sail furl easily enough, except in strong winds while moving along"; a fouled sail wrapped round the mast "looks like an ice cream cone" and comes undone by rattling the mainsheet (see [furling](/components/rig/furling.md)).[10][9] In years of sailing he **never shipped any water through the tiller hole**.[11]

His verdict on the **flat bottom offshore** is blunt and worth setting against the design's enthusiasts: "The bottom slaps hard, real hard in a big sea" — which is why his own was built as thick as it was.[3]

For [provisioning](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md) he found a **camp stove atop a gas bottle** unstable and cooking ashore best; on longer passages he carried food that needs no cooking, made **a thermos of hot water each morning** to use through the day, and rated his wife's **precooked, vacuum-sealed meals** the best of it, which together made the journey "a one meal a day" affair.[12] His advice to builders is of a piece with all this: "it's about sailing time too, build to a standard that makes it very strong, the finish is acceptable and get sailing!"[6]

## Building and launching "Thor" (2014-2015)

Correspondence signing "Nick, Australia" (`nickbell1955@…`) documented a Paradox built with a boat builder's professional help — including a birdsmouth mast — and launched under the name **"Thor"** on the Murray River, South Australia, in June 2015.[14] The full name **"Nick Bell"** given in the `From:` header, the country, and an active Paradox build together match Bell's own later account of having built and sailed a Paradox before selling her — this mailing-list build is treated as the same person's, though no message explicitly links the two accounts. Full build and launch account, including a temporary post-launch rigging arrangement worked around a healing shoulder injury: [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#citations).

## A jammed halyard and a close call under a different rig

Bell owns more than one boat: alongside "Thor," a second mast belongs to a **Stevensons Cruiser**. Its earlier rigging once jammed the sail locked fast up while motoring in toward a marina — the blocks bound tight by rope pressure after the sail had been laced too tight — forcing the anchor over the side to slow the approach and only narrowly avoiding a collision with a large, expensive cruiser, then climbing the mast "like a monkey on a slippery pole" and hanging from the sail until it came free, bruised, sore, and thoroughly embarrassed. The fix was to stagger the throat and peak halyard blocks so they angle apart, missing each other when the sail is fully hoisted; the rig now looks "awful," but does not bind.[15]

## An unconfirmed link to "Pamela B"

Bell followed **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)**'s refurbishment and relaunch of his secondhand Australian Paradox — later identified as **"Pamela B"** — with a proprietary warmth that reads as a previous owner's rather than a bystander's: "That's what she was made for, I'm overwhelmed to see the boat back at sea, I'm almost in tears, well done Mark. She won't let you down."[13] Brothers bought a **seven-year-old secondhand Paradox in South Australia** in 2022, one of about four then in the country, and Bell had sold his own after years of sailing — but **no source states that the two boats are the same**, and none names Pamela B's builder. It is recorded here as an **unconfirmed link**, not a fact.
