# Nickolas Bell

**Nickolas Bell** is an **Australian** who **built a [Paradox](/paradox.md) and sailed her for years** before selling her on — "I had a paradox, I sold it after years of adventure."[1][2] The boat was "**[Thor](/boats/thor.md)**," and the buyer, in February 2022, was **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)** of Newcastle, New South Wales, who renamed her "**Pamela B**" — a link neither man made on the mailing list or in the Facebook group, and which survives only in the description of Brothers's own YouTube channel (see [below](#sold-to-mark-brothers-and-renamed-pamela-b)).[37] His boat was built heavily: a **bottom about 25 mm thick** in all, **20 mm of marine plywood** under "coat after coat of resin in double-bias heavy fibreglass cloth," and West System epoxy throughout the inside in so many coats that he used the inside of the hull as the place to dry his rollers and brushes.[3][4] After selling her he built a **tiny pontoon houseboat** and took it **1,550 km down the Murray**, launching from **Yarrawonga** and drifting downstream over **79 days** with the motor at idle for steering only — a trip he says a Paradox could have made just as well, the shallows being easier for one.[2]

## Building: no metal left in the hull

Bell's fastening method is the archive's most thorough answer to the problem of screws in thin plywood. He assembled the hull **dry, screwed together**, and "measured and measured" until he was satisfied it was symmetrical — "It took me forever" — then glued it, and **days later took every screw out and backfilled the holes with wooden dowels**, so there is **no metal in the hull to heat up and crack the ply**. Where a screw is genuinely needed later he **oversize-drills the hole, fills it with resin and powder, and drives the screw into the cured resin** — "the long way around but it never leads to cracks."[4]

On epoxy technique he is against brute force: clamping hard "is not the idea, if you squeeze too hard on SOME joints is counterproductive," and **end grain especially should be coated, left a while, and recoated** so enough resin bonds rather than being squeezed out.[5] For laminated parts made in a batch he adds the detail that matters for symmetry — **clamp each one at exactly the same spot every time** and they will come out matching; otherwise expect to sand them to shape.[6] He also cast his **rudder gudgeon in a cut-out mould**, building it up in glass cloth and micro filler, sanding and shaping it after it released; several years on it was still in good shape.[7]

## Sailing her hard

Bell's sailing testimony is unusually specific about the boat's limits. He has sailed **23 nautical miles in a day** and calls it tiring, judging that anyone contemplating being out of sight of land for long would need **sea experience, Paradox experience, stamina, and above all a chosen weather window**.[8] Running in a seaway she **will broach**: "In a running sea with waves on a windy day mine has broached too. You need to concentrate when surfing down a wave it's very likely." The boat stays dry inside, the ballast keeps her stable, and it is "fun and a challenge" — unless you are tired, in which case his rule is to **reef hard and set a course well off the wind**.[9] The **furling gear** he furls by rhythm rather than force: lift the sail on the halyard, furl a little, lift again, and repeat, which "made the sail furl easily enough, except in strong winds while moving along"; a fouled sail wrapped round the mast "looks like an ice cream cone" and comes undone by rattling the mainsheet (see [furling](/components/rig/furling.md)).[10][9] In years of sailing he **never shipped any water through the tiller hole**.[11] A further sail-wrap incident, and the worst uncommanded turn on record — 270 degrees in a rain squall too thick to see the shoreline, steered back on course by watching the compass laid on the cabin floor — are told in full at [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-rain-squall-broach-compass-steering-and-a-giant-stingray-september-2018).[41]

His verdict on the **flat bottom offshore** is blunt and worth setting against the design's enthusiasts: "The bottom slaps hard, real hard in a big sea" — which is why his own was built as thick as it was.[3]

For [provisioning](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md) he found a **camp stove atop a gas bottle** unstable and cooking ashore best; on longer passages he carried food that needs no cooking, made **a thermos of hot water each morning** to use through the day, and rated his wife's **precooked, vacuum-sealed meals** the best of it, which together made the journey "a one meal a day" affair.[12] His advice to builders is of a piece with all this: "it's about sailing time too, build to a standard that makes it very strong, the finish is acceptable and get sailing!"[6]

## A jammed halyard and a close call under a different rig

Bell owns more than one boat: alongside "Thor," a second mast belongs to a **Stevensons Cruiser**. Its earlier rigging once jammed the sail locked fast up while motoring in toward a marina — the blocks bound tight by rope pressure after the sail had been laced too tight — forcing the anchor over the side to slow the approach and only narrowly avoiding a collision with a large, expensive cruiser, then climbing the mast "like a monkey on a slippery pole" and hanging from the sail until it came free, bruised, sore, and thoroughly embarrassed. The fix was to stagger the throat and peak halyard blocks so they angle apart, missing each other when the sail is fully hoisted; the rig now looks "awful," but does not bind.[15]

## A hand-tool cutting technique

Recounting how "Thor" was cut, once finished, Bell described using no table saw at all: a small **push saw** (100 mm diameter blade), worked slowly, did the job, with a clamped **aluminium straight edge** set back a measured distance from the cutting line serving as a rail to press the saw against for long, straight cuts. A jigsaw can do the same work, and did when tried, but "boy is it real slow" by comparison. See [the builder's toolkit](/construction/builders-toolkit.md#a-2016-recurrence-a-push-saw-and-clamped-straightedge-as-a-table-saw-alternative) for this alongside the archive's other tool-list philosophies.[17]

## A lead-fume caution, and Thor's own ballast trim

Recommended eating plenty of garlic while melting lead down for ballast — its sulphur content, itself partly responsible for the smell, bonds with lead in the body and helps remove it — on top of simply keeping well clear of the fumes; full detail at [construction/casting-lead-ballast.md](/construction/casting-lead-ballast.md#a-lead-fume-caution-and-a-garlic-remedy).[18] Separately reported "Thor" still carrying no ballast weight in compartment two at the time, giving her trouble tacking in anything but smooth seas, a trim issue expected to resolve once her battery was fitted there; full exchange at [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016).[19]

## A second boat, and a planned chine-runner retrofit

Bell has owned **four boats** in all, and built his Paradox specifically to go further than an open small sailboat could take him.[57] His other boat still sailing alongside "Thor" — the **Stevensons Pocket Cruiser** carrying the jammed-halyard mast above — is a hard-chined, flat, wide catboat with no weighted keel: his very first build, and by far the easiest of the four, taken on purely to hone building skills and occupy free time. She sails well enough in fair weather to be enjoyable, but proved a lakes-and-rivers craft rather than an offshore one — taken to sea despite her own built-in flotation, she rode uncomfortably in rougher water and let in spray, and he has never camped aboard her — simple to build and simple to sail, if slow to rig, and "no adventure machine" next to a well-built Paradox.[57] Her next overhaul is planned to add chine runners of her own, prompted directly by "Thor"'s own maiden-voyage performance.[16][16] Answering a prospective buyer's questions about "Thor" in October 2021, put the count differently — two other boats made before this one, with a shipwright's help on the harder parts of all of them, three in total rather than the four given above; the discrepancy is left stated rather than resolved.[58]

## A cushion tip, a night-sailing caution, a knockdown account, and a passage-fitness verdict (2018)

Replying to a fellow owner's first extended cruise report, offered a kiddies' bean bag as his own cushion of choice.[23] Night sailing worries him too, having only attempted it twice — the second time the hull was shrouded in phosphorescence, a dim blue light appearing wherever the water broke or splashed. He carries a very big compass, with a light built in and the biggest the store had, reasoning that sailing without one at all, as he once did, was at best foolish; the size also means he can still read it without his reading glasses.[24] Answering a newcomer weighing a 370 nautical mile open-Pacific passage, summarized having been knocked down twice, once deliberately and once by accident in a roll that shoved him along the cabin and let water in through an open hatch, costing some gear — narrated in full six months later as a deliberate regatta capsize test and a Spencer Gulf reef knockdown, with a technical exchange on chine-runner and mast-buoyancy mechanics; see [boats/thor.md — a deliberate capsize test, and mast buoyancy versus ballast in a knockdown](/boats/thor.md#a-deliberate-capsize-test-and-mast-buoyancy-versus-ballast-in-a-knockdown-august-2018) and [design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#firsthand-knockdown-accounts). Put the boat's practical range at about 6 knots flat out and 4 knots average in a moderate breeze. Days later, endorsed a proposed 50 nautical mile test passage and set out his own passage record for comparison — 25 nautical miles out and back over two days, 50 nautical miles his own longest, briefly surfing to 7 knots on a following swell while fighting an accidental gybe the whole way and arriving too exhausted to do more than collapse — concluding a long passage in a boat this size takes real fitness, while a three-day coastal passage in light air is genuinely enjoyable and, in his own words, "perfect for me in the Paradox." Full exchange: [design/blue-water-suitability.md — a 2018 test case](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2018-test-case-370-nautical-miles-of-open-pacific-to-lord-howe-island).[25][26]

A newcomer's question that same July, on what to expect sailing in tropical heat, drew a further working routine from his own experience sailing above 40°C: head and shoulders out of the cabin with the roof open all the way for the breeze, a dip over the side once the life jacket grows uncomfortable, and sleep aboard is manageable too so long as the lights stay off to keep insects away. See [vent box — design rationale](/components/vent-box.md#design-rationale-ventilation-for-the-bahamas-and-cold-climate-adaptations).[27]

Days later, on a different thread, revisited an electric outboard he had once recommended without reservation (see [auxiliary outboard power — a first heavy-weather failure, and a rebuilt mount](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-first-heavy-weather-failure-and-a-rebuilt-mount)): the Torqeedo is fine on calm water, but he no longer takes it offshore or even coastal cruising, citing a brittle battery casing, a fishing line's ability to flood the motor and ruin its shaft seal, and a lock-up pin that damages its own housing in rough conditions; switched permanently to a Honda BF2.3, judging its real-world power well above the Torqeedo's despite matching nameplate figures, and screwed a ballast weight into the hull to offset the heavier petrol motor and keep the boat level.[28]

In August 2018 passed on his own furling-friction fix — furl into the wind, a small amount at a time, re-centering the boom between pulls (full detail: [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2018-postscript-the-fix-confirmed-again-and-two-further-techniques)) — then, sailing "Thor" in a local wooden-boat regatta and surprised to keep up with and pass larger boats, raised a tiring, "always active" tiller running that the plans' rope steering never let him set and forget; extended the tiller into the cabin as an interim fix and asked the group whether his own trim was to blame. Full account and the diagnostic replies it drew: [boats/thor.md — a regatta surprise, and a tiller-fatigue question](/boats/thor.md#a-regatta-surprise-and-a-tiller-fatigue-question-august-2018).[29][30] Running at a sustained 7 knots not long after proved a genuine handful and left him needing days ashore to recover, prompting a chine-runner-shape hypothesis and a plan to reduce sail and move ballast further forward with the anchor chain already aboard. Full detail: [boats/thor.md — a regatta surprise, and a tiller-fatigue question](/boats/thor.md#a-regatta-surprise-and-a-tiller-fatigue-question-august-2018) and [operations/sailing-characteristics.md — a 2018 recurrence](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-2018-recurrence-tiller-fatigue-running-and-two-rudder-position-ideas).[31][32]

Days later, answering a fresh posting of a Paradox stability diagram, gave the fullest firsthand knockdown account on record — a deliberate regatta capsize test and the Spencer Gulf reef knockdown retold in full, plus a technical exchange on chine-runner and mast-buoyancy mechanics during a knockdown. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a deliberate capsize test, and mast buoyancy versus ballast in a knockdown](/boats/thor.md#a-deliberate-capsize-test-and-mast-buoyancy-versus-ballast-in-a-knockdown-august-2018) and [design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#firsthand-knockdown-accounts).[33][34] Days after that, opened a "Stability" thread into personal safety practice: wears a life jacket continuously at sea (its collar doubling as a pillow), ties in to a point inside the boat in rough weather, trails a floating rescue line offshore, carries the legally-required survival beacon plus a personal one on his jacket, and reefs early per an old gaff-rig adage. Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#continuous-life-jacket-wear-a-jackline-tether-and-a-trailing-rescue-line).[35] Two days later, in the same thread, agreed a trailing line not made off on the centerline would itself turn the boat as its drag changed, agreeing quick action matters, and passed on a caution relayed from a search-and-rescue contact in tropical Australian waters — that most who go overboard are men, most later found with alcohol in their system, the presumed pattern being a moment's distraction at the rail while under way and tipsy — and never drinks aboard himself short of a cruise ship. Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#alcohol-as-a-factor-in-falling-overboard).[36]

## Water tank technique, and a fore-ballast trim experiment (2018)

Days after the safety thread above, gave his own water-tank fill/drain routine — timing the fill rate against a bucket with a watch, always emptying fully before a refill, and pumping out with a drill-powered pump — and recounted a nearly three-year experiment substituting bottled water for the built-in tank, since abandoned as a failure: bottles gave nowhere near a tank's volume in the same space and needed lead shot added just to make up the lost ballast weight, so the tank and its pump went back in. At very high heel angles in strong wind a little water has come back out through the filler pipes, a design fault rather than a leak in the fittings themselves. Full technique: [construction/transom-deck-plate.md — a 2018 leak, and further builders' fill/drain routines](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#a-2018-leak-and-further-builders-filldrain-routines) and [construction/water-tank-lining.md — a tank found unexpectedly clean](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#a-tank-found-unexpectedly-clean-after-years-of-disuse-and-a-darkness-theory). Separately, was experimenting with adding ballast forward — anchor chain lifted over the bulkhead in canvas bags and jammed tight to the floor — to see its effect on handling.[39][40]

## A bow U-bolt, a topping lift proven, and a season's alterations confirmed (September-October 2018)

Answering a fellow owner's bow-U-bolt question that September, gave his own placement warning and retold, with a new towing-rope lesson, the same Coast Guard tow already on record above; two days later added a further tip on timing a tow rope to the waves.[42][43] The same week, re-fitted a topping lift removed months earlier for tangling, and refitted the boat for the season — ballast moved forward, rudder blade flattened, an anchor-line locking cleat added — grounding without drama on a shell-grit island along the way.[44] A full day's testing in October confirmed every change: hands-free steering for twenty minutes with the rudder locked, a relocated mainsheet exit, twelve-for-twelve topping-lift furls, and ballast finally run forward along the lower chine, bearing out the Adelaide University ballast advice already on record. Full account: [boats/thor.md — another day of sailing](/boats/thor.md#another-day-of-sailing-alterations-proven-and-ballast-physics-restated-october-2018).[45] Days later, gave the bow-U-bolt question an actual figure — his own sits level with the trailer's own winch — and rigged "Thor" off her trailer entirely with a chain block and a long tow strap hung from the shed roof, cushioned on plastic bags with a friend steadying her. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a bow U-bolt figure given, and Thor lifted off her trailer by chain block](/boats/thor.md#a-bow-u-bolt-figure-given-and-thor-lifted-off-her-trailer-by-chain-block-october-2018).[46][47] Answering a question the same week about whether a Paradox's weight is mainly water ballast, shared a photograph of part of "Thor"'s own lead — lowered by block and chain into the hull on either side of the water tank, 37 kg to a side — and restated having been rolled over 90 degrees by a rogue wave and come back upright quickly, "it works." Full account: [boats/thor.md — a ballast-composition clarification](/boats/thor.md#a-ballast-composition-clarification-and-a-rogue-wave-account-restated-october-2018).[48]

Days later, recalled two mistakes from early in his own sailing — a violent, panicked sail-haul that tore his back muscles, and a rudder left insecurely raised that later swung down and broke a toe — offered in reply to a fellow owner's own account of a rough grounding. Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md — two early mistakes](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#two-early-mistakes-a-hauling-strain-and-an-unsecured-rudder).[49] Reconnoitring that same month for an eventual Southern Ocean passage from South Australia's own west coast, scouted the Great Australian Bight and Bass Strait from land as the biggest obstacles to a circumnavigation of Australia by boat, and separately checked out Venus Bay's entrance from a hired open boat, judging the coast still needing more close-to-home sailing before heading offshore there. Full account: [boats/thor.md — reconnaissance for an eventual Southern Ocean passage](/boats/thor.md#reconnaissance-for-an-eventual-southern-ocean-passage-october-2018).[50][51]

Reading Thor Heyerdahl's own storm-tested small-craft voyages as an ongoing inspiration, weighed a clear-eyed read of South Australia's own hazards — night sailing forced by the distance between recognised ports, and a Southern Ocean approach through reef openings, sandbars, and breaking waves capable of a rollover — against an extreme benchmark of his own choosing, a 47-year-old who kayaked around Australia and a two-day continuous row across the Bight itself, and judged the Bight too far to attempt in a Paradox, though other long stretches of coast might still be sailed adventurously. Full account, including the surf-departure question this drew: [boats/thor.md — reconnaissance for an eventual Southern Ocean passage](/boats/thor.md#reconnaissance-for-an-eventual-southern-ocean-passage-october-2018).[52] Off Wardang Island days later, found that releasing the tiller entirely — rather than locking it centred as in the hands-free running already on record — spun the boat in a continuous hard-over turn; a pod of dolphins followed regardless for fifteen minutes into the island's shallows. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a tiller let go, and dolphins off Wardang Island](/boats/thor.md#a-tiller-let-go-and-dolphins-off-wardang-island-october-2018).[53] Answering a question the same week, confirmed the forward-moved ballast had also cut the bow's slapping in waves under power — "yes it slapped a bit. Now it's better, much better." Full account: [boats/thor.md — another day of sailing](/boats/thor.md#another-day-of-sailing-alterations-proven-and-ballast-physics-restated-october-2018).[54] Days later, attention shifted temporarily to his other boats — repainting the Stevensons Pocket Cruiser and building a pontoon-style houseboat, presumably the same one later taken down the Murray (see above) — with sea trials of the Cruiser's own recent modifications planned before returning to the Paradox.[55]

In February 2019, answering a newcomer finishing a rescued Paradox — "[Prescott Eagle](/boats/prescott-eagle.md)" — judged a sharp bow no different in performance from the plans' snub nose, if more easily damaged, and gave a fuller account of wave-handling technique together with a becalmed tow by sea rescue. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a becalmed tow](/boats/thor.md#a-becalmed-tow-and-further-wave-handling-technique-february-2019) and [operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md — a 2019 exchange](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md#a-2019-exchange-sharp-bow-versus-snub-nose-and-momentum-through-chop).[56]

## Sold to Mark Brothers, and renamed "Pamela B"

Bell followed **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)**'s refurbishment and relaunch of his secondhand Australian Paradox with a proprietary warmth that reads as a previous owner's rather than a bystander's: "That's what she was made for, I'm overwhelmed to see the boat back at sea, I'm almost in tears, well done Mark. She won't let you down."[13]

It reads that way because that is what it was. Brothers's boat is **"[Thor](/boats/thor.md)"** — Bell's own — bought from him in **February 2022** and renamed **"Pamela B."** Brothers states the provenance in the description of his YouTube channel: "Pamela B was built by Nick Bell in South Australia and sailed there as 'Thor' for several years before I was lucky enough to acquire her and fit her out for luxury dinghy sailing."[37] The two men's separate accounts corroborate each other in every particular without either ever naming the other: Brothers bought a **seven-year-old** boat in **South Australia**, one of about four then in the country, from a seller who towed her as far as the New South Wales border at Mildura to meet him;[38] Bell launched "Thor" in South Australia in **June 2015** and sold her "after years of adventure."[2] Full account of the succession: [boats/thor.md — sold across the country, and renamed "Pamela B"](/boats/thor.md#sold-across-the-country-and-renamed-pamela-b-2022).
