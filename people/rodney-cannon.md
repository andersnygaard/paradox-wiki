# Rodney Cannon

**Rodney Cannon**, who signs himself "Rod," is a builder from **Venice, California**, who took up boatbuilding and sailing at the same time in the summer of 2008, after an earlier career as a professional magician, followed by a window-cleaning business and then short-order cooking work.[1][2] He weighed three designs, Black Skimmer, Robbsboat and Paradox, and chose Paradox over the more established Robbsboat almost by accident: another builder's account of [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own story left him "all teary eyed."[3] He began construction that August, working from a one-car apartment near the beach with no workshop of his own, and kept at the boat through moves and setbacks for the next three and a half years.[4] By 2012 the hull was nearly finished when a move to **New Orleans** forced him to leave it behind in California and start again, a second [build](/boats/wizard-spawn.md) he found came easier than the first.[5] His own messages never name the boat; two outside sources do, six years apart and in different words: a 2008 registry lists her as **"Magic-Hat,"** a 2014 census as **"Wizard Spawn,"** each name a fitting label, in its own way, for a builder who once earned a living making things vanish.[6][7]

## A magician's road to Venice Beach

He grew up in **Kansas City** and spent his teenage years in **Seattle**, where he busked sleight-of-hand and took magic classes at the shop by **Pike Place Market**, and where, as a boy leaving home for the first time, he had gone "to be a wizard"; by 2008 he was weighing a return there, having reconnected with old friends.[8][9]

As a beginner, Paradox offered far more information and a more supportive community than Robbsboat did at the time, and, glad of the choice given his own Kansas origins, he put it plainly: "some of us do need to know how to wipe our butts on the ocean," since "there are no corncobs out there."[3] The magician's career itself had ended some eight years earlier, followed by a window-cleaning business that later closed and, by the spring of 2009, several weeks of unexpectedly enjoyable work flipping burgers.[1][2]

He kept comparing designs even after settling on one. He weighed a Swaggie as a bigger, distantly related alternative he might build himself in a few years, and later named Bolger's Micro his favorite-looking boat in the whole category, having also considered and ruled out the larger Black Skimmer; Paradox's build process suited his own needs better, he judged, even while allowing that the design, right for him, might not be the best boat for just anybody.[10][11] A sliding hatch that lets a sailor stand up, he noted in a 2009 headroom debate, made a fair standing comparison to "Little Cruiser"; in the same debate he separately guessed, correctly, that "Hogfish," seen only on a builder's website at the time, was a larger design sharing many of Paradox's own ideas.[12][13] Answering a new South African builder a couple of years later, he recommended John Welsford's Fafnir as an alternative worth a look before deciding on Paradox: heavier, wider and easier to build, but meant for genuine blue-water voyages.[14]

Kansas City recurred as a quiet thread through the years that followed. In October 2008 he closed out a rudder-blade discussion by mentioning an upcoming week's vacation there to see his grandmother, "a great person to be around."[15] Two and a half years later, a fellow builder's own parts-for-sale post led him to recognize the same city as his hometown again, with another visit to his grandmother planned that May and his own build "together," "but you never know."[16]

## Building from a one-car apartment

New to sailing as well as building, Rodney signed up that summer for ASA sailing classes, working through *Chapman Seamanship and Small Boat Handling* and *Learn to Sail in a Weekend* and using a paid copy of the *Virtual Sailor 7.01* simulator to learn the basics.[17] Within days of starting construction he was already thanking the builders who had gone before him: "As I go over the plans and work on this thing almost all my questions have been answered by searching this board or looking at all the great pics and documentation... Thank-you so much for helping me to fulfill a dream."[18]

He worked through every task he could complete before joining the hull together, planning to sew the newly ordered Sailrite sail kit among them, so that the day the boat itself was finished he could go straight sailing.[19] A finished bulkhead occupied every closet of the apartment, the spar hung from hooks screwed into the ceiling, and hand-sanding went on in the bathtub or bathroom at night, with power-sanding and cutting done out back by day; whatever the boat's own eventual fate, he judged the project already worthwhile for everything it had taught him unrelated to sailing or boatbuilding themselves, and said he would not regret the process even if he never got to sail her.[20]

Life did not always cooperate with the schedule. At the end of 2008 an unexpected personal turn meant relocating from Venice to Florida within the following eight months, and he expected to finish the boat there instead; by January the destination had narrowed from the Tampa area to Fort Lauderdale specifically, with a 2010 Bahamas cruise still the hoped-for goal.[21][22] The move had still not gone through by October 2009, and he was still building from the same apartment, encouraging a newcomer weighing whether so involved a design could really be tackled from a single one-car garage with his own account of working every snag out via the plans and what other builders had already recorded.[23]

Beyond the plans themselves, he floated a couple of his own ideas: old gym weight-lifting plates as a possible ballast source, unsure whether older plates were lead or cast iron, and a removable plug-in socket for the masthead light and lightning rod, sleeved and secured by a butterfly screw for quick removal.[24][25] He built his own beam-laminating jigs after finding another builder's own site far easier to follow than the construction manual's own method, fixed a missed bulkhead stiffening curve by extending the bulkhead's cleats over the panel's edge and filling the gap with epoxy rather than re-cutting the panel and built the yard, boom and yuloh with hand tools alone, believing the curve should run end to end rather than only at the ends and planning several yulohs of differing curvature to compare.[26][27][28][29][30] From Sailrite's two 2008 listings he chose the original Paradox sail pattern over the newer, taller-peaked one, favoring its easier furling and lower cost since he did not plan to race and sewed the kit himself.[31][32][33] Investigating why his own plans showed the tack strop's spacer tube tied off with a plain knot while completed boats' photographs showed a forked top, he found that his paper and digital copies of the plans simply disagreed: the fork was a dated Sheet 9 revision, not a builder modification.[34]

His own method for getting unstuck was to search first, then ask: early on he had downloaded a whole community's worth of photographs and build information into folders on his own computer, then searched the wider web for other Paradox builders' sites and downloaded what he found there too, crediting one owner's personal website less for technical build detail than for the inspiration and stories linked from it. Every snag got the same treatment, worked out by searching what he had already gathered before he ever asked a question, then getting help despite, in his own words, "my crazy ramblings."[35] New to sailing, he once could not find any clamp or fitting in the plans that held the mast against a strong gust, and asked for a plain step-by-step account of how it was actually stepped; the answer he got, he found almost suspiciously easy compared to what he had expected: "it just seemed... well... tooo easy. Gotta love this boat!"[36][37] Two months later, satisfied by a fuller explanation of why Paradox is self-righting where ordinary sharpies and dories are not, he summed up his choice of the design for a first build: "I knew I loved this boat, I just did not know why."[38] Nearing the end of his build, he found a fellow owner's trust-the-designer testimonial reassuring given the controversy that surrounds the design, and reflected that he had chosen Paradox "on faith," the word of actual owners rather than others' untested experience, finding it easier than a folding kayak he had built alongside it; the plans felt overwhelming at first, but building one step at a time let the design "begin to talk to you."[39]

## Dreams beyond the build, and starting over

Even before the hull existed, Rodney was imagining what came after it. A recurring feeling that weekend trips to Catalina would eventually lose their appeal led him to picture a trip to Hawaii once the boat was finished, and, only half hoping he was wrong about it, he thought a "big ass Paradox get-together" somewhere in 2010 would be great.[40] Admitting a fellow builder had planted the Hawaii idea in his head, he felt "the ember ignited" toward making the passage in the Paradox itself, and pictured sailing to a Bahamas gathering once built.[41] He floated a time-share scheme for such a gathering too: if his own boat was ready in time, he offered to help arrange accommodation or camping and share boats with builders who could not bring their own, so they could still fly in and pick up tuning insight from experienced owners.[42] That September he formally proposed sailing his finished Paradox from Marina del Rey to Honolulu, inviting the community's own experts to talk him out of it if enough of them judged a properly built Paradox unequal to the crossing; taking their answer in, he resolved simply to build the boat and build his seamanship alongside it, "and we will see."[43][44]

Two years on, still seriously weighing the same Hawaii passage, he no longer offered any completion estimate at all, a change he credited to what building itself had taught him; he professed a growing love for the craft, expecting a real dilemma one day between finishing the boat to sail her and simply continuing to build.[45] The same month he returned to a question that had satisfied him once before: asked whether Paradox wasn't already designed to be self-righting and self-rescuing, he said he would have no problem adding a proposed bolt-on keel anyway, so long as it could be removed; it was the same curiosity about the design's safety that "I knew I loved this boat" had first answered, twenty months earlier.[46]

His cruising plans ran to coastal Mexico at first and, later, fresh-water lakes and streams inland, a preference that kept the built-in water tanks free of routine salt water; between build sessions he flagged a review of the Torqeedo electric outboard as a possible standby for when the yuloh proved hard to use.[47][48] A funding gap that delayed the hull's glassing and finishing, and put a hoped-for May 2010 launch out of reach, led him to a parallel project: a Yost-designed folding kayak, the "Sea Bee 13," built from scrap wood while the Paradox waited on funds.[49] In the same 2011 exchange he wondered whether the pedal effort proposed for driving a watermaker could instead, by a lever, be switched to charging a battery.[50]

When Glen Maxwell put "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" up for sale in November 2010, Rodney called her and Glen's own adventures in her "the driving force" behind his choice to build a Paradox, judging the asking price about half what the boat was worth.[51] Answering a newcomer's Atlantic-crossing hypothetical the next year, he judged Paradox could very likely make an ocean crossing given the right sailor, weather and route, though "totally un-fun"; someone had likened her to "a floating coffin... too stuffy," though he thought a cruise ship would be too stuffy for her too.[52][53] By February 2012 the hull had come close to completion in California; in his own words, he "had to abandon it for a life changing move to New Orleans, La." Starting over there, he found the process easier the second time, weighed a Fafnir for the new build but chose Paradox again, judging her shallow draft still the better fit for his own sailing conditions. The record stops there.[5]
