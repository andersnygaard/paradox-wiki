# Rob Kellock

**Rob Kellock** is a Paradox builder and owner from **Kirwee**, near Christchurch, in New Zealand's Canterbury region, active on the design since **2014**.[1] He moved to Paradox from an open, slot-topped Michalak "Philsboat" for a closed cabin he could sleep and sail offshore in, spent three and a half years building his own hull, "[Mini](/boats/mini.md)" and put her through her first sea trial in **December 2017**.[2] In the years since he has sailed and tested her with unusual method and precision: he changed her rig from a standing lug to a four-sided sprit and set out, point by point, what the change actually bought him; he built a GPS-tracked test course specifically to check the archive's more excitable hull-speed claims against what his own boat would do; and he has taken her on repeated week-long cruises around the South Island, from Abel Tasman to Queen Charlotte Sound. His own description of the boat, unromantic and exact, is the throughline: "not even close to gold plated, definitely work grade, but still a very solid build."[3]

## Choosing Paradox over an open boat

Looking at a built Paradox near Auckland about four years before he started his own, he had passed on the design at the time, disliking how dark the cabin was forward of the ventilation and control box.[43] He came back to it by 2014, moving on from the Philsboat for three reasons stated in his own words: he was the only one who ever sailed her, he wanted to go offshore, and, sailing the colder waters he frequents, he wanted to sleep in comfort at night without the drafts a slot top lets through.[4] Pressed later on why he had picked Paradox over Jim Michalak's smaller "birdwatcher"-style boats, he gave his own finished Philsboat a total of about 200 hours' labour, "much simpler than Paradox," and was candid that wanting to go offshore was reason enough on its own, admitting he had also simply felt like building another boat; in his own words, "at the end of the day you've got out on the water with what you've built and really... that's what matters."[5] The Philsboat, nicknamed "The Lovely Duckling," sold in June 2015, leaving him boatless until his own Paradox could be launched, which he found helped keep him motivated, if it meant resisting the pull of watching that year's Race to Alaska tracker instead and wondering how a Paradox would have gone in the race.[6]

He gave his own name on the mailing list only once, in July 2015, explaining that Yahoo Groups had removed direct member-to-member messaging: Rob Kellock, of Christchurch.[7] In the same message he placed himself in [Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md)'s own history: he had inspected her not long after Peter Bennett bought her from her builder, Hugh Miller, before Bennett had even had a chance to sail her, having originally hoped to buy her from Hugh directly himself, but he "wasn't quick enough." Building his own boat from scratch, "Mini," was the result.[7]

The build itself drew in the rest of the household. His wife's suggestion to soak a broken sheer-clamp replacement in wet towels for a few hours fixed it "like a charm."[8] And when his 21-year-old son saw the half-built hull in three dimensions for the first time, his verdict settled any doubt about the shape she was taking: "This looks like a real boat this time, Dad!"[9]

## What a Paradox costs

Kellock's is an unusually complete costing from a completed boat: **NZD 6,500 all in, including the trailer**, at roughly 2018 prices, with marine plywood, a **home-made polytarp sail** and **no outboard**.[3] It came in well under his own mid-build guess three years earlier of **NZD 8,000 (USD 5,500)**, nearly half of that for the trailer alone, reasoned from an earlier boat's lesson that the trailer makes the real difference to whether a boat actually gets used; he built in nothing but marine plywood and West System epoxy despite a deliberately rough "work boat" paint finish, on the view that a Paradox is a boat meant to last and that the materials under the paint should still be worth something if she is ever sold.[10] His point is about where the money actually goes: "The big ticket items that we all look at aren't the killers. It's the small stuff that really adds up," litre upon litre of epoxy to do the bottom justice, then solar panels and controller, deep-cycle battery, switchboard, LED lighting, a pump, a good stove, anchors, chain, warps, radio and the rig's marine hardware. He rates the **interior fit-out** as the item that most changes how much you enjoy the boat, and notes that **insulation** is the thing most often left out of these lists, improving buoyancy as well as comfort.[3] Comparing to US prices in 2022 he put a build-and-fit-out at roughly **USD 4,000 plus another USD 2,000 for a proper trailer**, and warned that for anyone working full-time it takes **at least two years** from first cut to floating, "for me it took four."[11] See [build effort](/construction/build-effort.md).

## Build order, and building alone

His advice to new builders inverts the obvious sequence: **do not build the hull first**. "Begin with the mast, the spars, the yuloh, the tiller, rudder & rudder cheek. Building these things first gets you in the zone and once you're in the zone everything will come together."[12] He bought the building guide and never used it.[12] He held to the same philosophy from the very start of his own build: no Paradox on record finishes in under about 18 months regardless of build order, a small workspace leaves no room for the appendages once the hull itself is assembled and the temptation to shortcut or skip them altogether only grows once a boat-shaped hull already exists. Better, in his view, to spend the initial burst of enthusiasm on the fiddly appendages first, so that completing the hull assembly itself marks real, visible progress toward finishing, a case he restated to a newcomer weighing whether to redraw the plans before cutting wood three years later: time at a computer gets nobody closer to a finished boat.[13] Of the design's details, the **[vent box](/components/vent-box.md)** takes the prize: "I remember building that and being amazed at how it all worked out."[14] Moving the hull around the shop he settled on the low-tech answer after trying a wheeled platform: a **square of carpet** dragged across the floor, and **25 mm electrical conduit** used as rollers to get her out of the shed onto the trailer.[15]

## Hull speed, and what she does in a seaway

Kellock gives an unusually exact, self-bounded account of the hull-speed question. He has held a **4.5-knot average for long periods** but has never sat at 5 knots continuously; in his experience it takes about **12 knots of wind** to reach hull speed, and by then the sea state means the boat is "surging and pausing as the waves overtake you," since almost every wave in a seaway travels faster than 5 knots.[16] He has **exceeded 6 knots only while surfing down a wave front**, and once, caught in a **gale under full rig running downwind in 25+ knots**, hit 6 knots several times with the boat "verging on being uncontrollable."[17] Years later, a two-day comparative sail against a fibreglass Whiting 16 dinghy on a lake outpointed her to windward in light air on the first day, a rare head-to-head against another design; the second day's results on the other tack were less clear-cut.[18] He went further than testimony alone: in April 2020 he laid out a GPS-tracked, one-mile equilateral-triangle course on Lake Ellesmere specifically to check "magical" hull-speed claims circulating on the mailing list against measurement, calling the looser anecdotes "codswallop" and asking instead to "examine the facts rather than the fantasy," a methodologically deliberate test that only sharpens the figures he had already been giving for years.[19] Speeds recorded on video in June 2023, 5.5-6.1 knots sustained downwind at Lake Ellesmere, sit consistently within that same self-bounded picture.[19]

From that experience he offers an observation about the design, flagged as his own speculation: he thinks Matt Layden drew the **tightly tucked-up stern to stop the boat surfing**. His previous boat "would surf big-time" and he broached her a couple of times; the Paradox by comparison "is very sedate. She holds her line, accelerates briefly and then squats as the wave face passes under her."[16] He also separates the Paradox from the sharpie it is often compared to: **Hogfish Maximus** predates the Paradox in its smaller form, uses a daggerboard, sloop rig, skeg and a V-bottom aft and shares only the narrow heavy single-chine hull with submerged forefoot and chine runners, "so its possible (but don't quote me on it) that Hogfish influenced Paradox rather than the other way around" (see [Hogfish Maximus](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)).[20]

The same weight settles a construction question others keep raising: he would not add stringers to the outside of the hull bottom for beach protection, a lighter, thinner-bottomed boat might need them, but a Paradox is "anything but light... as heavy as hell," and the extra drag would only pile onto what the chine runners already cost. He thinks of his own boat as **"a miniature keeler, not a dinghy"** rather than a dinghy hull needing that kind of protection, and has changed his own habits to match: having beached her many times, he now increasingly prefers to anchor out instead, reasoning that the glassed bottom can take the wear from beaching but a boat left on a beach unattended is an invitation to vandalism.[21]

On rig and gear he keeps things simpler than the plans: he has **not needed the downward-sloping mainsheet tube into the cabin**, drilling instead a small hole through the back window with a **clam cleat** just forward of it.[22] After long runs in open water he concluded he really needs a **tiller pilot** (see [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md)).[23] For [trailering](/operations/trailering.md) he concurs with about **1200 lb** as the towed weight, notes that his trailer's manufacturer regards the **springs** as the real determinant of capacity and finds that towing **with the water ballast in** gives a smoother ride.[24]

## Changing the rig, and proving the change

The standing lug she was launched with never fully satisfied him. In February 2018 he set out a firsthand windward-performance theory: below about 10 knots of wind Mini made essentially no progress to windward, but performance improved steadily as wind rose, well enough at 20 knots, reefed and into chop, to contrast sharply with an earlier boat that made no headway upwind at all in the same conditions. Ten days later he retracted the light-air half of that finding, on a closer read of his own log: the apparent stall had coincided with sailing against an incoming tide, not a genuine characteristic of the hull or rig, a retraction he offered as readily as the original claim. Around the same time he had already designed and sewn a second, four-sided sprit sail, sized to match the standing lug's own centre of effort, meaning to sail and compare the two rather than commit to either on paper.[25]

The sprit rig was built, sailed and adopted outright that April, in place of the lug rather than as a permanent side-by-side option: better on every point of sail, especially light-air windward, tacking reliable in all conditions, gybing no longer an issue, every spar still stowing inside the boat.[26] He did not stop at a verdict. A systematic, four-point comparison against the lug followed that June and July, checked against years sailing the earlier rig: tacking, reefing effort, the disappearance of the standing lug's "Chinese gybe" and pointing, weighed point by point, with the one holdout kept as his own rather than smoothed away, a partially reefed lug still makes some windward progress in very strong wind, past the point a sprit would already be scandalised.[27] The conversion turned up an unplanned bonus that December: an unexpected self-steering effect from the sprit-rig reroute, against his own 2015 verdict on rigged self-steering after reading Sven Yrvind's own account, "a wild goose chase."[28]

## Learning the boat: sea trials, a maiden cruise and a grounding

Her first sea trial, in December 2017, was as candid in its disappointments as its successes: "as dry as a bone inside, the only moisture brought aboard on his own sandals," everything that had been built and fitted working as intended, but the cabin genuinely tight for a sailor his own height and the roller reefing "a disaster."[2] Full trip account: [Mini, launch and sea trials](/boats/mini.md#launch-and-sea-trials-december-2017).

The six-day Nelson maiden cruise that followed in January 2018 gave him his first-ever night sail, awake 27 hours, a wave breaking clean over the boat that proved the hatch modification dry and the sailing footage he later admitted undersold how rough the conditions had actually felt in the moment.[29] Full account: [Mini, the Nelson expedition](/boats/mini.md#the-nelson-expedition-january-2018).

An October 2018 outing on the Christchurch Estuary, "the day things went wrong" in his own words, cost him a grounding on a sandbar after a sail-raising mistake and a failed tack, an overnight dry-out and an 18-plus knot headwind departure that only ballast pumped out could free her from, at the price of losing the ability to point at all once she was light. He ultimately waded her ashore. It is a story currently told nowhere else in the archive, and it belongs beside his other maiden-year mishaps rather than apart from them.[30] Full account: [Mini, a grounding on the Christchurch Estuary](/boats/mini.md#a-grounding-on-the-christchurch-estuary-october-2018).

## A week cruising Abel Tasman, and a keeler reconsidered

Kellock sails year-round, saving the longer trips for summer, and in January 2020 spent a week cruising New Zealand's **Abel Tasman National Park**, from a grounding at the outset that forced a productive wait ("a sensible move," mapping the channel on his phone while stuck), through a Totaranui bar crossing saved by the deep rudder ("I'm preparing for disaster"), to an all-night return passage he flew on the chartplotter alone through the dark. Visiting a friend's 33 ft steel-hulled ketch left him newly struck by "the exceptional stability of a Paradox" by comparison and more and more disenchanted with the idea of a keeler: "she just sits in my garage and I can ignore her." Full account: [Mini, a week cruising Abel Tasman, January 2020](/boats/mini.md#a-week-cruising-abel-tasman-january-2020).[31]

## Further cruises: Tauranga Harbour and Queen Charlotte Sound

Three further South Island cruises, absent from the bundle until this record was assembled, extend his sailing well past the 2020 Abel Tasman trip. A four-day Tauranga Harbour cruise in February 2021, sailed alongside a junk-rigged companion boat, caught him overnight in a rain-swollen river running about 7 knots; footage from the trip left him newly aware his own boat sits lower in the water than he had realised, "a sailing submarine."[32] Two trips to **Queen Charlotte Sound** followed, a five-day liveaboard holiday in early 2022 that used the water ballast as drinking water as a deliberate test, and a five-day, four-night cruise from Picton to Blumine Island in August 2023 he called afterward the most relaxing sailing holiday he had had.[33] Full accounts: [Mini, the Tauranga Harbour cruise](/boats/mini.md#the-tauranga-harbour-cruise-february-2021) and [Mini, the Queen Charlotte Sound cruises](/boats/mini.md#the-queen-charlotte-sound-cruises-2022-and-2023).

## On taking one offshore

In September 2019 Kellock tested the [blue-water](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) question for himself with a deliberate overnight trial, 10 nautical miles off Banks Peninsula, scaled well back from an initial 100-mile plan once the real time and exposure involved sank in. He put the question to the wider list as plainly as anyone, declining nonetheless to answer it outright for a full ocean crossing: "I wish someone (I'm not putting my hand up!) would actually take a Paradox on an ocean voyage." Reading of a solo sailor rolled and dismasted in 7 m swells 60 miles off the North Island's east coast, he set Sven Yrvind's reasoning, that small size means smaller forces, the bobbing-cork scenario, against his own doubt: "What about falling down the face of a breaking wave? I can't imagine that ending well."[34] His own answer, two years on, was the overnight trial itself: a drogue only earns its keep with active sailing, the open sea itself simply boring and never once unsafe from the boat, only from other vessels' traffic.[35] Full account: [Mini, an offshore trial](/boats/mini.md#an-offshore-trial-september-2019).

## The 2024 refit

By 2023 five years of regular use had left her needing paint, and Kellock documented the whole job. To get her **off the trailer** without a crane he used **four 2000 kg truck tie-downs**, two doing the work, two as back-up, winched her clear and eased her down onto a piece of old carpet, mindful of Team New Zealand having just dropped an America's Cup boat onto its cradle.[36] The finding worth keeping is what paint stripper did **not** touch: the underlying **West System epoxy was completely impervious** to it even after sitting more than a day, and the plywood beneath was "just like new" where exposed.[37] Full account, including the redone glass layup and the eight coats of paint: [Mini, the 2024 refit](/boats/mini.md#the-2024-refit).

## Why he would build one again, and the one compromise he still weighs

Asked outright whether he would build a Paradox again, his answer is an unhesitating yes, for reasons as practical as everything else he reports: the build itself taught him a real amount about boat construction; she is safe enough that he is not afraid to sail her offshore; she is versatile enough to work shallow lakes and open water alike, a combination he doubts many other boats can match; her low-windage cabin, easily doused rig and yuloh mean she has never needed an outboard motor, which he judges more trouble than it is worth on a boat this size; she stores inside his garage yet tows anywhere without difficulty; and, as a recognisable design, she draws conversation on the water rather than the dismissive comments his previous, less conventional-looking Michalak "Philsboat" sometimes attracted from other sailors. Above all he rates Matt's boat-camping interior "superb" for a boat this size, a permanently made-up bed would be nicer still, but getting one means jumping to an 18 ft boat, which then will not fit his garage and is harder to trailer, launch and retrieve solo.[38]

The one alternative he has actually weighed against that compromise is Chesapeake Light Craft's "Autumn Leaves" canoe yawl, built around a central box housing the companionway, cooking station and bilgeboard trunk with the hull sides pulled around it by a Spanish windlass, a construction method he finds genuinely interesting, and a boat he judges would behave much like a Paradox on a similar build time, with a permanently made-up double berth and, on her original jib-headed rig rather than the lug yawl some are built with, possibly better windward performance. Weighed against that: at 18.5 ft she is more than twice Paradox's weight under tow, and he doubts the extra weight is worth it just to leave the bed made up.[39]

Working the comparison through in more detail, he sketched the changes he would actually make building an Autumn Leaves the Paradox way: drop the mizzen and turn the aft deck triangle into a watertight compartment; replace the skeg and rudder with a balanced, Paradox-style deep rudder; thicken the sides from 1/4 in to 1/2 in and do without the stringers; remove the anchor well and bowsprit; move her by yuloh; and swap the mainsail for a shorter, stronger-masted spritsail flown with a yankee jib. He liked the look of Autumn Leaves' slatted seats enough to want them for long, sun-sheltered stretches at the helm, but balked at what a comfortable self-steering setup built around them would cost him, facing backward under way, floated only half-seriously against a ceiling-mounted wide-angle mirror and bigger cabin windows. Calling the whole comparison "this arm-chair design exercise," more a matter of transplanting what he had learned sailing a Paradox onto another design than an actual plan, he settled on keeping the boat he already has, and decided that if he ever does go bigger, it should be the whole hog, a 30 ft keeler, rather than something in between.[40][41]

## Disambiguation

Two archive pages once covered this builder under different names before this one absorbed the other: mailing-list material from 2014-2018, under a placeholder built from his handle and city, and Facebook and video material from 2022 onward, under his own name. Three agreeing signals settle it as one person: the same account gives his own name as Rob Kellock in 2015; his boat's name, "Mini," and the earlier "Philsboat" he moved on from both carry across every source; and the YouTube channel that later carries his cruising videos runs under the identical handle string his mailing-list account used, active from December 2017 through August 2023, an overlap rather than a gap.
