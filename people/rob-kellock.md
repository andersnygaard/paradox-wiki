# Rob Kellock

**Rob Kellock** is a **New Zealand** builder-owner who built his own [Paradox](/paradox.md) — **four years** of part-time work while employed full-time — and had been sailing her regularly for five years by 2023.[1][2] He describes her without ceremony as "not even close to gold plated, definitely work grade, but still a very solid build," and his value to the archive is that everything he reports comes from having actually done it: what she cost, what she does in a seaway, and what it takes to keep her.[3]

## What a Paradox costs

Kellock's is the archive's most complete costing from a completed boat: **NZD 6,500 all in, including the trailer**, at roughly 2018 prices, with marine plywood, a **home-made polytarp sail** and **no outboard**.[4] His point is about where the money actually goes: "The big ticket items that we all look at aren't the killers. It's the small stuff that really adds up" — litre upon litre of epoxy to do the bottom justice, then solar panels and controller, deep-cycle battery, switchboard, LED lighting, a pump, a good stove, anchors, chain, warps, radio, and the rig's marine hardware. He rates the **interior fit-out** as the item that most changes how much you enjoy the boat, and notes that **insulation** is the thing most often left out of these lists, improving buoyancy as well as comfort.[4] Comparing to US prices in 2022 he put a build-and-fit-out at roughly **USD 4,000 plus another USD 2,000 for a proper trailer**, and warned that for anyone working full-time it takes **at least two years** from first cut to floating — "for me it took four."[2] See [build effort](/construction/build-effort.md).

## Build order, and building alone

His advice to new builders inverts the obvious sequence: **do not build the hull first**. "Begin with the mast, the spars, the yuloh, the tiller, rudder & rudder cheek. Building these things first gets you in the zone and once you're in the zone everything will come together."[5] He bought the building guide and never used it.[5] Of the design's details, the **[vent box](/components/vent-box.md)** takes the prize — "I remember building that and being amazed at how it all worked out."[6] Moving the hull around the shop he settled on the low-tech answer after trying a wheeled platform: a **square of carpet** dragged across the floor, and **25 mm electrical conduit** used as rollers to get her out of the shed onto the trailer.[7]

## Hull speed, and what she does in a seaway

Kellock is the archive's most exact witness on the [hull-speed](/operations/hull-speed.md) question, and he is careful to bound his own claims. He has held a **4.5-knot average for long periods** but has never sat at 5 knots continuously; in his experience it takes about **12 knots of wind** to reach hull speed, and by then the sea state means the boat is "surging and pausing as the waves overtake you," since almost every wave in a seaway travels faster than 5 knots.[8] He has **exceeded 6 knots only while surfing down a wave front**, and once, caught in a **gale under full rig running downwind in 25+ knots**, hit 6 knots several times with the boat "verging on being uncontrollable."[9]

From that he offers an observation about the design, flagged as his own speculation: he thinks Matt Layden drew the **tightly tucked-up stern to stop the boat surfing**. His previous boat "would surf big-time" and he broached her a couple of times; the Paradox by comparison "is very sedate. She holds her line, accelerates briefly and then squats as the wave face passes under her."[8] He also separates the Paradox from the sharpie it is often compared to: **Hogfish Maximus** predates the Paradox in its smaller form, uses a daggerboard, sloop rig, skeg and a V-bottom aft, and shares only the narrow heavy single-chine hull with submerged forefoot and chine runners — "so its possible (but don't quote me on it) that Hogfish influenced Paradox rather than the other way around" (see [Hogfish Maximus](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)).[10]

On rig and gear he keeps things simpler than the plans: he has **not needed the downward-sloping mainsheet tube into the cabin**, drilling instead a small hole through the back window with a **clam cleat** just forward of it.[11] After long runs in open water he concluded he really needs a **tiller pilot** (see [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md)).[12] For [trailering](/operations/trailering.md) he concurs with about **1200 lb** as the towed weight, notes that his trailer's manufacturer regards the **springs** as the real determinant of capacity, and finds that towing **with the water ballast in** gives a smoother ride.[13]

## The 2024 refit

By 2023 five years of regular use had left her needing paint, and Kellock documented the whole job. To get her **off the trailer** without a crane he used **four 2000 kg truck tie-downs** — two doing the work, two as back-up — winched her clear, and eased her down onto a piece of old carpet, mindful of Team New Zealand having just dropped an America's Cup boat onto its cradle.[14] Stripping the sides he found sanding alone hopeless where the hull was not perfectly fair, and settled on **paint stripper for 90% of the paint** with the orbital sander for the rest. The finding worth keeping is what the stripper did **not** touch: the underlying **West System epoxy was completely impervious** to it even after sitting more than a day, and the plywood beneath was "just like new" where exposed. He then added **another layer of epoxied cloth** — done properly this time, with the boat on her side rather than upright, so the fabric stayed wetted out — and **eight coats of paint**, hoping never to paint her again (see [topside painting](/construction/topside-painting.md)).[3][14]

## On taking one offshore

Kellock has put the archive's open question about [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) as plainly as anyone, and declined to answer it himself: "I wish someone (I'm not putting my hand up!) would actually take a Paradox on an ocean voyage." Reading of a solo sailor rolled and dismasted in 7 m swells 60 miles off the North Island's east coast, he set Sven Yrvind's reasoning — that small size means smaller forces, the bobbing-cork scenario — against his own doubt: "What about falling down the face of a breaking wave? I can't imagine that ending well."[15]
