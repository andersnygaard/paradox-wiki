# Rob Kellock

**Rob Kellock** is a **New Zealand** builder-owner who built his own [Paradox](/paradox.md) — **four years** of part-time work while employed full-time — and had been sailing her regularly for five years by 2023.[1][2] He describes her without ceremony as "not even close to gold plated, definitely work grade, but still a very solid build," and his value to the archive is that everything he reports comes from having actually done it: what she cost, what she does in a seaway, and what it takes to keep her.[3]

**Disambiguation:** a separate, earlier-created page, [Rob (Christchurch, New Zealand)](/people/rob-christchurch.md), documents a mailing-list correspondent (`creditscorenz`, active 2013–2015) who began building a Paradox, "Mini," in Christchurch, and who gave his own full name as Rob Kellock in a 2015 message. On name, location, and build-timeline grounds — a four-year build starting around September 2014 lines up with "five years of regular use" by 2023 — this appears to be the same builder, this page's own Facebook-sourced material picking up the story after completion. The two pages have not yet been consolidated.

## What a Paradox costs

Kellock's is the archive's most complete costing from a completed boat: **NZD 6,500 all in, including the trailer**, at roughly 2018 prices, with marine plywood, a **home-made polytarp sail** and **no outboard**.[4] His point is about where the money actually goes: "The big ticket items that we all look at aren't the killers. It's the small stuff that really adds up" — litre upon litre of epoxy to do the bottom justice, then solar panels and controller, deep-cycle battery, switchboard, LED lighting, a pump, a good stove, anchors, chain, warps, radio, and the rig's marine hardware. He rates the **interior fit-out** as the item that most changes how much you enjoy the boat, and notes that **insulation** is the thing most often left out of these lists, improving buoyancy as well as comfort.[4] Comparing to US prices in 2022 he put a build-and-fit-out at roughly **USD 4,000 plus another USD 2,000 for a proper trailer**, and warned that for anyone working full-time it takes **at least two years** from first cut to floating — "for me it took four."[2] See [build effort](/construction/build-effort.md).

## Build order, and building alone

His advice to new builders inverts the obvious sequence: **do not build the hull first**. "Begin with the mast, the spars, the yuloh, the tiller, rudder & rudder cheek. Building these things first gets you in the zone and once you're in the zone everything will come together."[5] He bought the building guide and never used it.[5] Of the design's details, the **[vent box](/components/vent-box.md)** takes the prize — "I remember building that and being amazed at how it all worked out."[6] Moving the hull around the shop he settled on the low-tech answer after trying a wheeled platform: a **square of carpet** dragged across the floor, and **25 mm electrical conduit** used as rollers to get her out of the shed onto the trailer.[7]

## Hull speed, and what she does in a seaway

Kellock is the archive's most exact witness on the [hull-speed](/operations/hull-speed.md) question, and he is careful to bound his own claims. He has held a **4.5-knot average for long periods** but has never sat at 5 knots continuously; in his experience it takes about **12 knots of wind** to reach hull speed, and by then the sea state means the boat is "surging and pausing as the waves overtake you," since almost every wave in a seaway travels faster than 5 knots.[8] He has **exceeded 6 knots only while surfing down a wave front**, and once, caught in a **gale under full rig running downwind in 25+ knots**, hit 6 knots several times with the boat "verging on being uncontrollable."[9]

From that he offers an observation about the design, flagged as his own speculation: he thinks Matt Layden drew the **tightly tucked-up stern to stop the boat surfing**. His previous boat "would surf big-time" and he broached her a couple of times; the Paradox by comparison "is very sedate. She holds her line, accelerates briefly and then squats as the wave face passes under her."[8] He also separates the Paradox from the sharpie it is often compared to: **Hogfish Maximus** predates the Paradox in its smaller form, uses a daggerboard, sloop rig, skeg and a V-bottom aft, and shares only the narrow heavy single-chine hull with submerged forefoot and chine runners — "so its possible (but don't quote me on it) that Hogfish influenced Paradox rather than the other way around" (see [Hogfish Maximus](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)).[10]

The same weight settles a construction question others keep raising: he would not add stringers to the outside of the hull bottom for beach protection — a lighter, thinner-bottomed boat might need them, but a Paradox is "anything but light... as heavy as hell," and the extra drag would only pile onto what the chine runners already cost. He thinks of his own boat as **"a miniature keeler, not a dinghy"** rather than a dinghy hull needing that kind of protection, and has changed his own habits to match: having beached her many times, he now increasingly prefers to anchor out instead, reasoning that the glassed bottom can take the wear from beaching but a boat left on a beach unattended is an invitation to vandalism. The water ballast itself he judges drinkable at a pinch, the epoxy having been cured for at least a couple of years by the time he wrote this, though he has never actually needed to.[20]

On rig and gear he keeps things simpler than the plans: he has **not needed the downward-sloping mainsheet tube into the cabin**, drilling instead a small hole through the back window with a **clam cleat** just forward of it.[11] After long runs in open water he concluded he really needs a **tiller pilot** (see [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md)).[12] For [trailering](/operations/trailering.md) he concurs with about **1200 lb** as the towed weight, notes that his trailer's manufacturer regards the **springs** as the real determinant of capacity, and finds that towing **with the water ballast in** gives a smoother ride.[13]

## The 2024 refit

By 2023 five years of regular use had left her needing paint, and Kellock documented the whole job. To get her **off the trailer** without a crane he used **four 2000 kg truck tie-downs** — two doing the work, two as back-up — winched her clear, and eased her down onto a piece of old carpet, mindful of Team New Zealand having just dropped an America's Cup boat onto its cradle.[14] Stripping the sides he found sanding alone hopeless where the hull was not perfectly fair, and settled on **paint stripper for 90% of the paint** with the orbital sander for the rest. The finding worth keeping is what the stripper did **not** touch: the underlying **West System epoxy was completely impervious** to it even after sitting more than a day, and the plywood beneath was "just like new" where exposed. He then added **another layer of epoxied cloth** — done properly this time, with the boat on her side rather than upright, so the fabric stayed wetted out — and **eight coats of paint**, hoping never to paint her again (see [topside painting](/construction/topside-painting.md)).[3][14]

## On taking one offshore

In September 2019 Kellock tested the [blue-water](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) question for himself with a deliberate overnight trial. An initial plan to go 100 miles offshore, then 50, was scaled back to a single night 10 nautical miles out once the real time involved — three or four days for the fuller versions — and the exposure to six-metre-plus Southern Ocean swells beyond Banks Peninsula's shelter sank in; even at 10 miles, onlookers predicted capsize, structural failure, and death by exposure or drowning. He sailed down-harbour past Quail Island to a waypoint off Kaiapoi at an average of **2.5 knots over 8 hours** — a figure he generalizes as the real planning speed for any Paradox passage, since the boat's 5-knot maximum is rarely sustained — then rode out the night lying ahull under a homemade drogue: 50 m of line between the two stern cleats, threaded through 5 m of garden hose. The drogue's motion made him seasick within five minutes and left him incapacitated on the cabin floor until midnight, worried about shipping traffic and drift; woken at half past midnight to find himself already 4 nautical miles closer to Southshore beach, he raised a scrap of scandalized sail and changed course, drogue still trailing, which let the boat broad-reach clear at about three-quarters of a knot and settle enough to sleep again. In 36 hours he covered 40 nautical miles including the overnight drift, averaging just over a knot, and resorted to paddling only twice — once for a missed tack, once to clear a windless patch near Godley Head. His conclusions: a drogue only earns its keep together with active sailing, not lying ahull, and a Paradox cannot make way to windward under one at all; the open sea itself he found simply boring, seasickness the only real discomfort, at about half a day; and despite the exposure he never once felt unsafe aboard the boat — only from other vessels' traffic.[16]

A video of the passage was posted to the mailing list the same week (`https://youtu.be/-ZzBKFZ-b10`).[18] Replying to it, Kellock filled in detail the written account had not covered: the scandalized rig went up only once seasickness had already set in — the boat had been "bouncing around all over the place" beforehand, which is also why no footage exists of that roughest stretch — and he was surprised to manage any sleep at all while seasick, needing two days ashore afterward to catch up on what he had lost; on ordinary trips to sheltered anchorages he typically sleeps badly the first night and "like a baby" from then on, and found the enclosed cabin itself comfortable specifically for keeping draughts away, unlike his previous boat, where draughts had been the problem.[19]

Kellock has also put the same question as plainly as anyone, declining nonetheless to answer it outright for a full ocean crossing: "I wish someone (I'm not putting my hand up!) would actually take a Paradox on an ocean voyage." Reading of a solo sailor rolled and dismasted in 7 m swells 60 miles off the North Island's east coast, he set Sven Yrvind's reasoning — that small size means smaller forces, the bobbing-cork scenario — against his own doubt: "What about falling down the face of a breaking wave? I can't imagine that ending well."[15] Sailing engine-free carries its own reward in his experience: it changes how the whole day on the water is spent, trades some range for improved sailing ability, and, in his own words, "you get much better at ghosting!"[17]

## A week cruising Abel Tasman, and a keeler reconsidered

Kellock sails year-round, saving the longer trips for summer, and in January 2020 returned to New Zealand's **Abel Tasman National Park** — cruising ground he had visited before — for a week that ran from a 6.5-hour drive down on 12 January to the drive home on the 18th.[21]

Launching into the **Marahau Estuary** as the tide began to ebb, he grounded on a sandbank while still weighing whether it was safe to go out into a 20-knot southeasterly — in a region with roughly a 4-metre tidal range, "things happen very quickly" — so the sandbank made the decision for him. He used the wait productively, mapping the channel on the free **OpenCPN** charting app on his phone, "a sensible move" that paid off on the return leg. Underway on the next high tide just after midnight, he paddled out of the estuary by masthead light and chartplotter, sailed clear of Sandy Bay as the wind eased to 12 knots, then ghosted the last of it into the Adele Island anchorage at 2:30 am, again navigating by masthead light through the moored fleet to the shoreline — two hours' passage at 2 knots.[21]

The next day supplied the trip's real test. A light southerly died within the hour and left him becalmed and seasick for two hours off Adele Island — "not incapacitated as I have been in the past," and gradually hardening to it — before a 15-knot southeasterly arrived without warning and sent "Mini" charging along at hull speed, the front deck regularly washed as she ploughed into the backs of the waves: "this is exciting stuff." Shifting his own weight from side to side made no difference at all — "such a heavy craft" — and once the day's tourist "fizz boats" fled the building swell he had the coast to himself, irritated to be twice passed by keelers, one of them motor-sailing by at 7 knots in what he judged perfect sailing conditions: "how dare they squander such perfect conditions!" Off Totaranui with surf up on the beach and only 30 minutes of high tide left, he chose to shoot the gap rather than wait, judging the wind behind him strong enough to beat the ebb; parents and children in the estuary fled the sight of "Mini"'s bow wave, and surfing over the bar she spun to starboard with the sail still to port — "I'm preparing for disaster" — until the deep rudder dug in and brought her straight, sailing on sedately to anchor off the shore. That leg took six hours at 2.2 knots.[21]

After a rest day camping ashore with friends but sleeping aboard, the passage back turned into an all-nighter: leaving Totaranui on the evening high in a dying 8-knot southeasterly, he was embayed off Awaroa Bay by early evening, working every puff offshore against a leftover swell too big to paddle into, and spent four hours in the dark "tormented with winds coming from every corner of the compass and dying within minutes" — enough to make him "question my sanity," alone a mile offshore with no engine and no wind. A 15-knot easterly finally filled in off Tonga Island at 11 pm and the passage resumed at speed in total darkness with no moon; mistaking the distant Pitt Head light for a nearer reef light, checked against the chart, told him he was moving faster than he had realised, and the final approach into the rock-strewn **Astrolabe Roadstead** by Adele Island was flown entirely on the chartplotter — glancing at the phone tucked under his seat for a heading, then reconciling it against the dark shoreline once his eyes adjusted: "nothing seems right, but I just have to trust the charts." The wind eased in the island's lee, and only the sight of moored boats' masthead lights told him he had arrived; ashore at 12:30 am, the leg had taken nine and a half hours at 1.4 knots, three and a half hours longer than the same passage run the other way.[21]

Visiting a friend's 33 ft steel junk-rigged ketch moored nearby, a turn in the man's inflatable dinghy — "like standing on an inflatable lilo" — left him newly struck by "the exceptional stability of a Paradox" by comparison, and hearing out everything the ketch still needed done to her left him "more and more disenchanted with the idea of a keeler": "Mini" does the same job for far less expense, and when he is not sailing her "she just sits in my garage and I can ignore her" — not so a keeler, with a maintenance task always waiting. He sailed off scandalised for his friend's benefit, then deliberately upwind scandalised too "just to show off" — only slightly worse for it than fully rigged — passing the ketch as its owner weighed anchor and keeping pace alongside at about 4 knots down the roadstead long enough for the two to film each other under way. The trip back up the Marahau channel was flown by the waypoints he had logged into OpenCPN at low tide on the way out; the ramp itself proved so shallow that the tow vehicle had to be unhitched and the trailer pushed the last 20 metres into the water by hand, the loaded boat then towed the rest of the way back up on a length of rope.[21]

Two further videos followed the written account: the whole passage[22], and a short clip of "Mini" sailing downwind scandalised that left him surprised by how much rocking it showed once filmed — "you don't notice it when you're on the boat. A Paradox certainly isn't going to win any beautiful boat competitions!"[23] Pressed further on the same disconnect between what a video shows and what a sailor actually feels, he called it "just one of the many trade-offs when Paradoxing" — a boat this short pitches and rolls even in quite small chop, a fact he "had no idea about" before he began the build. It happens at anchor as much as under way, which is why he always tries to seek out the most sheltered nook he can find; taking morning tea aboard a friend's 32 ft keeler moored in the same anchorage, he assumed from below that the wind had dropped, only to find on deck that it had not — the calm he felt was the bigger boat's own steadiness, not a change in the weather. See [sailing characteristics — why the seat's position damps the felt motion](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#why-the-seats-position-damps-the-felt-motion) for the same effect independently confirmed on another Paradox.[24]

## Why he would build one again, and the one compromise he still weighs

Asked outright whether he would build a Paradox again, his answer is an unhesitating yes, for reasons as practical as everything else he reports: the build itself taught him a real amount about boat construction; she is safe enough that he is not afraid to sail her offshore; she is versatile enough to work shallow lakes and open water alike, a combination he doubts many other boats can match; her low-windage cabin, easily doused rig and yuloh mean she has never needed an outboard motor, which he judges more trouble than it is worth on a boat this size; she stores inside his garage yet tows anywhere without difficulty; and, as a recognisable design, she draws conversation on the water rather than the dismissive comments his previous, less conventional-looking Michalak "Philsboat" sometimes attracted from other sailors. Above all he rates Matt's boat-camping interior "superb" for a boat this size — a permanently made-up bed would be nicer still, but getting one means jumping to an 18 ft boat, which then will not fit his garage and is harder to trailer, launch and retrieve solo.[25]

The one alternative he has actually weighed against that compromise is Chesapeake Light Craft's "Autumn Leaves" canoe yawl, built around a central box housing the companionway, cooking station and bilgeboard trunk with the hull sides pulled around it by a Spanish windlass — a construction method he finds genuinely interesting, and a boat he judges would behave much like a Paradox on a similar build time, with a permanently made-up double berth and, on her original jib-headed rig rather than the lug yawl some are built with, possibly better windward performance. Weighed against that: at 18.5 ft she is more than twice Paradox's weight under tow, and he doubts the extra weight is worth it just to leave the bed made up.[26]

Working the comparison through in more detail, he sketched the changes he would actually make building an Autumn Leaves the Paradox way: drop the mizzen and turn the aft deck triangle into a watertight compartment; replace the skeg and rudder with a balanced, Paradox-style deep rudder; thicken the sides from 1/4 in to 1/2 in and do without the stringers; remove the anchor well and bowsprit; move her by yuloh; and swap the mainsail for a shorter, stronger-masted spritsail flown with a yankee jib. He liked the look of Autumn Leaves' slatted seats enough to want them for long, sun-sheltered stretches at the helm, but balked at what a comfortable self-steering setup built around them would cost him: facing backward under way, floated only half-seriously against a ceiling-mounted wide-angle mirror and bigger cabin windows. Calling the whole comparison "this arm-chair design exercise," more a matter of transplanting what he had learned sailing a Paradox onto another design than an actual plan, he settled on keeping the boat he already has — and decided that if he ever does go bigger, it should be the whole hog, a 30 ft keeler, rather than something in between.[27][28]
