# Rob (Christchurch, New Zealand)

**Rob** (Yahoo handle `creditscorenz`) is a Paradox builder in **Christchurch, New Zealand**, active on the mailing list from January 2014. He owned and sailed a Michalak "Philsboat" — nicknamed "The Lovely Duckling," sold in June 2015 — before turning to Paradox for her closed cabin, began construction in September 2014, and named her, in his own words from June–July 2015, **"Mini"**.[21][22][23][26] After three and a half years' building, Mini went for her first sea trial in December 2017.[79]

## From an open Philsboat to a closed Paradox cabin

Looking at a built Paradox near Auckland about four years earlier, he had passed on the design at the time, disliking how dark the cabin was forward of the ventilation/control box.[1] He came back to Paradox by 2014, moving on from the Philsboat for three reasons stated in his own words: he was the only one who ever sailed her, he wanted to go offshore, and — sailing the colder waters he frequents — he wanted to sleep in comfort at night without the drafts a slot top lets through.[2] Pressed the following April on why he had picked Paradox over Jim Michalak's smaller "birdwatcher"-style boats, he gave his own finished Philsboat a total of about 200 hours' labour — "much simpler than Paradox" — and was candid that wanting to go offshore was reason enough on its own, admitting he had also simply felt like building another boat; in his own words, "at the end of the day you've got out on the water with what you've built and really... that's what matters."[17]

## Construction begun (September 2014)

Having bought the wood for his mast in late July 2014, he weighed keeping the plans' internal halyard sheave against the external-sheave-plus-wiring-conduit approach other builders have used to run a lightning-ground wire and masthead-light cable, and floated an untried idea of lining the mast cavity with tinfoil for radar visibility, reasoning it might also stop the internal wiring slapping about.[3] By early September his build was under way: the mast, yard, boom, and yuloh roughed out, and a masthead light built from a waterproof LED strip wrapped around a circular plywood form.[4] By late September the spars, yuloh, and stem were complete too — the stem, "a bit of a trick to make," cut successfully with a chain saw — with plywood next on his list to buy.[8] He weighed clear against tinted glazing for his own windows, opting for clear Perspex after tinted glazing had worsened a salt-smearing problem on his previous boat; see [window glazing — a 2014 recurrence](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#a-2014-recurrence-cleaning-reach-salt-spray-technique-and-uv-durability-compared).[9] By early November the boarding step and a hinged reefing rod were under way, worked at alongside the rest in an effort to finish as many components as possible before being forced to assemble the hull.[10] By late November hull construction itself had begun: bulkhead 3, the first bulkhead cut, came out with straight sides only, missing the plans' 5 mm curve; the fix chosen was to glue overlapping cleats onto the bulkhead's aft face, so the filled gap would not show from the helm — see [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists, and fixing one already cut straight](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight) for the design question this raised and its answer.[11][12] By late January 2015 the bulkhead cleats were still under way; to his own knowledge only one other New Zealand Paradox existed at the time, and his would be the second.[13] By early April 2015 the rudder and stock were finished — one and a half times heavier than the solid stock and rudder of his previous Philsboat — leaving only the lower gudgeon/pintle fitting, where the load concentrates, as a lingering concern; reasoned that six layers of woven roving, wrapped fully around the fitting, work the same way steel bands work around a barrel, holding even once the epoxy bond had aged, so long as they went all the way round and the bolts and hull friction did the rest.[15] The bulkheads were complete and the hull sides scarfed and glued by then too, with every appendage but the sail finished, and a dry-run assembly of the whole hull was planned for the coming weekend.[16] Arguing against a fellow prospective builder's plan to leave the mast and sailing gear until last, he set out his own build-order philosophy: no one on record finishes a Paradox in under about 18 months regardless of build order, so impatience for results cannot be solved by sequencing alone; a small workspace leaves no room to build the appendages once the hull itself is assembled; and the temptation to shortcut or skip the appendages altogether only grows once a boat-shaped hull already exists. Better, in his view, to spend the initial burst of enthusiasm on the fiddly appendages first, so that completing the hull assembly itself marks real, visible progress toward finishing.[16] Asked separately for his own build's full order to date, he listed: masthead light, mast, yard, yuloh, boom, lower gudgeon, a stainless-steel tack downhaul, the stem, the rudder cheek and rudder, three of the four bulkheads, the transom, the deck beams, and the mast step — most of them taken only to 90-95% complete, kept that way deliberately so each piece could still be checked against the assembled hull before final fitting; in his own experience the order rarely matters, with one clear exception — the mast step depends on bulkhead 2 being finished first. Struck by how widely completion times vary across builders, he guessed the build time for most of them falls somewhere between **900 and 1,100 hours**.[18] Days later, a dry fit of the whole hull confirmed two hopes: the mast, spars, and yuloh all stow neatly inside the boat, and the cabin comes out just the right length to fit an air bed exactly, so he won't slide around sleeping in rougher conditions — though reaching the forepeak, already a known tight fit, is expected to get harder still once the deck goes on.[19] Planning ahead to painting and wiring, he settled on white throughout, inside and out, for maximum light, though with most of the interior due to be covered in insulation he expects comparatively little interior painting will actually be needed; his wiring plan calls for permanently wired LEDs on the forward faces of bulkheads 1 and 2 and the aft face of bulkhead 4, plus one under the cabin hatch, each separately switched from the switchboard — remaining undecided on a bulkhead-1 lamp, since that compartment may see too little use to justify lighting it, and floating using the forepeak itself as the equivalent of a caravan's blanket box, doubling as bow-slap damping.[20] By June 2015, after a further dry fit, the boat came apart again for bench work: the mast was fitted into the vent box while still able to be positioned horizontally rather than dealt with vertically at the end, "quite tricky getting everything to fit," with the internal wiring secured against slapping by electrical cable ties every 25 cm, drawn through an intentionally oversized 16 mm bottom hole so a draw wire could later pull fresh wiring back up the mast; a planned tinfoil lining for radar reflectivity was abandoned once he learned tin foil is aluminum and gets eaten in salty conditions.[21] His previous boat, the Michalak "Philsboat" — known on the list as "The Lovely Duckling" — sold that same month, leaving him boatless until his own Paradox could be launched, which he found helped keep him motivated, if it meant resisting the pull of watching that year's Race to Alaska tracker instead and wondering how a Paradox would have gone in the race.[22][23] Elaborating a few days later, he set his own question against the design's résumé: Paradox is built to be sailed continuously — the trait that had already won her the Everglades Challenge — and even the fleet's remaining boats, much lighter than a Paradox, were themselves only averaging about 3 knots despite short spurts into the early teens; judged a continuously-sailed Paradox capable of windward work up to about 25 knots, a capability he judged necessary regardless, given how often New Zealand itself sees wind that strong. See [merits — a Paradox and the Race to Alaska, a 2015 mid-race assessment](/merits/race-to-alaska.md#a-2015-mid-race-assessment-how-would-paradox-have-fared).[25] Answering a fellow builder's budget question a fortnight later, put his own build's likely total at around **NZD8,000 (USD5,500)** — nearly half of that for the bespoke trailer alone, having learned on his previous boat that the trailer makes a big difference to whether a boat actually gets used — and set out his reasoning for building in nothing but marine plywood and West System epoxy despite a deliberately rough "work boat" paint finish: a Paradox is a boat meant to last, and if he ever had to sell her he would want the materials underneath the paint to still be worth something.[26]

## His name, and an earlier near-purchase of "Petite Lady"

In July 2015, replying to a new correspondent trying to reach him after Yahoo Groups removed direct member-to-member messaging, he gave his own name on the list for the only time on record — Rob Kellock — pointing out that searching "Rob Kellock creditscore" would find his address easily enough, and noted he lives in Christchurch.[27] In the same message he placed himself in [Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md)'s own history: he had inspected her not long after Peter Bennett bought her from her builder, Hugh Miller — before Bennett had even had a chance to sail her — having originally hoped to buy her from Hugh directly himself, but he "wasn't quick enough"; building his own boat from scratch, "Mini," was the result.[27] A separate, later-created page, [Rob Kellock](/people/rob-kellock.md), documents Facebook-sourced material (2022–2024) from a New Zealand builder-owner of the same name; on name, location, and build-timeline grounds this appears to be the same person, completing "Mini" roughly four years after this construction narrative begins, though the two pages have not yet been consolidated.

## Contributions, and a reflection on long solo passages

Offered his own theory on why Paradox resists tacking, prompted by a report that "Minnow" would not tack without help from the sculling oar — pointing to Phil Bolger's own insistence that a sharpie's forefoot should stay clear of the water to tack, illustrated from his own experience swamping his Michalak "Philsboat"'s forefoot, and separately likening the chine runners' course-holding and speed to a paper dart fitted with winglets; full exchange, cross-linked rather than repeated here, at [chine runners — a third strand: vortex inhibition](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy) and [sharpie lineage — whether sharpie theory explains her handling](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-sharpie-theory-explains-her-handling).[28][29] Returning to the same thread two days later, pointed out that nearly every Paradox in service has been modified from the original in some way, deliberately or not — recalling a modified "[Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md)" seen years earlier in Auckland with the aft part of her chine runners cut off after proving unbalanced (lee helm assumed), a contrast against this same thread's report of excessive weather helm on an unmodified boat — and concluded that, given how much balance seems to vary boat to boat, the soundest plan for his own new sail is to trim it empirically: cut a polytarp sail down bit by bit until "Mini" is balanced, before committing to a proper one.[30]

Returning to the same thread days later with a comparison from his previous boat's forward-sloping mast and balanced lug rig, confirmed he would build Matt's Mk1 sail — rather than a Chinese-style rig — for "Mini," curious how the sail would peak up increasingly as it is reefed, and considering a yard vang to the peak when on the wind.[31]

"Mini" was assembled and lined up straight and true in August 2015, most things fitting together as expected, with a fair amount of epoxy expected to go into filling gaps where the chine logs pass through the bulkheads.[33] See [hull assembly sequence — truing the hull](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method) for the general technique this confirms.

A firsthand comparison followed on the "Trunk Cabin vs Raised Deck Cabin" thread: his previous boat, a Birdwatcher, had windows set low on the side of the boat — essentially a raised-deck cabin's own arrangement — that were hopeless to see through and impossible to clean underway, so he mostly sailed looking out of the open slot instead; reasoned that a Paradox's own windows work because they are short and wide, set away from the sides where spray would reach them, and close enough to the sailor to use easily, with sloped glazing a possible further improvement. See [components/pilothouse.md — trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin).[34]

Commenting on a fellow correspondent's account of a dry, low-tide slipway launch in England, contrasted it with his own future cruising grounds: the slipways he expects to use around Christchurch sit in marinas and deep harbours with only a modest 3-4 ft tidal range, so boats launch regardless of the tide, and a few estuary slipways shared with dinghy sailors would face the same dangerous bar crossings that rule the technique out there too; expects his own sailing, once "Mini" is finished, to be in deep harbours, sounds, fiords, open water, and large alpine lakes, "quite different from what you have in the UK." Reported fitting the sheer clamps that September.[35] Asked in turn whether other New Zealand Paradoxes would be nearby for inspiration once "Mini" was finished, a fellow correspondent's own aside judged starting a build having never seen a real Paradox in person a genuinely bold move — the same position Alastair Law had been in building the first one in Europe.[36] Replying days later, reaffirmed his own decision was the right one: dry-fitting the hull had already told him her shape was going to be "a sweet mover," and his 21-year-old son's own verdict on seeing her in 3D — "This looks like a real boat this time, Dad!" — settled any remaining doubt. With the sheer clamps freshly fitted, this same message opened a chine-log-bending question that drew fixes from three other builders — a slow water-and-sawhorse soak, steaming, and lamination; see [chine log vs. taped-seam construction — a 2015 recurrence](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md#a-2015-recurrence-a-water-bucket-jig-steaming-considered-and-a-broken-sheer-clamp) for the full exchange.[37] One of his own sheer clamps broke loose overnight before it had been glued, at the bulkhead-1 crossing;[38] his wife's suggestion to soak a replacement length in wet towels for a few hours fixed it "like a charm," though whether the same trick would work on the thicker chine logs remained an open question.[39] Thanked a further correspondent's own low-tech gunwale-bending link, noting that wet towels, heat guns, and boiling water were all now easy techniques to hand — "those chine logs don't stand a chance!" — and wished he'd known before breaking his first sheer clamp.[40]

Days later, replying to a different builder's own stem-fitting difficulty on a reduced-scale build, described his preferred method: unscrewing each bulkhead and the transom on alternate sides to keep the frame upright, then reassembling and gluing stem-first back toward the transom — splaying the sides wide open for the stem rather than fighting them into place last — guided by a preliminary dry fit around the boat's widest beam to place the needed guiding screws, and driving drywall screws in front of each clamp to stop it slipping on the stem's curved timber before gluing, unconcerned by the extra holes since the whole hull would be fibreglassed regardless. Full technique at [hull assembly sequence — pulling a hull's sides together](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#pulling-a-hulls-sides-together-with-a-spanish-windlass).[41]

Opening a new "Bending chine logs" thread in October 2015, reported his own chine logs — left sitting outside in the rain for a couple of days, then bent into place with clamps and a heat gun — went in without a fight, though a week's drying in place left them with far more curve than intended, easily fixed by gluing them back in; also asked whether the bilge and tank dividers should be fitted before or after the bottom panel goes on. Full technique exchange: [construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md#an-october-2015-recurrence-a-rain-soak-and-heat-gun-result-and-a-steaming-in-a-bag-alternative) and [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#bilge-and-tank-divider-order-and-a-dowel-registration-technique-for-the-bottom-panel).[42] Confirmed a week later that the dividers did need to go in first, as advised — fitting the bottom first would have made it impossible to fit the dividers afterward — and remarked, unprompted, that even before the bottom went on, "Mini" was already "one hellava overbuilt boat" next to his previous, much lighter boat, which had "sailed over the water rather than through it."[43] The bottom went on in early November 2015: suspended above the hull on G-clamps and tie-downs, wetted out with epoxy minus hardener while upside down — "a messy job" — then bonded to the hull with thickened, hardener-added epoxy; lowering the bow, a tie-down released without warning and the bottom rocketed toward the front of the shed, held only by hanging on to its front edge as tools crashed off shelves, though both boat and builder came through unhurt. Getting the bottom on marked a watershed for the build in his own estimation: everything left — fitting out, fibreglassing, painting, and getting her onto a trailer — is finishing work rather than further construction.[45] A reply congratulating him singled out how much of a struggle bending 19 mm plywood around the bottom rocker must have been, glad the hardest work was behind him with a first sailing report not far off.[46] Days later, opened a new "Stem protection" thread of his own, asking a fellow builder for a close-up photograph of a stainless-steel stem-wear fix already described on the list, since fitting his own boat's stem protection was coming up "very shortly." See [components/stem.md — protecting the stem from wear](/components/stem.md#protecting-the-stem-from-wear).[47] Later that month the build stopped for a day or two while sanding and fairing the glass bottom in summer heat; having researched the question and concluded woven roving was now old technology and nearly impossible to find locally, substituted two layers of 400 gsm (14 oz) bi-axial cloth for the plans' three layers of 24 oz woven roving, coming out at about 3 mm rather than the recommended 4 mm — reasoned an acceptable tradeoff, since bi-axial cloth's straight, uncrimped fibers are held to be stronger for a given thickness than roving's woven ones. Full technique: [construction/fiberglassing-technique.md — a 2015 alternative: biaxial cloth instead of woven roving](/construction/fiberglassing-technique.md#a-2015-alternative-biaxial-cloth-instead-of-woven-roving).[49]

Floated an untried idea for a stronger, 2:1 halyard purchase — a second block fitted on the yard, with an attachment point just below the masthead sheave — independently already built and proven on "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)"; see [furling — a 2:1 halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-21-halyard-purchase-an-untried-idea-independently-built-on-scout).[5] In a 2015 debate over whether an off-center masthead pulley bends the mast, described his own solution: the halyard slot cut as per plan but left open rather than capped, with a home-made LED masthead light mounted on the mast's side using a single extra-long bolt that does double duty as both the halyard block's bearing and the light's own mount — letting the halyard be lifted out entirely for a quiet night at anchor, relying on reefing tension to keep it seated in the slot under way. Full technique discussion: [components/rig/mast.md — whether an off-center masthead pulley bends the mast, and two builders' fixes](/components/rig/mast.md#whether-an-off-center-masthead-pulley-bends-the-mast-and-two-builders-fixes).[32] Asked, after Dave Skaife's 2014 Hawaii cruise, whether the hatch-top solar panels kept up with the autopilot's load, and whether the verdict of Paradox as the "most comfortable boat" held true underway as well as at anchor; see [Dave Skaife's Hawaii cruise](/merits/skaife-hawaii-cruise.md#solar-power-and-why-the-boat-suited-him).[6] Pointed to a cruising blog documenting Geoff Davis's roughly 800 km, five-day voyage from Perth to Shark Bay, and separately to Dave Skaife's own Hawaii cruise blog; see [Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md#citations).[7] Congratulated a fellow builder on launching his own Paradox, "[Thor](/boats/thor.md)," and asked whether she would be sailed on the Murray Delta, recalling having looked at a Bolger AS-29 in the same area about eighteen months earlier and judging it a nice cruising area.[24] In November 2015 shared, without comment of his own, a link to a blog post by [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) addressing chine-runner mechanics on flat-bottomed sailboats specifically.[44] Welcomed a fellow builder's maiden-voyage report on his own newly launched Paradox, "Thor," crediting the chine runners for the speeds such short hulls keep being reported to reach and looking forward to a promised heavy-weather account, guessing it would not be long in coming if the sailing grounds were Australia's south coast. See [boats/thor.md — a maiden voyage](/boats/thor.md#a-maiden-voyage-november-2015).[48] When the promised heavy-weather account arrived days later, praised its honesty against the list's usual fair-weather reports, recalled Phil Bolger himself once saying a small boat like this cannot make progress to windward above about 25 kt, and suggested a sea anchor for next time; set the story against his own future plans to sail New Zealand's coast engineless. See [heavy-weather tactics — why short, steep seas stall windward progress even when reefed](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#why-short-steep-seas-stall-windward-progress-even-when-reefed-2015).[50] Puzzled, two months later, by a fellow builder's report that a newer, concave-luffed sail had cured a tack-to-tack performance imbalance — would have expected a standing lug, the type Paradox carries, to do better with the mast to leeward and a balanced lug the opposite, and a straight and a concave luff to sail identically — and wondered how much concavity a luff could carry before its own performance degraded, floating it as a possible way of curing lee helm. See [sailing characteristics — a one-tack-fluttering puzzle](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-one-tack-fluttering-puzzle-and-a-contrary-experience).[51]

Returning to steering hardware in February 2016, asked a more basic question about the lower gudgeon's rudder-cheek pintle fitting than the woven-roving reinforcement already worked out above: whether a short length of flared pipe should be sleeved into it so the pintle would not chew into bare epoxy as it rotated, and, separately, whether the plans' brass-sleeved holes for lines through the hull — the halyard and the tack lines — are flared only on their top side, flaring both ends in situ looking impossible.[52] Told in reply that a lower gudgeon left completely unlined since 2008 had shown no noticeable wear, and that the furling line deserves more concern than the halyard, since it works constantly against a deck hole under changing angles of pull, best sleeved with a short length of annealed copper pipe belled out at its outer end and epoxied in as low in the deck as possible; also noted that of the Paradoxes in the UK, only one carries water ballast at all, the rest using that space for dry stowage instead.[53] Decided, on that advice, to leave the lower gudgeon unlined so long as everything is correctly aligned, extending the same reasoning to the halyard hole and the mast-vent-box sheeting-off holes, and concluded only the furling-line hole and the transom sheet-entry point genuinely need flared sleeving; explained, in the same message, his own reasoning for choosing built-in water-ballast tanks over the bottled ballast carried on his previous boat — filling, emptying, and stowing bottles under the seats had been "a real hassle" — planning instead to fill by hose and empty with a plumber's siphon once trailered, the tank water doing double duty for washing while bottled water stayed reserved for drinking.[54] Told in reply of a fellow builder's own working solution for emptying ballast tanks — having tried and abandoned lead ballast for one season along the way, submersible bilge pumps fitted to the tanks' own filling pipes, run in reverse to empty a tank in a fountain once trailered, filled in turn from a mobile electric pump hung over the side — and warned that ballast-tank inspection hatches are usually built to keep water out rather than in, so a good one matters more there than elsewhere.[55] Also asked, separately, how a plain 12V car battery would compare with an expensive deep-cycle marine battery for his own modest planned load — a few LEDs, a 4W VHF radio, and an occasional laptop and floodlight, already anticipating, in his own words, that such loads "have a habit of multiplying."[56] A fellow builder's own sealed 80 Ah AGM battery, run with no on-board charging at all, was reported running lights, VHF, GPS, a ballast pump, a phone, and a computer for a full week's sailing, or about two hours with an electric outboard running continuously.[57] Told separately that a car battery's thin plates are built for a brief high-current cranking load rather than a deep-cycle battery's thicker plates and steady draw, that a car battery is damaged once discharged past 50% of its capacity, and that only around half of any battery's rated capacity is realistically usable regardless of type.[58] Returned to the ballast-tank question a few days later with an alternative fill/vent/drain design of his own, offered as a thought experiment rather than something built: a single large-diameter pipe (32 mm), fixed flush to the inside of the tank wall, with an ordinary garden hose threaded down through it both to fill the tank and, fitted to a pump instead, to empty it — omitting the plans' separate breather tubes altogether, on the reasoning that the wider pipe alone should pass enough air; left open how to cap the pipe at deck level without it standing proud, having ruled out plain expanding plugs as liable to work loose, and whether skipping the breather tubes would create a pressure imbalance not yet foreseen.[59] See [lower rudder fitting — bearing material options](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#a-2016-recurrence-whether-the-lower-gudgeon-needs-a-metal-liner) and [furling — a tack-rope chafe point where it crosses the deck](/components/rig/furling.md#a-tack-rope-chafe-point-where-it-crosses-the-deck) for the gudgeon and furling-line exchanges, [water tank plumbing — fill, vent, and suction](/construction/water-tank-plumbing.md#a-2016-alternative-submersible-bilge-pumps-and-a-combined-fill-and-drain-pipe-proposal) for the ballast-tank plumbing, and [radio and electronics — battery type](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md#battery-type-car-versus-deep-cycle-and-real-world-endurance) for the battery exchange.

Refined the fill/vent/drain thought experiment further a day later, proposing to drill the tank wall on a slight downward angle and run the pipe across the tank to exit near the inside wall at the bottom, so that once heeled 90 degrees or more the pipe would clear the water, taking the pressure off an expandable plug, and reasoning a reasonably rigid, larger-diameter pipe should stay put on the tank floor unaided.[60] A week later, having weighed the replies this drew, decided to keep the plans' own breather-vent routing into the mast vent trunk rather than deviate from it, then noticed on a closer read that the plans nowhere actually route the fill/vent pipe out onto the deck at all — the rainwater-filling idea must have come from elsewhere, perhaps Don Elliott's building manual — and, dropping the deck exits altogether, settled on a simpler scheme instead: a garden-hose snap-attachment fitting and manual on/off valve inside the boat, plumbed through a further pair of garden hoses and a 12V self-priming pump, reversed to fill or empty.[61] See [water tank plumbing — a 2016 alternative](/construction/water-tank-plumbing.md#a-2016-alternative-submersible-bilge-pumps-and-a-combined-fill-and-drain-pipe-proposal) for the fuller exchange this drew.

About to smelt 400 lb of lead into ingots that April, opened a new "Ballast placement" thread asking where other builders without a heavy outboard motor had ended up putting their own ballast: the plans call for most or all of the lead immediately aft of bulkhead 3, though scanning the archive turned up some builders who appeared to have placed some forward of it too. Building to plan with no outboard motor and using water ballast, with a 40 lb deep-cycle battery just forward of bulkhead 2, planned to stow a heavy anchor and chain forward of bulkhead 2 to starboard specifically to balance the battery, keeping his everyday anchor in a bucket to port behind bulkhead 4.[62] See [weight, ballast, and displacement — a 2016 recurrence](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) for how this fits the design's established ballast-placement pattern.

Looking into buying a trailer that same week, weighed an extendable tongue or a hinged drawbar arm to get the trailer deeper into the water at launch against two competing bunk layouts for a boat now carrying water ballast: transverse bunks under bulkheads 2 and 4, the approach that had floated his previous, unballasted multichine stitch-and-glue boat, against a transverse bunk under bulkhead 1 paired with two longitudinal bunks centered under bulkhead 3 and the chines. Reasoned U-shaped transverse bunks could self-secure the boat once loaded, where longitudinal bunks would instead need tie-downs, and wondered whether the boat — lighter once her water ballast is drained — would trim bow-up enough on the trailer to need a guide up to the bulkhead-1 bunk at all.[63] Weighing the replies this drew, noted New Zealand's unbraked-trailer weight limit (under 2,000 kg) is far more generous than the UK's (under 750 kg), and explained that dry-launching onto an exposed slipway and waiting for the tide, workable enough in the UK, would not suit his own sailing grounds, where the wait on some lakes could be endless; having priced a custom-built trailer and found it unrealistic, planned to modify a secondhand trailer instead.[64] A neighbour's free, if well-used, secondhand trailer solved the problem shortly after, with fitting a number of transverse rollers to it the next planned step.[65] See [trailering — a 2016 recurrence](/operations/trailering.md#a-2016-recurrence-transverse-versus-longitudinal-bunks) for how this compares with other owners' trailer-bunk arrangements.

The 400 lb of lead ballast went ahead as planned that April: casting it into ingots took two days and yielded 300 lb of usable lead after topping up the rudder's own lead pocket and skimming off dross, with several practical lessons learned along the way about fuel, tools, and safety. Full technique account: [construction/casting-lead-ballast.md](/construction/casting-lead-ballast.md#a-2016-smelting-account-yield-fuel-and-pouring-lessons).[66]

Offered a detailed, self-described speculative theory the same week on why a lug sail needs to be kept flat rather than full, and a hypothesis tying the Mk1 sail's tapered boom, low peak, and multi-purchase sheet together as a deliberate scheme for staying flat when reefed. Full text and citation also at [components/rig/sail.md](/components/rig/sail.md#a-2016-exchange-luff-tension-twist-and-why-the-mk1s-reefed-shape-stays-flat).[67]

On the same "ballast" thread, gave a fellow builder two untried suggestions for a Mk2 sail that would not roll up evenly on the reefing line: more hollow cut into the luff and leech, reasoning the crease likely comes from more draft sitting in the sail's centre than at its edges, worth trying first on a polytarp test sail; and a reinforced tack to survive the chafe a reefed sail takes against the mast on a long port tack.[68] Judged, on the same thread, a full junk-rig conversion not worth making on a boat this size — at only about 100 sq ft a junk sail is too small to justify the added weight and windage of its battens and running rigging, windage the greater enemy of the two by his own experience on an earlier boat, and the standard sheetlet sheeting arrangement will not work at all, since Paradox's centre of lateral resistance sits so far aft the leech would fall behind the transom. The one piece of the idea judged worth keeping was a hybrid: lightweight, flexible battens down the leech of the existing sail, with fixed batten parrels, keeping the stock sail shape and sheeting unchanged — trading roller furling and a clear boom at anchor for freedom from high luff tension and mast chafe, since the battens rather than the sail would then bear against the mast; a similar rig had worked well for him on an earlier, unrelated 15 ft boat, but doubted it suited Paradox, whose boom sits close enough to the water that losing the stock roller-furling boom's own clew-lifting taper, and the ability to control the whole sail from inside the cabin, both looked like real costs. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#answers-to-the-heeling-objection-leeway-without-heel-but-a-sheeting-conflict-remains).[69]

Asked, unanswered on record, whether a Paradox suffers bad yawing at anchor and whether it might be a ballast issue; see [ground tackle and anchoring — yawing at anchor, and taking the mast down before a blow](/operations/ground-tackle.md#yawing-at-anchor-and-taking-the-mast-down-before-a-blow).[10]

Raised, prompted by a 2015 discussion of a Western Australian capsize report, whether the plans give any way of keeping the mast aboard in a capsize once the sail is fully lowered, and floated a paired-fairlead tiedown as a possible fix; see [mast — keeping the mast aboard through a capsize or knockdown](/components/rig/mast.md#keeping-the-mast-aboard-through-a-capsize-or-knockdown).[14]

Reflecting on that same Shark Bay account, doubted his own readiness for very long nonstop passages: across several short trips so far, never more than a few hours at a stretch under sail (aboard the Philsboat, which does not sail herself the way a Paradox would), boredom had already been a real problem — "it's hard for me to comprehend what it must be like sailing non-stop day and night without the ability to get up and walk around."[4]

Nearly through the internal fit-out by mid-2016, found storage tighter than expected once the stove, battery, anchors, and pump plumbing were all in place, ruling out an ocean crossing on stowage grounds alone even while judging the build a success; full inventory and stowage discussion at [components/interior.md — storage capacity after fit-out](/components/interior.md#storage-capacity-after-fit-out-firsthand-inventories-and-small-stowage-ideas).[70]

## Cabin-top progress, and a case against a foredeck hatch (November 2016)

By November 2016 the interior was finished, painted, and fitted out, and work had moved on to the cabin top — progress that, in his own telling, only seemed to slow the closer it came to completion. ## A furling-avoidance philosophy (June 2017)

Answering a fellow owner's furling-friction question, doubted there is much call to furl the rig at all while actually under way — the pipe hoop the sail sits in creates virtually zero windage even left unfurled, so the real need to reef comes at anchor rather than mid-sail. Set out three anchoring scenarios built around dumping the whole rig into the hoop and tidying it up by hand once stationary: downwind of an offshore breeze, easing the halyard and letting the sail swing forward over the bow before snubbing the anchor to the rear cleat; into an onshore breeze, sculling ashore by yuloh to set a line before pulling forward against a dropped-back anchor; or simply near any hazard worth taking the whole rig in for. Judged a previously sailed junk rig only a "half pass" by the same test: quick enough to douse, but its lazy jacks left a reefed sail still draped over the cabin top directly above the sailor's head, with real windage. ## Launch and first sea trial (December 2017)

After three and a half years' building, Mini went for her first test sail in mid-December 2017, on a small lake about an hour's drive from home, with a night planned aboard her as part of the same trip.[79] Impatience moved the actual outing forward a day or two ahead of schedule — rather than wait for the planned Saturday, an afternoon was taken off work and the boat launched straight away.[80] Advice offered ahead of the trip cautioned that a first-time sailor should expect a Paradox to feel lively and tip more than a wider-hulled boat on first boarding, and that keeping notes on an early outing helps set the trim as wanted.[81]

The trip report that followed was candid in both directions. She sat deeper in the water than expected under 135 kg of lead ballast and trimmed a touch stern-heavy — perhaps a little too much lead went into the rudder — and, against a previous, lighter boat, felt notably sedate at the helm: left untouched, the rudder held a course for a long stretch rather than rounding up into the wind almost at once the way the earlier boat had. She proved bone dry inside, the only moisture brought aboard on sandals. Several systems worked exactly as built: the gimballed alcohol cooker, the ballast-tank filling/emptying pump, a push-up ceiling hatch on ropes that opens both fore and aft, the electrics and lighting, the rudder and its lifting crane, the trailer (easy to launch and retrieve), and stowing the yuloh, rig, and mast inside the hull — including the open masthead sheave that lets the halyard be lifted out at anchor to stop it slapping overnight (see [components/rig/mast.md — whether an off-center masthead pulley bends the mast](/components/rig/mast.md#whether-an-off-center-masthead-pulley-bends-the-mast-and-two-builders-fixes)). Fit proved tighter than roomy: at 5'10", the floor area was a little too small, ruling the stock cabin out for anyone 6 ft or taller; there was nowhere comfortable to sit except against the tilted seat back with the hatch closed; and the underfloor hatches were fiddly to reach. Two things needed sorting: yuloh technique — the figure-eight stroke took some finding, and the tiller needs fixing amidships before yulohing — and the roller reefing, which proved a real struggle (see [components/rig/furling.md — a December 2017 recurrence, on a newly launched boat](/components/rig/furling.md#a-december-2017-recurrence-on-a-newly-launched-boat)).[82]

The advice that followed mostly matched ground already well established elsewhere in the tree: checking trim by sitting in the boat and reading the waterline against the plans' own marks (see [design/weight-and-ballast.md — checking trim against the plans' waterline marks](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#checking-trim-against-the-plans-waterline-marks)); the same hatchway-and-floor modification already recorded for extra legroom (see [components/interior.md — easing access](/components/interior.md#easing-access-eliminating-bulkhead-2s-raised-edge-and-a-wider-entrance-on-enuf)); splitting the hatch lid so its forward section alone can open under way (see [components/hatch.md — a split hatch lid](/components/hatch.md#a-split-hatch-lid-opened-forward-only-while-under-way-december-2017)); continued yuloh practice — "you know you've got it sorted when some stranger says 'that looks easy'"; the same rubber workshop floor-matting already on record (see [components/interior.md — cabin-sole non-slip and kneeling comfort](/components/interior.md#cabin-sole-non-slip-and-kneeling-comfort)); and, once again, the standard topping-lift fix for the roller-reefing friction (see [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#a-december-2017-recurrence-on-a-newly-launched-boat)).[83]

With time short before a longer cruise planned over the New Year, the plan was to work on the rig ashore over the following few days and see what could be improved.[84]

Further replies over the following days added technique beyond what the tree already carried on the same roller-reefing struggle: dropping the topping lift altogether for a pulley in place of the stock thimble, a further pulley in the vent trunk, and reefing in small stages; a continuous halyard-and-reefing-line loop as an alternative to two separate ropes; the already-established "half topping lift" set out in fuller rigging detail alongside a larger fairlead; and an account of why a topping lift can specifically foul during a gybe, met by a differing experience that found no such trouble (see [components/rig/furling.md — a wider topping-lift debate, and independent fixes](/components/rig/furling.md#a-wider-topping-lift-debate-and-independent-fixes-december-2017)).[85][87][88][90][91] Taking up the same thread directly, questioned how essential the stock thimble really is — having taken his own off without real ill effect beyond a cosmetic jump near full hoist — and began trialling a short lazy jack above the reefing roller hoop instead, an idea he believed he'd first heard from a fellow builder, with promising early results; noted too an archive tip of winding extra line onto the reefing drum from the outset to lessen the turns needed reefing under way.[86] A further reply passed on seating, underfloor-hatch, and yuloh fixes from elsewhere in the tree (see [components/interior.md — seating position, back comfort, and a mini pilothouse idea](/components/interior.md#seating-position-back-comfort-and-a-mini-pilothouse-idea)), alongside a five-week cruise recalled as proof Paradox "really hits the sweet spot" of size against performance in a non-motorized boat.[89]

A second sea trial ten days later, in stronger wind, addressed nearly all of the first outing's concerns. Yulohing remained a slight issue, prompting a question of whether the pin should sit further to starboard and rake aft rather than stand vertical, since a higher angle of attack seemed to scull more efficiently (see [components/yuloh.md — raking the pivot pin toward the stern](/components/yuloh.md#raking-the-pivot-pin-toward-the-stern-and-a-further-pivot-hole-relocation-confirmed-2017)).[92] Tacking from starboard onto port proved impossible, catching in irons every attempt, where gybing onto port instead lost very little ground — settling, from that trial on, into tacking only onto starboard and gybing onto port (see [operations/sailing-characteristics.md — a further firsthand account](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-further-firsthand-account-aggressive-trim-forward-ballast-and-wave-timing-2017)).[92] Past four knots the boat made no pounding at all, only a rushing sound as the bow wave built, and sitting so deep in the water gave the sensation of going through the water rather than over it.[92] A reply matched the yuloh question with settled practice — a pin raked 20-30 degrees toward the stern, and a second hole closer to the handle for a deliberate "overdrive" gear — and met the tacking trouble with sailing more aggressively before the turn, some temporary forward ballast or shifted body weight, and timing the turn with a wave crest (see [components/yuloh.md](/components/yuloh.md#citations) and [operations/sailing-characteristics.md](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#citations)).[93]

## Mini's first major cruise: a six-day expedition (January 2018)

In early January 2018 Mini made her first extended cruise: six days and five nights away, with a written and video report promised in due course. A 15-minute video walkthrough — posted first as the part likely of most interest to fellow builders — went up straight away, with the sailing footage itself, some of it shot once the seasickness the first days brought had passed, following soon after.[94][95] The inside footage of sailing through rough seas, by his own account, undersold how much rougher the conditions had actually felt at the time: it was shot only minutes after a wave broke clean over the boat.[95] A partially inflated wheelbarrow inner tube fitted with a cover, and a kiddies' bean bag, were both offered as cushion tips while the fuller report was awaited.[96][97]

The written report, covering 28 December 2017 to 2 January 2018, opens with a 450 km drive to Nelson and a free visitor's berth from Nelson Marina, with car parking charged at $5 total for the six days. The first leg — a night crossing of 25 nautical miles that a predicted eight hours stretched to thirteen — was his first-ever night sail: yulohed out of the harbour at 9.30pm as the wind died, then rode out a bout of severe seasickness through the dark hours, unable to read without retching, and, with no VHF training to call the harbourmaster, briefly considered turning back before a rising breeze forced a course north instead. By dawn he was some seven nautical miles further offshore than expected, having been awake 27 hours, before a sea breeze let him run down through the Astrolabe Roadstead to anchor at Tinline Bay, where the falling tide left the boat high and dry to a small crowd of curious day-trippers. At Adele Island the next day a mishandled anchor retrieval and a very small boat among the local keelers drew a recurring reaction throughout the trip: sailors who recognised the design's cleverness, and others who simply doubted the owner's sanity — one visiting captain returned with a cold beer. Yulohing away from Adele Island on 31 December, now proficient with the oar, a 20-knot nor'wester brought the passage's roughest sailing: a wave breaking clean over the boat confirmed the hatch modification kept the interior bone dry despite a small leak at the top of the front window, and the seasickness did not return. The same day surfaced a marked tacking asymmetry — good pointing to windward on port tack, unable to point at all on starboard, the sail bowing and deforming badly — matching a pattern seen before on other lug rigs, where light air favours one tack and heavy air the other. A difficult gybe from starboard to port produced a bad tangle particular to a standing lug: the gybing yard's own upward pressure let wind get under the reversed sail, throwing it into a vertical mess up the mast, prompting the reflection that always keeping directional control matters more than the ability to reef, and floating a possible conversion to a bald-headed sprit rig with a loose-footed boom on that reasoning. New Year's Eve was spent anchored in an estuary, undisturbed until midnight fireworks and shouting from ashore. On 1 January a 15-knot northerly, met on a broad reach with an already-rigged gaff vang, added about half a knot of boat speed and cut the tendency to roll — thought, though untested, that it might also help prevent the same boom-up/yard-down snarl (see [components/rig/furling.md — an improvised temporary boom vang](/components/rig/furling.md#an-improvised-temporary-boom-vang-and-why-the-stock-boom-has-none-august-2015) for the established fix to the same downwind-rolling problem) — before the wind died to a two-knot ghost across Tasman Bay for hour after hour, alone with every other boat out of sight. An evening breeze carried the boat back into Nelson Harbour at 4-5 knots over the ground with a fair tide, tacking up the harbour and ghosting straight into the visitors' berth under sail to a gathering of photographing onlookers; cooking dinner that evening under the closed hatch in the rain confirmed the cabin's ventilation shortfall, prompting a plan to fit a 12V fan in the vent box on the model of Dave Skaife's own boat. The boat came out and was trailered home on 2 January.[98]

The report drew further replies: a firsthand account of two night sails elsewhere, one lit by hull-breaking phosphorescence, and a recommendation for a large, illuminated compass usable without reading glasses;[99] a first-time correspondent's scattershot list of outside suggestions — a cheap Android phone loaded with an offline topographic-map app as a redundant chartplotter (see [operations/gps-navigation.md — a smartphone-based alternative](/operations/gps-navigation.md#a-smartphone-based-alternative)), a Sunfish-style lateen rig floated as an alternative to the standing lug's tangling (see [components/rig/sail.md — an unbuilt proposal](/components/rig/sail.md#an-unbuilt-proposal-adapting-a-sunfish-lateen-rig)), and a sectioned, sleeve-spliced aluminum spar for compact stowage (see [components/rig/mast.md — a spliced, two-piece aluminum spar](/components/rig/mast.md#a-spliced-two-piece-aluminum-spar-for-stowage-proposed-2018));[100] a standing rule against ever letting the yard swing forward of the mast running downwind, to discourage both the rolling and a standing lug's "Chinese gybe," plus reassurance that sailing outside a harbour for a while before heading in eases first-time seasickness, and that curious onlookers and requests for a photograph are a fixture of cruising a Paradox anywhere she is beached (see [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#an-improvised-temporary-boom-vang-and-why-the-stock-boom-has-none-august-2015) for the fuller downwind-rolling fix this rule complements);[101] confirmation that the same wheelbarrow-inner-tube cushion was already in personal use;[102] and, from a correspondent expecting his own first sail that same summer, particular interest in the tack-to-tack and gybing behaviour described, and delight at the term "ghosting."[103]

## Advice to a newcomer, and the components-first order restated (January 2018)

Replying to a new correspondent weighing whether to spend time redrawing the plans before cutting any wood, restated his own build-order philosophy from three years earlier: time at a computer gets a builder no closer to a finished boat, since the plans only really make sense once cutting and gluing actually begins, and the archive itself already holds an answer to nearly every Paradox construction question likely to come up. ## A windward-performance theory: heel as the driving force (February 2018)

Opened a new thread on his own firsthand windward experience with the Mk1 sail he had made: below about 10 knots of wind, Mini makes essentially no progress to windward, slipping to leeward so much that her distance made good against the wind is zero, but performance improves steadily as wind speed rises — in the strongest wind sailed so far, 20 knots, she went to windward very well even reefed down slightly and into chop, a marked contrast with a previous boat that made no headway upwind at all in the same conditions. His own theory: the hull shape itself, not the sail or rudder, is the main thing pulling the boat to windward, and it only begins acting as a foil once heeled significantly and moving at 2.5 knots or better; doubted the rudder does most of the leeway-resisting work, since the boat only resists leeway at the same significant heel angles where a rudder would be expected to work least well. Wondered whether the Mk2 sail's own reported pointing improvement over the Mk1 comes from a genuinely better sail shape (a higher peak reducing turbulence over the sail's head) or simply from its higher centre of effort heeling the boat more at a given wind speed — and, preferring to sail early in the day ahead of the afternoon sea breeze, was contemplating building a high-peaked, 113+ sq ft, four-sided sprit sail instead of a Mk2, deliberately raising the centre of effort further to generate heel, and with it windward drive, in light air. See [components/rig/mk2-sail.md — windward performance and reefing point](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#windward-performance-and-reefing-point-firsthand-comparisons) for the wider, still-unresolved Mk1-versus-Mk2 windward-performance question this adds a new hypothesis to.[105]

Ten days later, reviewing his own log, retracted the light-air data point specifically: the apparent below-10-knot stall had coincided with sailing up-harbour against an incoming tide, not a genuine hull or rig characteristic. Modified the Mk1 sail meanwhile to remove a persistent leech hook, and separately designed a second, 102 sq ft four-sided sprit sail — sized to match the Mk1's own centre of effort rather than raise it, unlike the higher-peaked 113+ sq ft version floated the same day above — for his wife to sew, planning to sail both and report which he preferred.[107]

## A four-sided sprit rig replaces the standing lug (April 2018)

The four-sided sprit sail floated the previous February was built, sailed, and adopted outright in place of the standing lug rather than kept only as a side-by-side comparison. Performance improved on every point of sail, most markedly to windward in light air; tacking became reliable in all conditions and gybing stopped being an issue. The rig still stows entirely inside the boat — mast, sprit, and boom each cut to 4 m — and its lines can be left permanently rigged in the vent box, with every line load, including the sheet, lighter than the standing lug's; the stowed spars also project less far behind the boat than before. The sail's centre of effort came out balanced, much as the standing lug's had been. The one real trade-off is reefing: rather than a graduated series of reef points, the rig has only three states — full sail, scandalised, and fully struck — workable for sheltered-water sailing but flagged as a caution for anyone taking the rig into open water; the other cost is four control lines to manage rather than two, leaving noticeably more rope inside the cabin than before. Measurements and a how-to video were promised to follow.[109] The promised video followed four days later.[110]

Sharing a video from a favourite Lake Ellesmere sailing spot that same month, raised a rudder-cheek sheet-fairlead idea prompted by the new sprit rig — a notch cut into the rudder cheek to keep the sheet from riding up over the upper gudgeon — then, after weighing a fellow owner's alternative deck-and-baffle reroute, chose to cut the notch rather than add further blocks to a rig already carrying more purchase points than the old standing lug. Full exchange at [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md — a 2018 recurrence](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-2018-recurrence-a-rudder-cheek-notch-a-deck-and-baffle-reroute-and-a-boat-hook).[111][112]

## A systematic lug-versus-sprit comparison, and a cold-weather overnight trial (June-July 2018)

A first-hand comparison, checked against years sailing the standing lug beforehand, weighed the sprit conversion against the four problems most often reported on the mailing list about that rig. Tacking from starboard to port, once difficult, was no longer a problem at all. Roller reefing, which had deformed the standing lug's own block cheeks under the force needed to operate it, now took far less effort — the sprit's snotters still need some force, but nothing like what the lug's roller reefing demanded. A standing lug's characteristic "Chinese gybe" cannot happen with a sprit boom, removing gybing as a concern altogether. And pointing improved: a low-peaked standing lug depends on the sheet alone to hold leech tension and is prone to leech hooking, where the sprit's boom, yard, and luff — all borne against the mast — give a more aerodynamic shape and noticeably better performance, particularly in light air. One respect still favoured the lug: in very strong wind, a partially reefed standing lug keeps making progress to windward on port tack, the yard pressing against the mast (it will not go to windward at all on starboard, where the luff bows away from the mast badly) — by the same point a sprit rig will already have been scandalised, and can do no better than a beam reach, itself reachable only after building boat speed downwind, since scandalising moves the centre of effort far forward. With only three strong-wind outings under the sprit rig so far against one under the lug, and the scandalising technique gotten right only once, the comparison was offered as provisional, further learning still ahead.[113]

A fortnight later, an overnight sail deliberately tested how cold a closed Paradox cabin could get and still stay comfortable: waking to frost on the ground, feeling a little cold himself (a hot water bottle forgotten), though judged the real problem was his back rather than the temperature. Condensation covered the windows by morning, but the air stayed dry inside, and a light draught could be felt passing over the face while sleeping — just enough air entering the tiller hole and exiting through the vent box. The one design shortcoming found: the cooker sits beneath the main sheet, no trouble while cooking, but the sheet, wet from sailing and hauled in through the transom's sheet-entry hole, sheds most but not all of its moisture — some still drips onto the cooker's edge, tolerable with fresh water but less so with salt. The transom baffle's edge takes heavy abrasion loading and unloading the mast, spars, and yuloh; a single layer of fibreglass tape laid there in anticipation proved nowhere near enough and needs serious reinforcement, perhaps a layer of tin. Recommended sailing without a motor as a way to become a better sailor.[114] [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) answered the transom-baffle point with a fix already proven on his own boat: slit a length of plastic tube and glue it on over the edge.[115]

## Questions on a fellow owner's river cruise, and his own toileting practice (July 2018)
