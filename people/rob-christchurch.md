# Rob (Christchurch, New Zealand)

**Rob** (Yahoo handle `creditscorenz`) is a prospective Paradox builder in **Christchurch, New Zealand**, active on the mailing list from January 2014. He owned and sailed a Michalak "Philsboat" — an open, slot-topped design — before turning to Paradox for her closed cabin, and by September 2014 had begun construction. No boat name is yet on record.

## From an open Philsboat to a closed Paradox cabin

Looking at a built Paradox near Auckland about four years earlier, he had passed on the design at the time, disliking how dark the cabin was forward of the ventilation/control box.[1] He came back to Paradox by 2014, moving on from the Philsboat for three reasons stated in his own words: he was the only one who ever sailed her, he wanted to go offshore, and — sailing the colder waters he frequents — he wanted to sleep in comfort at night without the drafts a slot top lets through.[2]

## Construction begun (September 2014)

Having bought the wood for his mast in late July 2014, he weighed keeping the plans' internal halyard sheave against the external-sheave-plus-wiring-conduit approach other builders have used to run a lightning-ground wire and masthead-light cable, and floated an untried idea of lining the mast cavity with tinfoil for radar visibility, reasoning it might also stop the internal wiring slapping about.[3] By early September his build was under way: the mast, yard, boom, and yuloh roughed out, and a masthead light built from a waterproof LED strip wrapped around a circular plywood form.[4] By late September the spars, yuloh, and stem were complete too — the stem, "a bit of a trick to make," cut successfully with a chain saw — with plywood next on his list to buy.[8] He weighed clear against tinted glazing for his own windows, opting for clear Perspex after tinted glazing had worsened a salt-smearing problem on his previous boat; see [window glazing — a 2014 recurrence](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#a-2014-recurrence-cleaning-reach-salt-spray-technique-and-uv-durability-compared).[9] By early November the boarding step and a hinged reefing rod were under way, worked at alongside the rest in an effort to finish as many components as possible before being forced to assemble the hull.[10] By late November hull construction itself had begun: bulkhead 3, the first bulkhead cut, came out with straight sides only, missing the plans' 5 mm curve; the fix chosen was to glue overlapping cleats onto the bulkhead's aft face, so the filled gap would not show from the helm — see [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists, and fixing one already cut straight](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight) for the design question this raised and its answer.[11][12] By late January 2015 the bulkhead cleats were still under way; to his own knowledge only one other New Zealand Paradox existed at the time, and his would be the second.[13]

## Contributions, and a reflection on long solo passages

Floated an untried idea for a stronger, 2:1 halyard purchase — a second block fitted on the yard, with an attachment point just below the masthead sheave — independently already built and proven on "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)"; see [furling — a 2:1 halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-21-halyard-purchase-an-untried-idea-independently-built-on-scout).[5] Asked, after Dave Skaife's 2014 Hawaii cruise, whether the hatch-top solar panels kept up with the autopilot's load, and whether the verdict of Paradox as the "most comfortable boat" held true underway as well as at anchor; see [Dave Skaife's Hawaii cruise](/merits/skaife-hawaii-cruise.md#solar-power-and-why-the-boat-suited-him).[6] Pointed to a cruising blog documenting Geoff Davis's roughly 800 km, five-day voyage from Perth to Shark Bay, and separately to Dave Skaife's own Hawaii cruise blog; see [Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md#citations).[7]

Asked, unanswered on record, whether a Paradox suffers bad yawing at anchor and whether it might be a ballast issue; see [ground tackle and anchoring — yawing at anchor, and taking the mast down before a blow](/operations/ground-tackle.md#yawing-at-anchor-and-taking-the-mast-down-before-a-blow).[10]

Reflecting on that same Shark Bay account, doubted his own readiness for very long nonstop passages: across several short trips so far, never more than a few hours at a stretch under sail (aboard the Philsboat, which does not sail herself the way a Paradox would), boredom had already been a real problem — "it's hard for me to comprehend what it must be like sailing non-stop day and night without the ability to get up and walk around."[4]
