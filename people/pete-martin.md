# Pete Martin

**Pete Martin** is a builder in **Cheltenham, England**, originally from Carrickfergus, Northern Ireland. He ordered the plans for a stock [Paradox](/paradox.md) in October 2006[1], started construction on **1 December 2006**[2] and launched her as **[JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md)** on the evening of **11 June 2011**[3], a build of about four and a half years. He has sailed her since, including a 22-night, 140-mile river cruise made mostly under yuloh.[36][37]

## Before Paradox

Long before he built anything, Pete Martin sailed dinghies from the age of nine, cruising a 13 ft "Voyager" microcruiser round the north coast of Northern Ireland and, a few years later in a Caprice, through the Inner Hebrides. He crewed offshore on larger boats throughout the same years, took up a Laser dinghy in the late 1980s and then became a committed windsurfer. When he found the Paradox community in October 2006, he had never built a boat before, and said so plainly.[1]

He is a licensed radio amateur, callsign **G4ISJ**, with some thirty years of low-power QRP operating behind him on a station that is almost entirely homebrew, bar a single Junker straight key and a Bencher paddle.[4] He cycles around Cheltenham, and his cruising ambitions for JoHanna run past her early sailing to a hoped-for crossing of the Irish Sea to show her off at Carrickfergus and nearby Strangford Lough, and on to the Clyde and the Inner Hebrides.[5]

## Building JoHanna

He priced UK marine plywood before he had even decided for certain to build: Robins of Bristol's "Elite" grade at about £548 against £252 for Far Eastern ply from Clarkswood, a £300 saving that settled the choice, and he sent for the plans the same week.[1][6][7] Choosing a workspace took a little longer, weighed as a car port that could be fully enclosed against a garage still holding twenty years of accumulated belongings, with a passing worry about epoxy-sanding dust troubling the neighbours; the car port won.[8] Working through Don Elliott's build manual before ever cutting wood, he also wondered whether a table saw was really essential against a bandsaw, and what a "Skilsaw" even was.[9] Construction began on 1 December 2006.[2] He built almost entirely alone, about 95% of the work done inside a one-car garage barely large enough to open a parked car's doors, working from a wheeled trolley for access all round the hull.[10]

A few days before Alastair Law measured "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)'s" righting moment at Beale Park in June 2009, he had been shown round her in person, and called it "an interesting experiment" that gave him fresh incentive to get moving on his own build.[11] His first scarf joint, as a first-time builder, felt like a moment of "impending doom" beforehand that proved far less intimidating in practice.[38] One of the few departures he made from the stock plans was a removable panel at the aft end of the cabin roof, modelled on Little Cruiser's own removable back window; see [JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md#a-removable-aft-cabin-top-panel-tried-may-2010) for how it was built and sealed.[13] Complimented on his build photographs, he waved off the praise: "don't make me blush," it was his first build, he was no craftsman, "there are few bodges here and there which will remain out of sight. Epoxy is a wonderful filler......"[12]

Rigging the sail for the first time exposed a knack to furling it that took some working out, and he entered the Amateur Boat Building Association's competition at Beale Park not to compete for the award but as a fixed target to build toward.[14] JoHanna was launched on the evening of 11 June 2011, slipping "gracefully off her trailer" for a near-calm first outing.[2][3] Looking back over the long build, he credited "[Zoe](/boats/zoe.md)'s" sea trials, Little Jim's construction-photo website and both Little Jim's and Faith's cruise reports with keeping his own motivation alive.[15]

## A voice among fellow builders

Pete Martin's contributions run from 2006 to 2023, one of the longest active spans on record, and a handful of them changed how other builders actually read the plans or built the boat. He worked out the paper-masking method for reading Sheet 12's mast-layout drawing, a fix for a recurring point of confusion, and wrote it up in full.[20] He independently spotted a May 1994 revision to the tack strop's spacer tube and a related May 1996 revision to the reefing drum's Y-fitting, in each case confirming a plans change that other builders had missed.[18][19] A cardboard-template method of his own for shaping the stem made a job other builders found error-prone "genuinely enjoyable" instead.[33]

He brought the first word of Matt Layden's newly designed **Enigma 460** across from EnigmaBuilders, a separate builder community, while the boat was still under prototype construction; see [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-enigma-family-the-360-and-the-plywood-cabin-460) for the full report.[16] He later flagged a new Taiwan-based builder's rapid, professional-looking progress.[17] He filmed his own hull-rollover technique and, years later, built a simple frame that let a hull be turned over single-handed.[21][22][23] He kept a personal video-reference page collecting links to Paradox and to Matt Layden's other designs, one of the archive's few curated collections of its kind.[24] In the summer of 2012 he sailed JoHanna to Seafair Haven on the Milford Haven Waterway, where he filmed "Little Jim" tacking down the estuary against a force 5, footage later used to show how little a Paradox's pitching is actually felt from the helm.[25]

His opinions, when he gave them, were forthright. On epoxy brands: "I personally wouldn't touch West Epoxy with a barge pole! Why use an outdated and expensive epoxy when there are excellent non blushing epoxies available?" He used MAS throughout his own build, for a total epoxy bill of about £400.[26] On the trunk cabin against a raised-deck cabin, weighed against the doghouse on Matt Layden's own stretched Enigma 460: "There's a good reason Matt still has Paradox!"[27] He twice steered discussions that had drifted off-topic toward better homes for them, once pointing a sliding-canopy question toward the Proteus design's own acrylic bubble hatch and a dedicated small-craft community elsewhere, and once judging that a detailed, unrelated build log for a different design was crowding out space meant for Paradox itself.[28][29]

He served the community's own institutional memory too: he traced the UK owners' registry back through Bill Serjeant's since-closed site to its web-archive snapshot, and separately corrected a stale ordering route for Don Elliott's building manual once sales had passed to Don's widow, Wanda.[31][30] When a fellow owner worked out how to get a Paradox off her trailer and onto a Florida beach for a possible Everglades Challenge entry, he pointed toward the one person who had already solved it: Matt Layden himself, who had faced the same problem winning the race in the original Paradox in 2003.[32] In July 2013 JoHanna joined "Little Jim" and "Faith" at a Dinghy Cruising Association rally at Poole Harbour, probably the first time three Paradoxes had ever gathered together in one place; see [merits/gatherings.md](/merits/gatherings.md#three-uk-paradoxes-together-at-poole-harbour-july-2013).[35] Late in the same long span, in 2022, he confirmed for the record that a table saw had never been necessary for his own build, done instead by hand and with a circular saw.[34]

## A 140-mile river cruise under yuloh

The best story of Pete Martin's sailing years is a July 2018 river cruise: 22 nights aboard, 140 miles covered, 90 of them made under yuloh power alone.[36] He called the trip "a bit experimental." A mast crutch meant to double as a boom-tent frame proved too low and got in the way of yulohing, ending up stowed on deck instead, though it made a handy washing line; the boom tent itself failed too, an umbrella pressed into service for the one rainy day. He tried a composting toilet, a bucket with sawdust and a urine-separating bottle, as a deliberate long-term-cruising experiment, and used a solar shower three times.

The cruise itself was meant as a fully off-grid test of a 100 W solar array, an 80 Ah battery and a 40 lb-thrust electric trolling motor, and it worked well for the first 65 miles downstream, never close to depleting half the battery even against the current. Then torrential rain flooded the river and kept amber and red warning boards up for most of the return leg; a backup outboard sent ahead failed to start, so a borrowed Tohatsu took over instead, burning about a gallon of fuel where the electric motor alone could only crawl at half a mile an hour against the flow. He still yulohed where he could, and judged the electric-and-yuloh combination well suited to a long canal cruise, "but it's still hard to beat a small IC engine which is easily refuelled instantly too."[37]
