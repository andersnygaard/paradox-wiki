# Tom Sorensen

**Tom Sorensen** (Yahoo handle **`TDSoren`**, signing "Tom") built Jim Michalak's small **"Robbsboat"** design — by his own account possibly the first one ever completed — and used it, alongside a separate cambered-junk-rigged Marples 3m Sea Clipper trimaran he also built, as running comparison points for [Paradox](/paradox.md) builders through 2015-16. He described Robbsboat as "essentially a poor (or lazy?) man's Paradox," a "very easy/quick build... with many of the same virtues," and said in his first post to the list that he was "seriously considering an actual Paradox" for his next build.[1] No Paradox plans, wood cut, or boat name was ever confirmed on his own account.

## Building "Robbsboat," a comparison point for Paradox builders

Introducing his own build in April 2015, said he had picked the Michalak design specifically so he could finally see pictures and video of one under sail, surprised himself it had not been completed by anyone yet.[2] Watching Robbsboat go three-dimensional for the first time, months later, found it matched another builder's own account of the same milestone on a Paradox hull almost exactly.[3] Building the boat's chine logs, hung them to steam-bend from a pair of sawhorses with a five-gallon bucket of water slung between them, topping the bucket up and wetting the wood down daily for about a week; ended up laminating the gunnels instead of bending them solid, since they ran twice the chine logs' own thickness — "not a big deal" — having also considered, but not used, a YouTube-sourced plastic-sheet steam box for the chine logs themselves.[4][5][6]

Answering a 2017 question about whether Robbsboat suits a taller or less flexible sailor than Paradox, confirmed the design does carry real ballast of its own — about 200 lb, split between a pair of deep-cycle batteries (~86 lb) and bolted steel plates on the bulkheads per Michalak's own plans, needing most of it forward unless the forward storage is itself loaded — and fitted rope steering to keep his own weight forward sailing solo, trimming the boat properly for day sailing without piling gear up front. Traced the chine-runner-versus-leeboard choice to hull shape rather than to ballast: Matt Layden's boats carry a flatter run at the bow, keeping it in the water, where his own boat's Bolger-sharpie curvature keeps the bow clear and carries more rocker instead; had weighed chine runners himself but chose the proven leeboard, since his own sailing grounds call for frequent tacking, and judged Paradox by contrast built to track straight for long stretches rather than pivot constantly. See [design/larger-paradox.md — an April 2017 continuation](/design/larger-paradox.md#an-april-2017-continuation-an-alternative-for-a-taller-or-less-flexible-sailor) and [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — Jim Michalak's "Robbsboat"](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-a-critique-of-his-view-of-chine-runners).[15] In solidarity with a fellow correspondent's own first-sail account the following month, recalled a first-sail mishap of his own: the tie holding the tack of Robbsboat's balanced lug once blew out, turning the sail into "a great triangle kite that didn't want to come down."[16] Asked in 2018 why no Paradox seemed to carry a view dome in the hatch, judged one unnecessary if the view from inside is anything like Robbsboat's own. See [components/hatch.md — the plexiglass-dome question recurs](/components/hatch.md#the-plexiglass-dome-question-recurs-2018).[23]

## Materials and systems advice carried over from other boats

Weighing in on a wood-sourcing thread, judged real Douglas fir a good boatbuilding wood and hemlock "much more prone to rot and more toxic to work with — looks pretty, but stay away from the hemlock."[7] On heating, described his own (non-Paradox) trawler's Force 10 propane heater, flued through a single stainless rail pipe, single wall, with a ventilated thimble at the hull crossing to keep a combustible clearance from the surrounding structure.[8] On ballast-tank plumbing, suggested fitting a ball valve where the fill pipe terminates near the deck so it can be left open and flush, warned that a large temperature swing after filling could need a small air gap or a separate breather tube designed in for water expansion, and floated a hand-operated diaphragm pump to empty tanks while afloat that could double as a bilge pump given the right piping and valves.[9] On rebedding acrylic or lexan windows, favored plain latex caulk over a specialized marine sealant — paintable, cheap, and easy to clean up — running it under the lexan and smoothing it into the panel's edge to keep paint sealed over it, a treatment that held up four years in his own use.[25]

## Reading, endorsing, and a cambered junk rig

Read a fellow builder's blog start to finish in one sitting after being pointed to it — "OK, you suckered me in... now i'm hanging on the next installment."[10] Answering a fellow correspondent's question in April 2016 about whether a cambered junk rig had ever been tried on a Paradox, drew on his own experience: a cambered junk sail should do extremely well on the design, given how longitudinally stable it already is — "almost like a full keel boat" — citing the cambered junk rig he built and sailed for years on his own Marples 3m Sea Clipper trimaran, which performed well to windward, proved very easily (if not infinitely) reefable, and, its mechanics being old and well proven, was judged not especially finicky. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-2016-exchange-a-cambered-rig-endorsed-then-a-heeling-moment-objection-raised).[11] Returning to the same thread the next day, offered a boomkin as the simplest fix for a junk rig's aft-leading sheets, paired with cutting the sail narrower and taller so its own quick, easy reefing lets it simply be sheeted to the transom.[12] Countered a fellow correspondent's worry that a junk conversion would cost the ability to steer the sail from inside the cabin with his own contrary experience: the cambered junk rig on his Sea Clipper trimaran was raised, lowered, reefed, and stowed clear for paddling entirely from the cockpit, without ever leaving it.[13] Weighing the stock rig against a junk conversion once more, credited Matt Layden's roller-furling sail with a real advantage of its own — it can be disconnected from the mast and dropped onto the cabin top quickly, where a junk needs every batten parrel released first, unless it is already stowed furled in lazyjacks — and judged that advantage especially worth keeping for a cruising ground with frequent low bridges, where striking the mast repeatedly needs to be quick. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#further-sheeting-fixes-a-trimarans-cockpit-only-rig-and-the-cost-case-for-conversion).[14]

Repeated the same "full keel boat" framing over a year later on a different rig question, weighing a Bermuda rig this time rather than a junk: its centre of effort moving forward could give a lot of lee helm, but Paradox is essentially a "full length keel" boat, so it might handle the shift fine regardless. See [boats/thor.md — a second Bermuda-rig temptation, and a windward-performance account](/boats/thor.md#a-second-bermuda-rig-temptation-and-a-windward-performance-account-june-2017).[17]

## A 2017 sheeting comparison, and a planned junk conversion for Robbsboat

Comparing notes with Eerik Mee on THU's junk-sail sheeting in July 2017, reported that a comparable arrangement had worked well on his own boat too, provided the panel count was right. Full exchange: [boats/thu.md — how she sails](/boats/thu.md#how-she-sails).[18] Once Mee confirmed the panel count was right and described leading the sheet through the ventilation chamber, replied that Robbsboat's own balanced lug is sheeted the very same way — settling a question of his own, whether to route a future junk rig's sheet identically once built.[19] Returning to the thread the next day, reported having already built one cambered junk rig, for the Sea Clipper trimaran (above), and having decided to build a second to replace Robbsboat's own balance lug: cited Phil Bolger's own Chinese-gaff conversion approvingly for the same virtues — sheet-twist control and load spread across the battens, besides easier reefing — against, by contrast, a Bermuda-style tall, skinny mast's "whole tangle of wires and connections," worth carrying, in his view, only "to satisfy a racing rule when you don't even race"; Robbsboat's current balance lug performs well, but raising, furling, and reefing on the water would all be "Hugely simplified by Junking it up."[20]

Answering a skeptic's doubts about the junk rig's weight, complexity, and windward ability the same week, reframed the whole comparison around handling off the wind rather than to windward — where a junk rig's real edge lies, especially running dead downwind — and argued the weight concern is overstated: a junk sail's centre of mass sits lower than a sloop rig of the same area, and the rig saves the weight of multiple headsails and all the standing rigging besides. Itemised a simple junk's actual parts list against a sloop's much longer one — unstayed mast, one working sail with no spares, batten parrels tied once and left alone, a halyard, a yard parrel, a single mainsheet, lazyjacks, and, on a lighter build, a light downhaul — noting that in ordinary sailing and reefing only the sheet, halyard, and yard parrel ever need touching, where a sloop's downwind routine alone can mean easing the main, sheeting out a genoa, poling it out, and raising a spinnaker. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md — a 2017 objection](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-2017-objection-is-the-underlying-windward-question-even-settled).[21] Returning to the thread the next day, traced the standing lug's own sail-twist weakness specifically to its downhaul sitting at the luff itself, gaining none of the benefit a balance lug gets from luff tension forward of the downhaul holding the boom's aft end down; doubted a balance lug could be fitted with roller furling at all, since doing so would bring the sail's area forward, risking lee helm, and would likely complicate rigging a vang too.[22]

## An outside comment on rudder effectiveness (August 2018)

Weighing in on a thread about a fellow correspondent fighting the tiller running, offered the mechanism from his own non-Paradox experience: a bow riding high moves a boat's centre of effort aft and gives lee helm, matching how his own Potter needed weight carried well forward to get its chine into the water and sail well to windward. Full exchange: [operations/sailing-characteristics.md — a 2018 recurrence](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-2018-recurrence-tiller-fatigue-running-and-two-rudder-position-ideas).[24]
