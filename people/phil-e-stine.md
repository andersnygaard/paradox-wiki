# phil_e.stine

**phil_e.stine** drew on a working life spent in a technical field for a first-principles habit of mind that runs through his record.[1] He has not built a Paradox of his own. What holds it together is patience with one hard question, worked out mathematically within his first few months and returned to for the next three and a half years: whether a Paradox could be meaningfully enlarged and what that would cost in displacement, draft and sail. Alongside it he worked out a footwell and hatch alternative for cabin access, traced several rig details to their answer and compiled the design's sail-insignia photo album.

## Tracking the scaled-up Paradox question

He tracked, from first principles, whether a Paradox could be meaningfully enlarged. Early on he worked out that a 20 percent linear enlargement multiplies displacement roughly 1.7 times, putting a stretched hull's new displacement near 2,400 lb, in his own words "a BIG little boat," with draft rising by about another inch and a half.[2] The figure held up well against John Welsford's Swaggie, a genuine comparison boat of a similar length.[3] He kept the sail's centre-of-effort height distinct from the growth in sail area and used the cube of the linear factor, not the factor itself, for the displacement multiplier.[4] Years later a fellow builder's own eighteen-foot, roughly 30 percent enlargement gave him a real example to weigh against his own earlier numbers, length, beam, displacement, draft and sail area, with a side-tank ballast layout and a strongback build standing out to him as approaches worth following.[5] By 2017 the question he had first worked out on paper still had no completed example to test it against; five months later it did, when Eerik Mee's "THU" became the first enlarged Paradox finished and sailed.[6] Full account: [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md).

## Cockpit and cabin access

A footwell forward of the seat, covered by a panel for sleeping, was his own proposed alternative to raising the roof or lowering the sole; once given the boat's own figures, he judged the arrangement workable.[7] A foredeck hatch, prompted by an ambiguous sketch in one of Don Elliott's magazine instalments, came up as a way to load something as heavy as a battery without hauling it the length of the boat; a narrower fix, favoured when the question resurfaced, kept bedding and a portable head in a crate with a pull-line instead, reachable from the main cabin.[8] The trunk cabin, on his own account, gives much better visibility, particularly forward, when buttoned up for heavy weather.[9] Full account: [components/interior.md](/components/interior.md#cockpit-and-cabin-egress-with-limited-knees-or-shoulders-and-footwell-dimensions-december-2013), [a foredeck hatch, revisited](/components/hatch.md#a-foredeck-hatch-revisited-2016-access-and-ventilation-against-dryness-and-a-solar-panel) and [components/pilothouse.md](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin).

## Rig, stowage and the sail insignia album

Three rig details carry his name at their start: how the boom is secured at the mast end, whether a topping lift is worth fitting and how the mainsheet might route past the tiller baffle to clear the outboard; each is worked out in full on the rig pages.[10][11] The sail-balance principle he worked from is plain: moving a boat's centre of effort further aft helps her tack, not further forward.[12] He compiled the design's sail-insignia photo album, opening it with an entry of his own rendered in mathematical notation, in his own words "arcane enough to be appropriate," endorsing a black swan already proposed for suiting his own sense of humour and adding a bumble bee of his own alongside it.[13][14] On stowage, he weighed converting the built-in ballast tanks to general storage against the loss of the ability to refill drinking water from rain and proposed sand-filled watertight bags as ballast instead, made up in advance and dumped when a ramp was needed.[15] He also traced a route around the lower Mississippi's tow traffic for America's inland "Great Loop" circuit, by way of the Ohio, Cumberland and Tennessee-Tombigbee waterways, judging a single 250-mile stretch with no realistic refuelling the real obstacle to covering it under power in a boat as small as a Paradox.[16] Full account: [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#the-topping-lift-question-answered-zero-tension-and-matt-laydens-own-private-advice-against-fitting-one-december-2013), [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-further-december-2013-proposal-routing-the-sheet-past-the-tiller-baffle-inspired-by-dave-bolducs-enigma), [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#why-the-mast-and-the-sail-on-it-are-offset-to-port-rather-than-centered), [components/rig/sail.md](/components/rig/sail.md#whether-an-official-sail-insignia-exists), [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) and [the American Great Loop](/merits/inland-waterway-and-canal-touring.md#the-american-great-loop-canal-current-rules-out-human-power-though-a-short-mast-helps).
