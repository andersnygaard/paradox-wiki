# David C. Smith

**David C. Smith** is a Pacific Northwest professional boatbuilder of twenty years[1] who began building a Paradox strictly to the plans in 2003 and named her **"Swash."**[2] She was built from **Joubert Okoume BS-1088 plywood**, **Honduras mahogany** and **West System epoxy**,[3] and sails the lower Columbia River in Oregon. Full build account: [boats/swash.md](/boats/swash.md).

## A designer-fidelity credo

Smith held that building strictly to the plans was owed to the designer: Paradox was drawn only after an evolution of a single type, refined through the prototype's own extensive use, unlike most designs' more casual origin.[1] He drew a line between changing a design's geometry or specifications, which he held was off limits, and changing details that did not touch them, a builder's prerogative he compared to routine practice at some of the world's most regarded yards.[4]

The credo had a personal origin. His only earlier boat, a modified **Elver** canoe yawl built with leeboards in place of a centerboard, he judged "a disappointing boat in general," a result he tied to having "disrespected the work of the designer."[4][1]

The same reasoning ran through a fuller defense of the design's strength. The Paradox as drawn was already robust and matched to its specified materials, and no ordinary use should trouble a properly built hull: a full set of marine plywood for the whole boat had cost him $766, cheap enough that strengthening an already sound design past its plans solved a problem that did not exist, and a well-built, well-maintained plywood Paradox, in his view, could be passed down for generations.[5] He drew the same line from a yacht-class analogy: a fiberglass Folkboat replica built faithful enough for the class association to accept her was still arguably not a true Folkboat, and later "improved" derivatives pushed the drift further; a design could be varied from, but its core geometry and materials, in his view, could only be changed, never bettered. Full account: [construction/hull-planking.md](/construction/hull-planking.md#the-case-for-a-second-layer-of-cloth) and [construction/hull-planking.md](/construction/hull-planking.md#museums-materials-and-the-case-for-building-to-spec).[6]

He extended the same fidelity to material substitution: a Paradox hull built in steel would be a serious mistake, since the design's construction was calculated specifically for plywood, epoxy and glass, and a different material could only still carry the Paradox name if Matt Layden himself recalculated its weights and centers for it. Full account: [design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#smiths-rebuttal-why-a-steel-paradox-would-be-a-horrible-mistake).[7]

## Seamanship over seaworthiness

A sharp line between seamanship and seaworthiness ran through his view of how far offshore a Paradox could safely venture. Seaworthiness, to him, is a label a boat earns only in hindsight, while what actually matters is a seamanlike attitude gained through personal experience: a foot dragging on the beach counted as a greater danger than open sea room well out from shore, where distance from shipping, fishing gear, shoals and land-influenced weather let a sailor sleep with less worry. He saw no reason the Paradox as designed could not make an ocean crossing at the hands of an experienced sailor, though he reserved judgment on blue-water bravado until he had built and sailed his own, and called the design itself, evolved through years of its own designer's experimentation, "a masterful design" not to be improved upon. Full account: [design/blue-water-suitability.md](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#seamanship-over-seaworthiness-a-professional-builders-endorsement).[8]

## Rig, anchoring and heat

Easing the sheet until the luff or the sail's belly loses its shape, then sheeting in just enough to firm it before tacking, was standard rig practice for him;[9] leech telltales cut from ordinary cassette tape worked well, especially in light air.[10] See [components/rig/sail.md](/components/rig/sail.md#the-dabbler-re-cut-mark-ii-sail-a-decisive-improvement).

He also weighed building a spar as a wood and carbon composite: a birdmouth-built spruce mandrel sleeved in braided carbon fiber sourced from aerospace and orthopedic suppliers, worked tight over the wood and wetted out with epoxy, the real payoff more the experience and bragging rights than any measurable gain since the weight difference against the plans' wooden mast would be negligible.[11] A fellow builder estimated the result at roughly half the weight and triple the strength of the stock spar. Full account: [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#a-birdmouth-carbon-fiber-composite-spar-as-an-alternative-to-the-plans-rig).[12]

For ground tackle, a flush afterdeck well for a Danforth-type anchor, draining through the vent baffle, and a parallel scheme built around a 4 in PVC pipe for a folding grapnel with its own rode bin were his two proposals, either judged an easy retrofit even on a boat already built. See [operations/ground-tackle.md](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-flush-anchor-well-weight-distribution-and-drainage).[13]

On cabin comfort, galley stoves drew the same comparison years before he chose one for himself: the Force 10 Seacook was rated good quality, set against the Forespar Mini Galley as a comparison worth making. See [operations/cabin-heating.md](/operations/cabin-heating.md#choosing-a-galley-cooking-stove-the-force-10-seacook-and-the-forespar-alternative).[14]

## Still building

In 2019, having at last secured one of the last Forespar Mini Galley stoves before production ceased, Smith built a lighter alternative instead: a Jetboil canister stove on a homemade gimbal, portable enough to move between the aft galley and the cabin proper.[15][16] He also proposed a group-authored Paradox build manual and authored its introduction and two installments, on the build site and on deck-beam lamination jigs; see [construction/build-effort.md](/construction/build-effort.md#the-build-manuals-introduction).[17] By then he was also refitting a Freedom 40, **"Eyrie of Astoria,"** converting her from a cat-ketch to a cambered-panel junk cat-schooner rig, while "Swash" remained under construction.[18]
