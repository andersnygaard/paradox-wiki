# Eerik Mee

**Eerik Mee** (byline **"Eerik"** on his blog) is a boatbuilder in **Tartu, Estonia**,[1] and the builder of **"[THU](/boats/thu.md),"** a [Paradox](/paradox.md) built **20% larger than the plans** and rigged as a **Chinese junk**, launched in 2017. He documented the whole build on a personal blog, `paradoxbuild.blogspot.com`, which is preserved as a first-class source at [references/eerik-mee-blog.md](/references/eerik-mee-blog.md); this page draws the builder and the build out of it.

He is notable in the archive for having actually built two things it had otherwise only argued about. THU is the **only scaled-up Paradox on record built and sailed** — the recurring "[larger Paradox](/design/larger-paradox.md)" realised, at almost exactly the 20% figures the mailing list had worked out years earlier — and it carries the **only purpose-designed cambered junk rig** on the design, as against the stock lug *converted* to junk on [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md).

## The build

Mee built THU from **July 2015**, scaling every measurement on the plans by **1.2** — "I multiplied just all measurements with 1.2, so yes, it is also wider and higher. LOA turned out about 5.05 m and width about 1.5 m."[2] The hull, chine runners, rudder, and a 6 m birdmouth mast were built through 2015–16; the cabin, deck, and fit-out followed, and the boat was **launched and christened "THU"** on an inland lake on **5 July 2017**.[3][4] Fitting-out continued for two more years — the ballast cast as lead pigs, a full electrical system and an electric outboard added — with more lake sailing in 2019.[5]

The build was **repeatedly paused for overseas work**: his projects took him away for long stretches — a Thailand project that "took about half a year longer than initially planned," later work on Bali and elsewhere in Indonesia, and a spell in Norway — each of which cost him a building or sailing season.[5] He built at home in **Estonia**, doing much of the work outdoors in a garden and garage and waiting on warm-enough weather for epoxy; the blog's own references fit an EU builder, citing EU rules on handling lead and the difficulty of sourcing the right aluminium alloy "in this country."[5]

## What he changed, and why

Mee's departures from the plans are the substance of his contribution, and he explained his reasoning in the blog's comment threads:

- **The 20% scale-up.** He sized the boat up uniformly to fit himself, arriving at **LOA ≈ 5.05 m, beam ≈ 1.5 m** — within centimetres of the worked 20% scale-up the list had carried since 2008.[2] The stock scantlings were kept; the spars scaled with the hull.
- **The cambered junk rig.** Rather than convert the stock lug, he designed a **six-panel cambered junk sail** from scratch, following **Arne Kverneland**'s method and drawing on the **Junk Rig Association**.[6] He first tried a flat-cut sample plan, "but I didn't like the looks of the boat; so I modified it towards Reddish type at roach. The HML is actually flat cut sail with not so good winward performance. It's the AK's camber that improves it."[6]
- **The mast moved forward.** To carry the taller (6 m vs ~4.8 m) junk rig in balance, he moved the mast **from aft of bulkhead 2 to as far forward of it as possible**, wanting more mast below deck and clearance between the transom and the upper sheet.[6] He intended the boat to be **moored, not trailered**, accepting the heavier mast's rig-up time in exchange.[6]
- **A worked-out ballast and systems boat.** THU carries **~240 kg of ballast** (twelve cast-lead pigs plus water ballast) in a compartment aft of bulkhead 3, and — unlike most Paradoxes — a **full electrical system and an electric outboard**.[7][5]

His full account of how she was built, equipped, and sails is at [THU](/boats/thu.md); the source itself at [Eerik Mee's blog](/references/eerik-mee-blog.md).

## The record's limits

Mee posted to the mailing list thirty times on record: once before his own build began and twenty-nine more times once it was under way. In November 2014 — nearly a year before starting THU — he cautioned a fellow member that a magnetic aquarium-glass cleaner, floated as a way to wipe salt off a Paradox's windows underway, would scratch its acrylic or polycarbonate glazing immediately, since such cleaners are made for glass fish tanks.[10] In August 2015, with the hull already a season old, he asked the list's short-lived registry thread whether a photographed boat — an unfamiliar rig, and on a second look no visible chine runners at all — still counted as a Paradox; see [people/john-starkie.md](/people/john-starkie.md#a-modified-paradox-with-no-chine-runners-and-a-keel-theory-of-tacking) for the fuller technical exchange it drew.[11][12] Days later, on the "Paradox tacking difficulties" thread the registry exchange had spun off, floated a further theory of his own — that heavy reefing might move the sail's centre of effort forward rather than aft, since the head is shorter than the foot, adding lee helm and making tacking harder; see [operations/sailing-characteristics.md](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#reefed-sail-tacking-trouble-and-a-chine-runner-as-keel-theory-2015) for the fuller thread.[13] Refining the theory the next day, argued the sail's own forward tilt means reefing furls away its lower, more-aft part while the more-forward part stays set, adding lee helm masked in normal sailing by the large rudder but showing up once heavily reefed — offered as the likely reason Petite Lady's chine runners were cut down aft, and noted a second Paradox, his own, nearing the chine-runner stage and being considered for runners set a little further forward than stock.[14] A further message, three days later, finally tied that second Paradox to the blog build by name: having "locked" her name and posted his own "going 3D" photo — a tradition he noted every Paradox seems to observe — he confirmed on the list that the boat under way in Estonia was a **20% larger version**, and described checking and fixing the diagonals separately at every section while assembling bulkheads to sides, since sections can each be out of true differently; full detail on [THU](/boats/thu.md#citations) and at [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method).[15] The same week he twice gave general cutting technique to a second builder constructing an unrelated Puddle-Duck-based design ("Gorfnik") — a router-and-bearing trick for trimming identical panels off a first-shaped one, and a circular-saw method producing smoother curved cuts than a jigsaw — and mentioned in passing treating THU's own okoume plywood with a monoethylene-glycol solution before epoxy sealing; full detail at [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#building-the-cabin-panels) and [THU's build notes](/boats/thu.md#build-details-worth-noting).[16][17] Answering a fellow correspondent's direct question three days later,[18] he confirmed the 20% enlargement ran through every dimension of the boat, not just her length — the earliest on-the-record confirmation of that point, years before the 2019 blog comment above restated the same figures.[19] Later the same day he reported THU's bottom now fitted: a straight aluminium bar sighted along the chine logs confirmed they ran level to each other and to the bulkheads over the full length, corrected with a block plane and finished with a sanding block, and a pair of witness-dowel holes drilled through the dry-fitted bottom into bulkhead #3 let it be relocated in exactly the same position for the permanent glue-up; full detail at [THU](/boats/thu.md#build-details-worth-noting) and [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method).[20] A further message the next day added a bulkhead-fitting refinement: when dry-fitting bulkheads and sides, never took the box fully apart again, instead pressing the sides a little way apart to free a single bulkhead at a time for a bevel adjustment and reinstalling it immediately with thickened epoxy, found easier that way; full detail at [THU](/boats/thu.md#build-details-worth-noting) and [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method).[21] A week later reported the bottom and sides glassed in a single session, judged too much work for two people to want to repeat, except the chine runners' top sides, glassed separately and covered with peel ply to save some fairing work later; full detail at [THU](/boats/thu.md#build-details-worth-noting).[22] The following month gave a fellow builder starting his own bilge and tank dividers a firsthand comparison: fitted his own dividers in before the bottom went on, easier and more precise that way, checking them with a straight bar laid crosswise over the chine logs so everything else in between sat lower; for locating the bottom panel itself, marked a centerline on the bottom and on each bulkhead, drilled two dowel holes through the bottom into the centermost bulkhead ahead of time, then at glue-up lowered the bottom onto the dowels first with a person holding down each end, catching the holes already in place with no glue misplaced, and worked the fastening outward toward both ends. Full exchange: [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md — bilge and tank divider order](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#bilge-and-tank-divider-order-and-a-dowel-registration-technique-for-the-bottom-panel).[23] Returned to the list once more in January 2016, on a new "Sail position" thread, to say a centered sail would not equalize pointing ability, since the sail's own shape differs on each tack; floated a further guess that the sail's port-side stowage may simply suit working the yuloh with the right hand, and suggested a builder wanting the sail centered instead consider building the boat's whole interior, including the mast position, as a mirror image. Full exchange: [components/rig/index.md — why the sail isn't flown from the starboard side instead](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-sail-isnt-flown-from-the-starboard-side-instead).[24] Three months later, closed a question of his own from eight months earlier: research into the same chine-runner-less, Bermuda-rigged Paradox he had first flagged in the 2015 registry thread found she had lost her chine runners first and gained a leeboard afterward, a one-way conversion. Full exchange: [people/john-starkie.md — a modified Paradox with no chine runners, and a keel theory of tacking](/people/john-starkie.md#a-modified-paradox-with-no-chine-runners-and-a-keel-theory-of-tacking).[25] Two more messages followed the same week, continuing a different correspondent's rig tangent on the same "ballast" thread: noted the Paradox registry listed no boat yet sailing under junk rig, though one — his own, still under construction — was; and, replying to a fellow correspondent's worry that a junk rig might lack the heeling power needed to work a Paradox's chine runners at all, explained a junk sail's characteristically low rig stress comes from its battens acting as so many small extra booms, each panel carrying only a small share of the total load, while passing on a separate, opposite concern (from a correspondent named only "Jim") that the sail's own weight and extra camber cloth could tip the boat too far the other way instead. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md — a 2016 exchange: a cambered rig endorsed, then a heeling-moment objection raised](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-2016-exchange-a-cambered-rig-endorsed-then-a-heeling-moment-objection-raised).[26][27] Asked directly, a day later, whether the boat under construction was indeed his own, confirmed that it was.[28] A further message the next day summarized his own already-made design choices in reply to the thread's accumulated objections — a vertical-leech sail plan chosen over a fan type to hold the centre of effort aft, the mast and chine runners both moved forward to compensate, and a real cost already accepted: losing the stock rig's easy on-deck lowering of a reefed sail. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md — THU: a purpose-designed cambered rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#thu-a-purpose-designed-cambered-rig-method-not-yet-fully-solved).[29] A final message four days later, replying to being told his own design work had been noticed on the Junk Rig Association's technical forum, explained the forum thread — titled "Building a junkrigged Matt Leyden's Paradox" — was less a display of expertise than a way of sharing his thinking so others could skip a few steps; he had joined the association specifically to get help from experienced junk-rig designers and users, "and that is pretty much what has happened in that thread." Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md — THU: a purpose-designed cambered rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#thu-a-purpose-designed-cambered-rig-method-not-yet-fully-solved).[30] A further exchange, over a year later in June 2017 and continuing across several days, helped a newcomer marking out his own bulkhead and transom panels for the first time — reassured that the tight fit he had hit at bulkhead #2 was the plans' own known geometry, not an error, since other Paradox builders (Mee included) had met the same joint without complaint, and gave the transom-panel joint's own dimensions besides.[31][32] Days later, when the same newcomer's transom-height arithmetic still came up short, confirmed the reasoning was correct — his own hull, a 20% scaled-up Paradox, uses a different cutting pattern that could not be checked against it directly — and suggested swapping the transom panel with bulkhead #2's own smaller piece, just 451mm at its widest point, resolving the question. Full exchange: [people/sybe-starkenburg.md](/people/sybe-starkenburg.md#citations) and [construction/panel-layout.md — tight spots builders hit](/construction/panel-layout.md#tight-spots-builders-hit).[33][34] Days later, on a "Furling Friction" thread weighing a Bermuda-rig conversion, gave the fullest account yet of the chine-runner-less, Bermuda-rigged Paradox first flagged in 2015: named her builder, **Tom Steinmetz**, for the first time on record and linked a video of her sailing, adding that photographs of the boat once online had since gone down, and that a further video showing her leeboard also existed but could not be relocated. See [boats/thor.md — a second Bermuda-rig temptation, and a windward-performance account](/boats/thor.md#a-second-bermuda-rig-temptation-and-a-windward-performance-account-june-2017) and [people/john-starkie.md — a modified Paradox with no chine runners, and a keel theory of tacking](/people/john-starkie.md#a-modified-paradox-with-no-chine-runners-and-a-keel-theory-of-tacking).[35] Three weeks later he gave the sealing details of his own experimental hatch: built with its forward end 1 cm lower than the aft, matched by the structure beneath it, so the gasket runs under slight compression throughout its travel — a gap just under 15 mm closed, about 25 mm open — and worked smoothly with silicone oil smeared on the gasket; plastic strips stuck to the cabin top under the hatch-runner grooves kept the runners from scratching the finish. See [components/hatch.md — an asymmetric hatch slope and a measured gasket-compression system](/components/hatch.md#an-asymmetric-hatch-slope-and-a-measured-gasket-compression-system-2017).[36] THU herself was launched the following week, over the weekend of 1-2 July 2017, reported to the list the same evening with friends aboard to celebrate and everyone taking a turn at the helm — tacking unexpectedly well once enough sail was up, in conditions ranging from near-calm to a breeze strong enough to reef down a panel.[37] Asked for photographs showing how the junk rig was fitted and sized, he promised more within days once friends' own photos came in, cautioning the sheeting was not yet in its final setup — the same boom-twist-and-sheeting problem the blog's own later account confirms was never fully solved on the water (above).[38] A further message, replying to a fellow correspondent's own years-old recurring question about whether any scaled-up Paradox had ever actually been completed and sailed, confirmed that one had — his own, just launched the weekend before.[39] Days later, posted photographs of the sail and its still-unsettled sheeting arrangement — the boom running too twisted in, with only a very light, dying breeze to test it in — and, once a fellow correspondent confirmed a comparable arrangement worked well for him too, confirmed his own panel count was right, having led the sheet through the ventilation chamber with two blocks on the boom; the boom still pulled in too much, so he changed the arrangement and took her out once more in very light wind, with further options yet to try. Full exchange: [boats/thu.md — how she sails](/boats/thu.md#how-she-sails).[40][41] A final message the following month, unconnected to his own build, answered a fellow owner's restoration question about finishing a yard's end holes: drill them oversized, tape one end, coat the sides with neat epoxy, then fill with thickened epoxy and redrill at the correct size once cured — the same treatment recommended for the whole yard before painting — and described tying his own halyard on by winding a turn of line around the yard with a loop held from slipping with double-sided tape, rather than fitting a pad eye. Full account: [components/rig/index.md — preventing rot at the yard's end holes, and two halyard-tie methods](/components/rig/index.md#yard-end-holes-purpose-and-a-rot-prevention-technique-for-drilling-them) and [boats/edge.md](/boats/edge.md#sold-to-deric-and-restoration-begins-2017).[42] Beyond this exchange, his build was reported only on his own blog. The blog answers a few readers' questions and links out to the Junk Rig Association, but it is a single builder's record of a single, heavily modified boat, and the sailing it documents is a handful of day-sails on a lake rather than a cruise.[3][4] What survives is a completed, launched, and repeatedly sailed boat, tuned but not finished tuning when the record ends in 2019.[5]

In January 2018, prompted by a fellow builder's report of a cam-cleat orientation fix, drew up and posted a corrected drawing of the halyard and furling-line cleats, working out the mounting height needed for both cleating and uncleating from the seat. Full technique: [components/rig/internal-rigging.md](/components/rig/internal-rigging.md#cam-cleat-orientation-cleating-uncleating-and-reefing-from-the-seat-2018).[43]

Six months later, replying to a fellow owner's call for summer sailing videos, shared video of THU's own 2017 launch, noting that the Thailand project (above) was running longer than planned and so the 2018 sailing season had not yet begun for him.[44]

Back on the mailing list in May 2019, on an unrelated builder's gudgeon-fastening question, reported having epoxied his own gudgeon in place with no problem, and, pressed further the next day, confirmed he had in fact backed the epoxy with bolts too; general practice on the question remains divided — see [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — fastening the finished gudgeon, not casting it](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#a-may-2019-recurrence-fastening-the-finished-gudgeon-not-casting-it).[45][46]

Back on the list again in April 2020, on a thread about what builders would change on a second build, suggested changing the vent trunk's PVC pipe to copper to discourage growth inside it, and reported having built a rear hatch as well — a departure some builders who tried it have reversed, judging it left the cabin too weak, but one that has worked on his own boat. See [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md#a-2009-drain-routing-question-and-a-mast-step-electrical-contact-detail) and [components/hatch.md — an asymmetric hatch slope and a measured gasket-compression system](/components/hatch.md#an-asymmetric-hatch-slope-and-a-measured-gasket-compression-system-2017).[47][48] Answering a question about the outer rail's carriage bolts that August, gave a second, independent reason alongside strength — bolting the rail on, rather than fixing it permanently, makes it removable for repainting or revarnishing, and seals the bolts themselves with o-rings for watertightness. See [components/hatch.md — the outer rail's carriage bolts](/components/hatch.md#the-outer-rails-carriage-bolts).[49] The next day, on the same thread, gave a screw-bedding technique distinct from his own earlier yard-hole method: predrill the screw hole to size, counterbore the top of it a little wider, fill the counterbore with slightly thickened epoxy, and drive the screw home while the epoxy is still wet, rather than letting it cure first and redrilling.[50]

He was, however, an active member of the [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) from soon after it started, posting sailing videos and the first-hand sailing experience the blog's 2019 endpoint doesn't reach, and helping other builders — for instance advising a builder on rudder-gudgeon geometry — with that material folded into [THU](/boats/thu.md).[8][9]

Back on the mailing list once more in July 2025, answering a newcomer's plywood-sourcing question for a fellow 20% scale-up, gave a grain-direction caution drawn from his own build: on oversized 3000 x 1500 mm sheets the grain often runs in the 1500 mm direction, which is fine for the bottom but worth checking against which way the panel needs to bend for the deck. Full exchange: [construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md — a 2025 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2025-recurrence-sourcing-oversized-sheets-for-a-20-scale-up-grain-direction-and-a-foam-hull-alternative-floated).[51]
