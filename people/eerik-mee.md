# Eerik Mee

**Eerik Mee**, who signs himself **"Eerik"** on his own blog, is a boatbuilder in **Tartu, Estonia**.[1] He first wrote to the Paradox community in **November 2014**, nearly a year before starting his own boat, and has stayed part of it since, into **2025**. From **2015** he built **"[THU](/boats/thu.md)"**, a [Paradox](/paradox.md) enlarged **20%** on every dimension of the plans and rigged with a six-panel cambered junk sail designed from scratch to the **Junk Rig Association**'s method. Launched on an inland Estonian lake over the weekend of **1-2 July 2017**, she tacked cleanly from her first trials and has been sailed, and reefed down in a real breeze, across several seasons since.

He is one of the very few people to have actually built something the Paradox community had spent years arguing about instead of building. Matt Layden had sanctioned a 20% scale-up of Paradox in principle since the design's earliest years, and the idea recurred for a decade without anyone finishing one; Mee built and sailed her, landing within centimetres of the dimensions worked out over a decade earlier. His junk sail is the only one built for the design to a purpose-drawn, cambered pattern; Planktos, the only other junk-rigged Paradox on record, carries a stock lug simply converted rather than a rig designed for camber from the outset. He has kept helping other builders work through their own boats' problems ever since, most recently in 2025.

## The build

Mee built THU from **July 2015**, scaling every measurement on the plans by **1.2**: "I multiplied just all measurements with 1.2, so yes, it is also wider and higher. LOA turned out about 5.05 m and width about 1.5 m."[2] The hull, chine runners, rudder, and a 6 m birdmouth mast were built through 2015-16; the cabin, deck, and fit-out followed, and the boat was **launched and christened "THU"** on an inland lake on **5 July 2017**, tacking cleanly on her first trials.[3][4] Fitting-out continued for two more years, the ballast cast as lead pigs, a full electrical system and an electric outboard added, with more lake sailing in 2019.[5]

The build was **repeatedly paused for overseas work**: a Thailand project that "took about half a year longer than initially planned," later work on Bali and elsewhere in Indonesia and a spell in Norway each cost him a building or sailing season.[5] He built at home in **Estonia**, doing much of the work outdoors in a garden and garage and waiting on warm-enough weather for epoxy; he worked under EU rules on handling lead and found it hard to source the right aluminium alloy "in this country."[5]

## What he changed, and why

Mee's departures from the plans, and his reasoning:

- **The 20% scale-up.** He sized the boat up uniformly to fit himself, arriving at **LOA ≈ 5.05 m, beam ≈ 1.5 m**, within centimetres of the 20% scale-up figures worked out on paper back in 2008.[2] The stock scantlings were kept; the spars scaled with the hull.
- **The cambered junk rig.** Rather than convert the stock lug, he designed a **six-panel cambered junk sail** from scratch, following **Arne Kverneland**'s method and drawing on the **Junk Rig Association**.[6] He first tried a flat-cut sample plan, "but I didn't like the looks of the boat; so I modified it towards Reddish type at roach. The HML is actually flat cut sail with not so good winward performance. It's the AK's camber that improves it."[6]
- **The mast moved forward.** To carry the taller (6 m vs ~4.8 m) junk rig in balance, he moved the mast **from aft of bulkhead 2 to as far forward of it as possible**, wanting more mast below deck and clearance between the transom and the upper sheet.[6] He intended the boat to be **moored, not trailered**, accepting the heavier mast's rig-up time in exchange.[6]
- **A worked-out ballast and systems boat.** THU carries **~240 kg of ballast** (twelve cast-lead pigs plus water ballast) in a compartment aft of bulkhead 3, and, unlike most Paradoxes, a **full electrical system and an electric outboard**.[7][5]

His full account of how she was built, equipped, and sails is at [THU](/boats/thu.md); the source itself at [Eerik Mee's blog](/references/eerik-mee-blog.md).

## Helping other builders

Mee's record of helping other builders predates his own boat by nearly a year: in November 2014 he warned a future builder that a magnetic aquarium-glass cleaner, floated as a way to wipe salt off a Paradox's windows underway, would scratch its acrylic or polycarbonate glazing immediately, since such cleaners are made for glass fish tanks.[8] Once his own build was under way the pattern kept recurring: he gave a fellow builder constructing an unrelated Puddle-Duck-based design, "Gorfnik," a router-and-bearing trick for trimming matched panels and a circular-saw method for smoother curved cuts;[9][10] he talked [Sybe Starkenburg](/people/sybe-starkenburg.md) through his first bulkhead and transom layout, confirming a tight joint at bulkhead #2 was the plans' own known geometry rather than an error, and worked out a swap that resolved a shortfall in his transom-height arithmetic;[11][12][13][14] and he gave rot-prevention and halyard-tie advice for [Deric Tallman](/people/frederic-tallman.md)'s newly restored **"[Edge](/boats/edge.md)."**[15] He drew up and shared a corrected cam-cleat orientation drawing once another builder's own fix prompted it.[16] Two years later, in a gudgeon-fastening exchange that also drew Deric Tallman's own view, he gave his own practice: epoxied in place, and, pressed further, confirmed backed with bolts too.[17][18] He kept up the same habit years later, sharing his own sailing videos from 2020 and working out the bushing clearance and swing angle a fellow builder needed for a cast rudder gudgeon.[19][20] The pattern was still running years later: he talked Saidul Haque out of moving the tiller above the aft deck, since the standing tiller hole stays above the waterline on any knockdown and doubles as an air intake when the hatch is shut;[21] told [Benjamin Bloss](/people/benjamin-bloss.md) that a cracked sheer log, already glued and nailed to the side panel and due to be fixed to the deck as well, needed nothing more than the crack filled with epoxy against damp;[22] told [Andreas Westerberg](/people/andreas-westerberg.md), redesigning his steering around a sealed aft compartment, to fit rudder horns so the control lines would enter their pipes straighter;[23] gave a fellow 20% scale-up builder a plywood grain-direction caution drawn from his own build, that oversized sheets often run their grain the short way, fine for the bottom but worth checking before cutting a deck;[24] and, answering Rob Stracey's plea for a Paradox materials list, closed with the best line in the record for him: "I ordered my wood, and the only plank that was left over was the one meant for yuloh that I didn't make."[25]

He also settled questions nobody else on the record had. Long before his own chine runners were cut, he offered his own theory for why a heavily reefed Paradox grows hard to tack: the sail's own forward tilt means reefing furls away its lower, more-aft part while the more-forward part stays set, adding lee helm that the large rudder normally masks; he later applied the same reasoning to set his own chine runners a little further forward than stock.[26][27] Answering a 2016 question about sail position, he reasoned that a centered sail would not equalize pointing ability, since the sail's own shape differs on each tack, and suggested that a builder wanting the sail centered instead mirror the boat's whole interior, including the mast position.[28] And across nearly two years, from a 2015 registry sighting of a chine-runner-less, Bermuda-rigged Paradox to research that finally closed the question in 2017, he traced her history and named her builder, Tom Steinmetz, for the record.[29][30][31][32]
