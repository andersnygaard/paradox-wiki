# Mat

**Mat** contributed materials-science and engineering judgment on chine-runner construction, hull scaling and building materials for the [Paradox](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) design in the early 2000s. He is not the designer [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md), despite the similar name.

## Chine-runner materials and geometry

He researched armoring or ballasting the chine runners with metal in place of plain plywood, weighing lead, ferrocement, steel and copper-nickel plate on cost and strength before settling on 90/10 copper-nickel, prized for combining antifouling protection with strength despite its high cost and lower yield strength than mild steel.[1][2][3] He also traced the runners' curve to their length rather than to hydrodynamics: a straighter runner at a better heel angle could be smaller and lower-drag, on a hull he judged "by far the strongest 14 ft sharpie" he had seen.[4] Both ideas are covered in full, with the cost and strength figures, at [a metal-shoe materials study](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#metal-chine-runner-shoes-as-ballastarmor--a-materials-tangent) and [the runners' curve and drag](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#chine-runner-curvature-and-drag). He also tracked a different designer's use of chine runners on a dory-sided catamaran hull,[5] and doubted the technique would transfer to Jim Michalak's "Jewelbox Jr," a hull with much less rocker in the bow than Paradox.[6] See [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-dory-hulled-catamaran-design-using-chine-runners) and [adapting chine runners to a different hull](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#adapting-chine-runners-to-a-different-hull-in-place-of-a-leeboard).

## Scaling and hull comparison

For enlarging Paradox, he set a rule of thumb: a hull scales up to about 10 percent in length without complication; beyond that a designer should be consulted. For larger scaling he cited Lars Larsson's *Principles of Yacht Design* (length by 1.5, beam and freeboard by 1.33), warning against excess beam since a chine-runner hull's stability depends on reaching the right heel angle.[7] He favored external ballast over internal and a dory hull over a ballasted sharpie, pointing to Jay Benford's designs as reference points.[7][8] Full detail at [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#scaling-paradox-to-18-20-ft-and-the-bilge-keel-question-revisited). He separately compared Paradox's resistance to pounding with Matt Layden's newer, lighter 12 ft design, attributing the difference to displacement and rocker. Phil Bolger's "Micro," with a great deal of rocker and a known pounding problem, served as the cautionary case that led Bolger to retrofit a V-bottom bow to several of his own designs.[9] See [sailing characteristics](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#pounding-rare-at-anchor-occasional-underway).

## Plywood, resin and finish choices

For sheet goods, 3/8 in plywood was judged acceptable for the sides; stock construction called for 1/2 in unglassed plywood on the bottom (or a 9 oz. cloth layup for extra insurance), with epoxy preferred over polyester. For a trailer-sailed boat, skipping fiberglass altogether in favor of repainting every year or two was judged good enough.[10][11] Full detail at [Paradox building plans](/references/building-plans.md#a-community-written-beginners-guide). Workboat-grade spray-applied non-skid coatings suit the deck.[12] Further resource pointers include the AbeBooks bookfinder service for an out-of-print title[13] and general boatbuilding sites, including a cedar-strip site and two discussion forums.[14] See [non-skid deck finish](/construction/non-skid-deck.md#truck-bed-and-industrial-coatings), [undersize side panels](/construction/undersize-side-panel-ply.md#aside-the-shrimpy--lugworm-books-and-a-working-book-finding-method) and [materials sourcing links](/references/materials-sourcing-links.md#general-boatbuilding-resource-links-cedar-strip-site-newsgroup-forum).
