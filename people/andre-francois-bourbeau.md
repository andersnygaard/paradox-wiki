# André-François Bourbeau

**André-François Bourbeau** is a retired professor of adventure tourism, wilderness survival and outdoor risk management at the University of Québec, and a longtime wilderness paddler and birchbark-canoe builder, based near **Lac Kénogami** in the Saguenay.[1] He came to sailing relatively late and already skilled: after two years' hesitation before he found the nerve to start, he bought his first **[Paradox](/paradox.md)**, "**[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)**," sight unseen from Stephen Yahn in January 2013, more than 5,000 km from his home in northern Québec, and added a second, "**[Pequeno](/boats/pequeno.md)**," in 2015.[2] He went on to design the **[Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md)**, a Puddle Duck Racer hull built with a Paradox-style cabin, and in 2021 scratch-built a twin-masted **[Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md)** ketch of his own design, "**Question**," with demountable beaching wheels and a rudder that rises clear when she grounds.[3] Fully bilingual, he has stood for years as a dependable bridge for francophone builders joining an English-language community, and the decades he spent teaching wilderness survival and travelling Québec's winters by canoe show up constantly in how he heats, provisions and rescues himself afloat.[4] He launches from the boat ramp beside the church at St-Cyriac, on Lac Kénogami.[5]

## Paradox and Gorfnik

He keeps two Paradoxes: **[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)** (later renamed **Iota**), bought from Stephen Yahn in January 2013, and, since 2015, **[Pequeno](/boats/pequeno.md)**, bought secondhand from Alan Ferris of Meaford, Ontario, which he lends out to friends so he always has company on the water.[6] The purchase and delivery of Ironsides, and a roller-furling arm Stephen Yahn had built that later failed outright in a hard Saguenay Fjord sail, are recorded in full at [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#the-purchase-and-a-cross-continent-shakedown-in-his-own-words); the round trip he later put at roughly 12,000 km, and the purchase price, revealed only in 2021, at $5,000, which he judged easily worth it.[7] That July, Stephen Yahn, whom he had met picking up the boat, died; from the archive Bourbeau could tell how many good ideas Yahn had brought to the community's discussions.[8] Pequeno's own refit, including closing up a non-standard open rear cabin found on the boat as bought, was finished over a winter and she was christened on the Saguenay river in June 2018; full account, including her permanent-ballast interior layout, at [boats/pequeno.md](/boats/pequeno.md#refit-completed-and-launched-june-2018).[9]

By 2021 he had six years' experience sailing both the Paradox and his own Gorfnik design, with a longest Paradox trip of five weeks and a longest Gorfnik trip of four weeks; he finds the Gorfniks see more use because they are smaller and easier to launch, and likes travelling with two or three of them loaded on the same trailer so friends can come along, but still rates the Paradox above the Gorfnik for its performance and its margin of safety.[10] Raced against each other, his two Paradoxes have proven evenly matched, sailing at exactly the same speed.[11] He offers his own free, 11-page Gorfnik plans to anyone who wants them, findable simply by searching "Gorfnik plans," and judges the design buildable in as little as three to four weekends.[12] In December 2016 a UK magazine invited him to contribute modification ideas and camping experience for a planned 2017 Paradox feature; he confirmed his front-only topping lift still worked "like a charm" a year on, and that Iota's steel-reinforced middle bulkhead did give a much wider inside sleeping area.[73] He traces much of his boatbuilding approach to years of wilderness canoe travel and building traditional birchbark and spruce-bark canoes, above all his insistence on knot-free, straight-grained wood for load-bearing members such as gunnels, black ash in his case, cut from adjacent sections of the same tree with one piece reversed, which let him hold a hull within an eighth of an inch of the centreline even without bulkheads, a habit of mind the next chapter follows onto the water.[13]

He has never fitted a motor to either Paradox: doing so would cost the boat its charm and the welcome that comes with arriving under sail or oar alone, and if he wanted the convenience of a motor he would simply sail a bigger boat instead. For friends who want the option, he keeps a separate 26 ft Norwalk Island Sharpie fitted with a motor as a social boat, reserved for passenger-friends on a schedule, with both Paradoxes kept engine-free.[14] He set out the case at length in 2021: a motor's stink, weight, line tangles, fuel danger and maintenance are exactly the burdens a small boat is meant to leave behind, and an electric motor only substitutes a different burden, battery weight and upkeep for poor performance; avoiding a motor was the main reason he chose the Paradox in the first place, with a properly practised yuloh judged the sail's true complement. A motor, in his view, will always fail exactly when it is needed most, and learning to do without one is the safer habit to build.[15] He endorses keeping a Paradox strictly for solo use, with a second, larger boat for when family wants to come along; sleeping two aboard, he judges, is workable but cramped next to a tent's open staging area outside.[16]

## Wilderness training, brought aboard

Decades of wilderness canoe travel and teaching outdoor survival show up constantly in how he manages a Paradox. On multi-day trips through Québec's frequent stretches of rain, he keeps the cabin dry by gluing a foam layer to the inside roof to cut condensation, stepping out only under an umbrella, opening the hatch about 18 inches to pass in and out without letting rain in, leaving wet footwear on the rear deck and going through a roll of shop towels a week.[17] Sailing into a Québec winter as ice began forming on the lakes, he described in some depth how each of his two boats is kept warm and how each combustion heater is kept safe: aboard the Paradox, a propane heater with two independent automatic shutoffs, one for stale air and one for the heater tipping over, a safety margin he judges priceless, though the hatch still has to stay open to regulate the heat; the smaller Gorfnik cabin he judges too small even for that heater, relying instead on a simple candle lantern carried over from winter wilderness tent-camping, which gives little heat but cuts humidity substantially. He also passed on a chimney-siting rule from the same tradition: the flue must extend above the cabin top for its draft to work, and a metal ring fitted where it crosses the cabin side, an old wall-tent term for the fitting is a "plate," keeps the surrounding wood from burning; plus a wider set of low-tech ways to stay warm carried over from two decades of winter wilderness travel, a bottle of boiling water at the feet, down booties, chemical toe warmers, a fur hat, wool layers and a large breathable wind shell worn over the life vest. His standing rule throughout: never go to sleep with any combustion device still lit.[18]

A rare Québec heatwave the following July put the same practical instinct to a different-weather test: sailing and camping in briefly extreme 40°C heat, he relied on a silver Birdiepal outdoor umbrella as a portable sunshade strong enough to survive any wind, doubling as rain cover so sailing could continue through a passing storm; repeated overboard dips, aided by the swim step, provided the only real relief. For the same trip's biting insects, he fits self-adhesive female velcro around the hatch and forward vent-hole openings to mate with male-velcro-edged insect netting, with a lengthwise zipper sewn into the hatch netting itself so it can be opened and closed without stripping the whole seam every time it rains; a small fan hung over the vent hole, needed only for a night's first couple of hours, is recharged the next day by a solar panel.[19]

He also described a tested self-rescue technique for climbing back aboard by a trailing line after going overboard: lying on the back while pulling on the line overhead keeps the face out of the water, favors lifting over diving and lets the stronger arm muscles work through straight wrists rather than bent ones, arrived at, in his words, by testing it a lot, on the reasoning that testing beats surprises in a real emergency.[20] As an outdoor professional and former canoe-poling instructor in rapids, he also adapted a canoe-poling technique to the yuloh for shallow water: shorter than a dedicated canoe pole and carrying a bend a canoe pole lacks, the yuloh still serves well as a push-pole, worked with the curve's convex face turned outward so it poles partly under the boat with the blade running parallel to the hull. The technique moves the boat upwind without the sail in shallow water and frees her from a grounding too, since poling backward works as well as forward.[21]

## Question, an Enigma ketch of his own design

As early as January 2021, some six weeks before construction began, he had already resolved to build an Enigma clone that winter, framing it explicitly as a faster alternative to his Gorfniks for reaching places with no boat ramp, and sought out Ontario's own Enigma owner, Tom Pike, hoping to compare notes.[22] Confirming with a fellow owner that no official Enigma plans existed, he was pointed instead to the informal notes and photographs on Matt Layden's own website, the same resource already documented at [matt-layden/enigma.md](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-external-construction-photo-link), and reported he had already made a test model that "looks right."[23] In March 2021 he began building, without any plans, a boat based on the Enigma hull but reworked with ideas drawn from years of Paradox and Gorfnik trips, describing the project at the time as an Enigma/Gorfnik combination meant to combine the best of both boats.[24] Laying out the partitions took real thought: he wanted to slide straight into sleeping position, as on the Paradox and Gorfnik, without first emptying the rear storage locker, to have room for his folding Brompton bicycle and to be able to step the mast from inside the cabin, all of which pushed him to relocate the mast and rework the sail plan and the boat's balance. Other ideas carried over from years of Paradox and Gorfnik trips went into the cabin layout: an accessible anchor/fish/muddy-boots locker with drain holes on the starboard side, under the folding hatch so the hatch itself doubles as a cooking platform; the rear deck lowered three inches and sloped aft to drain water, giving a flat storage shelf with sides that also serves as a sheltered second cooking counter, eases reboarding from the swim step and keeps the tiller and steering lines above deck rather than through a transom hole that would need waterproofing and mosquito-proofing; a 24-by-1-inch foredeck aeration gap matching the Gorfnik's own screened rear window.[76] Working out the cabin roof and windows by trial and error, since he had no plan, he settled on a 17-degree rake for the front window, 6 degrees on the side windows and a 1.5-degree slope from back to front so rainwater runs off.[25]

By 19 April 2021 the build stood three-quarters complete, from Saguenay, some 200 km north of Québec City; expecting to know within the month whether the boat turned out as planned, he set that outcome as the trigger for selling one of his two Paradoxes.[26] He launched the finished boat at the Lac Kénogami ramp beside the St-Cyriac church on 18 July 2021, with a champagne christening.[27] Asked on the spot what he had named her, he had not yet settled on anything and answered that it was a good question, so he named her **Question**, which he reckoned to be the fourth Enigma launched.[28] Weighing in that September on a build-or-buy discussion, he gave the finished build's own totals: four and a half months at an average of four to five hours a day, without any plans to follow, "and there was a lot of thinking time"; materials came to over $3,000, not counting the trailer. His verdict for a newcomer weighing the same choice: buying beats building "by far, at any price, if you can get your hands on one."[29] Looking back on the build in January 2023, he recalled it as a pastime taken up during the COVID pandemic, and by then had sailed Question through two full seasons.[30]

## Build technique

Some of his technique carries over from boat to boat. While the side panels are still flat and easy to reach, he draws the waterline on them, trivial while flat, in his view, and a real headache once the sides are curved up, and glues an extra eighth-inch layer of plywood into the front four feet of each side panel, where impacts are most likely.[32] For ballast, he casts his own removable lead bricks: he collects old wheel weights, melts them over a propane camp stove (he found butane does not run hot enough), skims the floating iron clips off with a slotted spoon and pours the molten lead into a mould while wearing heavy gloves, since the lead sets faster than the wooden mould can burn, then weighs the finished bricks against his own calculation from lead's density, coming out to a bit more than 60 lb per side. He pads the bricks with Ensolite foam so they cannot bang around and add a little flotation, and totals a boat's ballast at roughly 30 kg of lead per side, plus a battery pack on one side, a spare anchor on the other and water jugs on each side.[33]

He frames storage capacity as a question of expectations: packed like a lightweight backpacker or a portaging canoe camper, a Paradox has generous storage; packed like a household, it runs out fast.[34] To help a Paradox come about cleanly in strong wind, he keeps two gallons of water and a heavy anchor in the far-forward locker, a trick that works up to about 25 knots, above which he simply circles the boat around the other way instead.[77] Reaching or beating, the same forward weight lets either of his Paradoxes sail long tacks with the ropes untouched entirely, and running, he learned to reduce sail rather than fight it, after which the boat holds the same speed downwind under far less canvas and digs in less at the bow.[35] Because the Paradox's mast sits off-centre, he also balances uneven steering between tacks by shifting the halyard's position on the yard rather than moving the mast itself.[36]

Answering a fellow owner's question on protecting the rudder's leading edge, he reported having tried rope, thick fiberglass and split copper pipe before settling on the best fix found: a webbing strap wetted out in epoxy and folded lengthwise around the edge, held in place with masking tape until cured, the tape then sanded away, "a great depth finder."[37] Answering a question about a Paradox's payload weight, he clarified that the widely quoted 1,410 lb design-displacement figure is the boat's finished weight fully loaded with water and lead ballast, not her lightship weight, and that because he does not reduce the lead ballast to compensate for stores taken aboard, his own long-distance cruising weight usually comes to around 1,600 lb.[38] Answering a dissenting theory that credited most of a Paradox's lateral resistance to the rudder rather than the chine runners, he restated his own firsthand conviction the other way: the runners have little effect sailed flat in light air, but once the boat is leaned hard they dig in and act "as a long keel right underneath the boat," making "a WORLD of difference" to pointing, "yes the boat will sail without the chine runners, but it won't point nearly as high. Believe in the design! The harder I sail the boat, the more I believe."[39]

The topping lift he settled on early has stayed settled: a front-only "half topping lift" that eases lowering the sail without the snarling risk of a full topping lift, a risk he traces to a real drawback of his own former rig, the sail getting hung up while flying low exactly when the wind is strongest, and one he recalls **Matt Layden**, asked once about a topping lift, simply answering that he did not think it a good idea.[40] Explained again in 2017 to a different owner puzzling over furling friction: an insufficiently tightened rope inside the furling drum's brass pipe leaves it sitting at an awkward angle that keeps the furler from rolling cleanly. Pushed back a day later on overstating the difficulty, he clarified that the gear as designed works fine for a strong young man, and it is declining strength with age, not any fault in the mechanism, that makes it feel hard; furling from full down to about a third and back up again is easy, since the sail's full weight is not yet on the roller, and it is only the last third that grows heavy for a weaker sailor.[41] Answering a question about the rope-steering loop's mechanics, he set out the underlying geometry directly: pulling front to back on the starboard rope turns the boat right, as if pulling the bow that way, while the port rope simultaneously runs back to front, pulling the stern around for the same turn.[42] He recommends hanging every tool on a wheeled cart kept beside the boat, for instant access from whichever side or end is being worked on, rather than repeatedly walking to a fixed bench.[43]

His own verdict on cabin width is the residue of a friend's experiment. **[Michael Beebe](/people/michael-beebe.md)** widened his Paradox's side decks out to the sheer line for easier sitting, then found it cost him the decks' use for stowing the yuloh and mast, and eventually sold that boat and built a second one to the plans instead; Bourbeau's own conclusion, watching the whole arc, is that a genuinely good reason is needed before departing from the designed cabin width.[44] In January 2018 he passed on a cam-cleat orientation fix credited to Beebe: facing the halyard and furling-line cleats upward so the pull runs horizontal and straight to the rear, rather than downward, lets both be cleated and uncleated without leaving the seat, easing reefing considerably.[45] He gives the same general reasoning for bolts over screws through a hull: water finding its way past a fastener eventually is almost impossible to prevent entirely however well maintained, but a bolt lets it drip down visibly inside the boat, an early warning, easy to fix before the wood rots, where a screw traps the same water invisibly until rot forces the whole piece to be replaced, and a screw, unlike a bolt, can also let go entirely at the worst moment, "I have learned to use bolts, as Matt probably did long ago too."[46] Across furling, seating and yuloh questions over many years he has stayed a reliable, repeat answerer, passing on fixes worked out on his own two boats to newcomers as they arrive.[47] In his own experience the Paradox windows are perfect, and when beaching he usually carries the yuloh along too, using it to pole-vault off the front onto dry land and back aboard again without getting his feet wet.[48]

## Paradox, Enigma, and Gorfnik compared

He put Question through a deliberate capsize test soon after her first sail, and sailed the Paradox and the Enigma together at 13 knots to compare their sail-carrying power directly; full record, following the same page's established comparison pattern, at [matt-layden/enigma.md - a 2021 capsize test, and a 13-knot comparison](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2021-capsize-test-and-a-13-knot-comparison-against-the-paradox).[49] After several multi-day sails and two camping trips in the Enigma, one in light air, one in a puffy 20 knots, sailed alongside a friend's Gorfnik, he judged the Enigma tender at first but stiffening as she heels, forgiving enough at 20 to 25 degrees that he was comfortable sailing her there; downwind the lighter Gorfnik keeps pace, but upwind the Enigma's pointing and hull shape leave the Gorfnik well behind. For camping he found the Gorfnik roomier and so stable that he could lean out and wash his hands while cooking, where the Enigma is stable enough to cook aboard but demands care not to move too fast; on safety, he rated the Gorfnik's nil secondary stability a real limit, leaned too far, or caught by a hard puff without releasing the sheet, she can flip instantly and without warning, against the Enigma, which he found would right herself from at least 50 degrees of heel, giving him time to react. Paddling the Enigma disappointed him a little, making only about 2 knots against the Gorfnik's 1.75, and he thought the Enigma's tenderness made her twitchier and harder to hold straight. His summary verdict was that the Gorfnik amounts to a square live-aboard canoe and the Enigma to more of a sailboat, while the Paradox remained, for him, the fastest and most relaxing of the three. The ketch rig itself left him unconvinced: once he had experienced the Paradox's infinite roller reefing, tying off reefing lines and shifting sail position between two masts felt a step backward, especially since the long chine runners make the boat comparatively insensitive to sail position anyway, and he considered reverting to a single lug sail with roller reefing.[50]

Still sailing both boats side by side, alone and with friends, four years on, he restated the comparison in 2025: the Enigma remains the tenderer, more demanding boat, where the Paradox stays faster and more relaxing on every point of sail; her 180 lb of removable lead ballast, near the chines, still rights her from a knockdown by body movement alone. He rates the Enigma's own advantages as practical rather than nautical, launching without rigging in a minute or two against some fifteen minutes to rig the Paradox, and overland portage on her own wheels once the lead is out, an option the Paradox lacks entirely, worth more to him now that stepping the mast has grown harder with age. Full comparison: [matt-layden/enigma.md - a 2025 comparison](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2025-comparison-an-owners-enigma-and-paradox-sailed-side-by-side).[51] Answering a separate correspondent building an Enigma from photographs alone the next day, he pointed him to his own public Facebook page (searchable as "Paradox Sailboat") for the full construction record, and restated the build-time figure once more, four and a half months, at least three hours a day, six days a week. Full exchange: [matt-layden/enigma.md - a 2025 continuation](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2025-continuation-reverse-engineering-from-photographs-and-the-george-van-sickle-blog-reconfirmed-live).[52]

## Cruises across the continent

Ahead of a planned cross-continent drive from Québec to Washington state and back in 2013, he canvassed for other Paradoxes' locations in North America, hoping to sail alongside their owners and compare notes; being retired, he judged he could visit almost anywhere over the following year, and pledged to be "a nice guy and especially very respectful of your privacy." Replies confirmed **[Darren](/people/darren-victoria.md)**'s build then under way in Victoria, BC, and **[David Beard](/people/david-beard.md)**'s "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" in north Mississippi.[53][74][75] Watching Sean Mulligan's video of "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)"'s second shakedown sail, he recognized the sailing spot from a Christmas visit to Lake Havasu the year before, and called Mulligan "one hell of a sailor."[54] That October he passed on the fix he had worked out on his own Ironsides for a yuloh popping out of its pivot socket, deepening the socket and gluing in a length of hose, to Scout's owner ahead of a challenge sail; full technique and exchange at [components/yuloh.md](/components/yuloh.md#ball-joint-versus-oarlock-and-pivot-socket-wear-over-time) and [boats/scout.md](/boats/scout.md#the-bosunator-challenge-a-recurring-yuloh-socket-problem-and-a-fourth-shakedown-video).[55] That December he made the round trip a second time to collect Ironsides, heading south from Washington in search of warmer weather.[56] The following January, after a run of weather delays, a family emergency and two broken ribs, he finally put her back in the water at Lake Amistad, Texas, sailing through a bad topping-lift/furling tangle to a safe beaching before finding the fix and sailing a successful second day.[57] Driving on toward the Gulf coast, he met a fellow Paradox owner, **[Michael Beebe](/people/michael-beebe.md)**, who quickly became a good friend; sailing alongside him there for six days of cold rain taught him a great deal and built his confidence.[58]

In 2015 he cruised Ironsides for three weeks on Florida's St-Johns River, judged worth the 6,500 km trailer haul from northern Québec, building himself a canoe-style seat that solved a long-standing sore-bum problem.[59] That March he restated the boat's cross-country purchase and delivery, and reported a roller-furling arm Stephen Yahn had built failing outright in a hard Saguenay Fjord sail; full record at [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#the-purchase-and-a-cross-continent-shakedown-in-his-own-words) and [boats/ironsides.md - a roller-furler failure on the Saguenay Fjord](/boats/ironsides.md#a-roller-furler-failure-on-the-saguenay-fjord-2015).[60]

Asked in November 2016 whether he had thought about America's inland-waterway "Great Loop" circuit, he answered that he was still considering it: the boat could certainly handle the trip, but his concerns lay elsewhere, a long time to be alone, and better places to go sailing than the Mississippi. Days later, having weighed it further, he came down against it: the only real reason to attempt the loop would be bragging rights, and at his age he no longer needed those, preferring instead to spend the same time and energy on North America's best cruising grounds, Florida's St-John's River, Ontario's Thousand Islands Park, and the Saguenay Fjord among them, while allowing there is something to be said for the adventure and the "torture" of completing a genuine challenge: "suffering is healthy, in some strange way. As a survival expert, I know."[61]

While Pequeno sat in the shop that January awaiting a full interior refit, he set out that March with Iota on a five-week loop cruise around North Carolina's Albemarle Sound, into freezing weather rather than the heat a fellow owner was then sailing in on the other side of the world.[62] He departed with a bum left shoulder from a hockey injury picked up just before leaving Canada, expecting to sail one-armed for the first few days but still able to use the yuloh, and Iota had never yet shipped a drop of water under his ownership.[63] At 64, having just completed the five-week cruise, he sailed the first three weeks using only one hand, alternating releasing the furling line about six inches and cleating it, then raising the sail about six inches and cleating that, repeated until the sail reached the top, and reversed to lower.[64] Looking back on the loop that December, he judged it proof Paradox "really hits the sweet spot in terms of size vs performance in a non-motorized vessel"; full route at [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#a-planned-2017-albemarle-sound-cruise-and-its-march-start).[65]

By January 2021 he had also sailed a Paradox in Ontario waters, and reported completing, with a friend, a six-day circumnavigation of Lac St-Jean, the 250 km trip first set as Pequeno's planned inaugural outing back in 2018, while weighing a further circumnavigation of Lake Huron's Manitoulin Island and weeks on Nova Scotia's Bras d'Or Lake.[66] That February, answering a newcomer's question about his own experience on the St-Lawrence river, he warned that it demands real homework for its major currents, powerful tides, shoals and heavy commercial traffic, from firsthand experience: standing in calm, knee-deep water off Île d'Orléans while boarding a Gorfnik, he was hit from behind without warning by a five-foot wave thrown up by a tanker passing on the river's far side toward Québec City, "lesson learned." He separately recalled an earlier promotional stunt, his own idea: three friends floated down the St-Lawrence between Trois-Rivières and Québec City on a large piece of ice, drawing enough disruption to commercial traffic to make national headlines.[67]

## A francophone bridge on an English-language list

In December 2017 he replied to a new francophone builder's self-introduction and confirmed being Québécois and fully bilingual.[68] Sylvain Caro, replying in turn, thanked him for the welcome, said he had just bought the stock plans and Don Elliott's building manual but had not yet started construction and asked whether Bourbeau had built his own boat from scratch.[78] A few weeks later, on the same still-open exchange, he went further, arguing publicly that love of the Paradox overcomes any language barrier and that it would be too bad to leave out a potential builder over language alone; he offered to translate for anyone who needed it, and proposed that any non-English exchange move to private correspondence if an English-speaking member objected, closing with a sign-off in English, French and Spanish.[69] A third party's own words corroborate the effect: of everyone who had been asked, Bourbeau was the only francophone who actually answered.[70] Answering the same correspondent's questions about the design's real-sea handling that January, he gave a firsthand verdict drawn from his own two boats: he has personally sailed in waves of a metre and a half in 25-knot winds and judges the boat capable of taking considerably more than that; he cannot imagine a better boat without an engine; her windward pointing does not match a performance sloop but "holds her own"; and roller-furling needs careful, precise adjustment to the plan, though it gives him no trouble.[71] A New York-based owner restoring a secondhand Paradox introduced himself on the same exchange via machine translation soon after, recalling a visit to Québec, touring Bourbeau's Paradoxes and sailing the smaller boats, and hoping the two could sail together that summer, each in his own boat; see **[people/frederic-tallman.md](/people/frederic-tallman.md)** for his own fuller story.[72]
