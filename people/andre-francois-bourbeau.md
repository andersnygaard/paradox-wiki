# André-François Bourbeau

**André-François Bourbeau** is a retired professor of adventure tourism, wilderness survival and outdoor risk management, and a longtime wilderness paddler and birchbark-canoe builder, based near Lac Kénogami in the Saguenay region of Québec, Canada.[1] He came to sailing relatively late and already skilled at building: after two years' hesitation, he bought his first **[Paradox](/paradox.md)**, "**[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)**" (later renamed Iota), sight unseen from Stephen Yahn in January 2013, more than 5,000 km from home, and added a second, "**[Pequeno](/boats/pequeno.md)**," bought secondhand from Alan Ferris of Meaford, Ontario, in 2015.[1][2] He designed the **[Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md)**, a Puddle Duck Racer hull built with a Paradox-style cabin and offered free online, and in 2021 scratch-built a twin-masted **[Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md)** ketch of his own design, "**Question**," with demountable beaching wheels and a rudder that rises clear when she grounds.[3][4] Fully bilingual, he has stood for years as a bridge for francophone builders joining an English-language community, and decades spent teaching wilderness survival and travelling Québec's winters by canoe show up constantly in how he heats, provisions and rescues himself afloat.[5] Full build accounts: [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md) and [boats/pequeno.md](/boats/pequeno.md).

## Paradox and Gorfnik

He keeps two Paradoxes, sailed from the Saguenay: Ironsides, later renamed Iota, and Pequeno, which he relaunched in June 2018 after a winter spent closing up a non-standard open rear cabin found on her hull as bought.[6] By 2021 he had six years' experience sailing both the Paradox and his own Gorfnik design, with a longest Paradox trip of five weeks and a longest Gorfnik trip of four weeks; the Gorfniks see more use because they are smaller and easier to launch, though the Paradox remains, in his judgment, the better boat for performance and its margin of safety.[3] He offers his free, eleven-page Gorfnik plans to anyone who wants them, findable simply by searching "Gorfnik plans," and judges the design buildable in as little as three to four weekends.[7] Over the winter of 2015 and 2016 he crossed the Everglades solo in his eight-foot Gorfnik, provisioned for two weeks without trouble, finding the Paradox roomy by comparison.[8]

He has never fitted a motor to either boat: doing so would cost the boat its charm and the welcome that comes with arriving under sail or oar alone, and a properly practised yuloh is, in his view, the sail's true complement.[9] For friends who want the convenience, he keeps a separate motorised boat reserved for passenger outings, leaving both Paradoxes engine-free.[9] A motor, he holds, will always fail exactly when it is needed most, and learning to do without one is, to him, the safer habit to build.[9]

## Wilderness training, brought aboard

Decades of wilderness canoe travel and teaching outdoor survival show up constantly in how he manages a boat. On multi-day trips through Québec's frequent rain he keeps the cabin dry with a foam lining against condensation and a careful hatch routine, and heats the Paradox with a propane heater fitted with two independent automatic shutoffs, a safety margin he judges priceless, while the smaller Gorfnik cabin relies instead on a simple candle lantern carried over from winter tent camping.[10] A rare Québec heatwave tested the same instinct the other way: an outdoor umbrella doubled as sunshade and rain cover, and a home-made velcro-and-netting system kept biting insects out of the hatch and vents.[11] Through repeated testing he also worked out a technique for climbing back aboard by a trailing line after going overboard, and, drawing on his years as a canoe-poling instructor, adapted a poling technique to work the yuloh as a push-pole in shallow water, moving the boat upwind without the sail and freeing her from a grounding.[12][13] Full technique: [operations/cabin-heating.md](/operations/cabin-heating.md), [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md) and [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md).

## Question, an Enigma ketch of his own design

In January 2021 he resolved to build an Enigma of his own that winter, as a lighter, faster alternative to his Gorfniks for reaching places with no boat ramp, and sought out Ontario's own Enigma owner, Tom Pike.[14] With no official Enigma plans in existence, he worked from Matt Layden's own informal notes and photographs, reworking the boat with ideas carried over from years of Paradox and Gorfnik trips: an accessible bow locker, a lowered and sloped rear deck doubling as a cooking counter and a mast moved to leave the rear storage locker free for sleeping.[15] He launched her on Lac Kénogami in July 2021 with a champagne christening.[16] Asked on the spot what he had named her, he answered that it was a good question, so he named her Question, which he reckoned to be the fourth Enigma launched.[17] The finished build took four and a half months at four to five hours a day, without plans to follow, for a materials cost of over $3,000; weighing the choice for a newcomer, his verdict was that "buying is by far the best option, at any price, if you can get your hands on one."[18] Her beaching wheels, balanced rudder, folding hatch and twin raked masts were all worked out from scratch, reasoning that a design good enough for Matt Layden was good enough for him.[4] Full building story and scantlings: [matt-layden/enigma.md](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2021-owner-build-question-an-enigma-derived-ketch).

## Build technique

Some of his practice carries over from boat to boat. He casts his own removable lead ballast bricks from scavenged wheel weights, melted over a stove and skimmed of their floating steel clips before pouring; full method: [construction/casting-lead-ballast.md](/construction/casting-lead-ballast.md).[19] He favours bolts over screws through the hull: a bolt lets water that finds its way past a fastener drip down visibly inside the boat, an early warning fixed before the wood rots, where a screw traps the same water invisibly until rot forces the whole piece to be replaced.[20] Full fastening account: [components/hatch.md](/components/hatch.md#the-outer-rails-carriage-bolts).

## Paradox, Enigma and Gorfnik compared

After several multi-day sails and camping trips in the Enigma alongside a friend's Gorfnik, he judged the Gorfnik a square live-aboard canoe and the Enigma more of a sailboat, with the Paradox remaining, for him, the fastest and most relaxing of the three on every point of sail.[21] Four years on, still sailing both his own boats side by side, he restated the comparison: the Enigma remains the tenderer, more demanding boat, where the Paradox stays faster and more relaxing; the Enigma's advantages are practical rather than nautical, launching without rigging in a minute or two and portaging overland on her own wheels once her lead ballast is out, an option the Paradox lacks entirely.[22] Full comparison: [matt-layden/enigma.md](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2025-comparison-an-owners-enigma-and-paradox-sailed-side-by-side).

## Cruises across the continent

Collecting Ironsides that spring meant travelling from Québec to Stephen Yahn's home near Seattle, at a purchase price, revealed only years later, of $5,000, for a round trip of roughly 12,000 km that he judged easily worth it.[18] He found the original trailer cracked in half once the boat was off it, a yuloh that kept slipping its pivot socket and a sail yard built backward with its heavy end on the wrong side; each was fixed on the spot before a first sail on Washington's Black Lake, and a returning light breeze carried him back under full sail after a missed tack and a windless stretch spent waiting out a calm among the local turtles.[23] Yahn, whom he had met picking up the boat, died in July 2013.[24]

Returning to Washington that December to bring the boat south a second time, he broke two ribs in a hiking fall the day before reaching Lake Amistad, Texas, delaying the launch by two weeks; a gusty, reversing wind then fouled his halyard, topping lift and sheet while reefing on the first attempt, but a fix found overnight gave him a clean second day out.[25] Continuing along the Gulf coast, he met a fellow Paradox owner, Michael Beebe, who became a lasting friend after they sailed six days of cold rain together.[26] He cruised her for three weeks on Florida's St-Johns River in 2015, judged worth the 6,500 km trailer haul from northern Québec.[27] A five-week loop cruise around North Carolina's Albemarle Sound followed in 2017, which he judged proof the Paradox suits size to performance better than any other engineless boat he knew; that same winter a British sailing magazine had invited him to contribute modification ideas and camping notes from his own cruising to a planned Paradox feature.[28][29] Weighing America's Great Loop circuit the year before, he had decided against it, preferring to spend his time on cruising grounds he already knew: "suffering is healthy, in some strange way. As a survival expert, I know."[30] In 2021 he added a six-day circumnavigation of Lac St-Jean with a friend, and has sailed Québec's Thousand Islands and the Saguenay Fjord as well.[14]

## A bridge for francophone builders

Bilingual and Québécois, he offers to translate for francophone builders joining the mostly English-language community, judging that a shared love of the Paradox outweighs the language barrier; he made the offer as early as December 2017, when Sylvain Caro joined in French.[5][31] A New York-based owner restoring a secondhand Paradox visited his home waters, touring both his Paradoxes and sailing the smaller boats with him; see [people/frederic-tallman.md](/people/frederic-tallman.md) for his own fuller story.[32]
