# André-François Bourbeau

**André-François Bourbeau** is a boatbuilder and sailor based near **Lac Kénogami** in Québec's Saguenay region, who came to sailing already an experienced wilderness paddler and a builder of traditional birchbark and spruce-bark canoes, and who owns two **[Paradoxes](/paradox.md)**, designed the **[Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md)**, and in 2021 scratch-built a twin-masted **[Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md)** ketch of his own design.[1][2] He launches from the boat ramp beside the church at **St-Cyriac**, on Lac Kénogami, and his builds are documented in detail, post by post, in the **[Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md)**.[2]

## Paradox and Gorfnik

He keeps two Paradoxes — **[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)** (later renamed **Iota**), bought from Stephen Yahn in 2013, and, since 2015, **[Pequeno](/boats/pequeno.md)**, bought secondhand from Alan Ferris of Meaford, Ontario — lending the second one out to friends specifically so he always has company on the water, and by 2021 had six years' experience sailing both the Paradox and his own Gorfnik design, with a longest Paradox trip of five weeks and a longest Gorfnik trip of four weeks.[1][42] By January 2021 he had also sailed a Paradox in Ontario waters, and reported completing, with a friend, a six-day circumnavigation of Lac St-Jean — the 250 km trip first set as Pequeno's planned inaugural outing back in 2018 — while weighing a further circumnavigation of Lake Huron's Manitoulin Island and weeks on Nova Scotia's Bras d'Or Lake; the loaned-out second boat, then seeing less use than hoped, was under consideration for sale, though nothing was decided.[83] He finds the Gorfniks see more use because they are smaller and easier to launch, and he likes travelling with two or three of them loaded on the same trailer so friends can come along, but he still rates the Paradox above the Gorfnik for its performance and its margin of safety.[1] Raced against each other, his two Paradoxes have proven evenly matched, sailing at exactly the same speed.[80] He traces much of his boatbuilding approach to years of wilderness canoe travel and building traditional birchbark and spruce-bark canoes, above all his insistence on knot-free, straight-grained wood for load-bearing members such as gunnels — black ash, in his case, cut from adjacent sections of the same tree with one piece reversed, which let him hold a hull within an eighth of an inch of the centreline even without bulkheads.[3]

## Question — an Enigma ketch of his own design

As early as January 2021 — some six weeks before construction began — he had already resolved to build an Enigma clone that winter, framing it explicitly as a faster alternative to his Gorfniks for reaching places with no boat ramp, and sought out Ontario's own Enigma owner, Tom Pike, hoping to compare notes.[83] Confirming with a fellow owner that no official Enigma plans existed, pointed instead to the informal notes and photographs on Matt Layden's own website — the same page already documented at [matt-layden/enigma.md — the external construction-photo link](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-external-construction-photo-link) — as the resource he meant to build from, reporting he had already made a test model that "looks right."[84] In March 2021 he began building, without any plans, a boat based on the Enigma hull but reworked with ideas drawn from years of Paradox and Gorfnik trips, describing the project at the time as an Enigma/Gorfnik combination meant to combine the best features of both boats.[1][4] Laying out the partitions took real thought: he wanted to slide straight into sleeping position, as on the Paradox and Gorfnik, without first emptying the rear storage locker, to have room for his folding Brompton bicycle, and to be able to step the mast from inside the cabin, all of which pushed him to relocate the mast and rework the sail plan and the boat's balance.[4] He carried several trip-tested ideas over from the Paradox and Gorfnik: an accessible anchor, fishing-gear, and muddy-boots locker on the starboard side (his bike lives to port) with drain holes, sitting under the folding hatch so it doubles as a cooking platform; a rear deck lowered three inches and sloped aft to shed water, which also gives him a flat, railed spot for gear or a wind-sheltered second cooking counter, an easier step up from the swim ladder, and lets him keep the tiller and steering lines above deck rather than running them through a hole in the transom; and a long, narrow aeration gap between the flat and rounded parts of the foredeck, matching the screened rear window that had already proven itself on the Gorfnik.[5] Working out the cabin roof and windows by trial and error, since he had no plan, he settled on a 17-degree rake for the front window, 6 degrees on the side windows, and a 1.5-degree slope from back to front so rainwater runs off.[6] By **19 April 2021** the build stood **three-quarters complete**, from **Saguenay**, some 200 km north of Québec City; expecting to know within the month whether the boat turned out as planned, he set that outcome as the trigger for selling one of his two Paradoxes.[87] He launched the finished boat at the Lac Kénogami ramp beside the St-Cyriac church on 18 July 2021, with a champagne christening.[2] Asked on the spot what he had named her, he had not yet settled on anything and answered that it was a good question — so he named her **Question**, which he reckoned to be the fourth Enigma launched.[7] Weighing in that September on a build-or-buy discussion, gave the finished build's own totals for the first time: **4 1/2 months at an average of 4-5 hours a day**, without any plans to follow, "and there was a lot of thinking time"; materials came to **over $3,000**, not counting the trailer. By comparison, even with several boats already built, still expected a Paradox built from scratch would take him **at least six months**; a Paradox's interior alone, refitted rather than built new, had once taken him about a month. His verdict for a newcomer weighing the same choice: buying beats building "by far... at any price, if you can get your hands on one."[88] Looking back on the build in January 2023, he recalled it as a pastime taken up during the COVID pandemic, and by then had sailed Question through two full seasons.[89]

### Beaching wheels, rudder, hatch, and rig

Question carries integrated, demountable beaching wheels: half-inch axles run through standard copper pipe let into the chine wings, set at roughly 5 degrees of positive camber, an angle he arrived at by building a temporary test buggy that also served to move the boat around on land.[8] Because the axle holes sit underwater, he sealed the inner pipe ends with epoxy against infiltration and thickened the three-quarter-inch wings with added ash strips to support the pipe; unsure whether aluminium would resist salt corrosion any better and unable to find stainless pipe, he used ordinary copper left over from building his house, unworried after keeping an earlier Gorfnik in salt water for two full seasons without a sign of corrosion.[9] He also glued in wooden support blocks, drilled to the exact depth needed, before epoxying the pipes permanently into place and fibreglassing the chines, which left each axle supported along five and three-quarter inches of pipe rather than the two and a half inches the wing thickness alone would give.[10] The rudder is a balanced design, worked out from scratch with cardboard mock-ups since he had no plan to follow, with roughly 30 percent of the blade forward of the pivot to lighten the steering; he built the rudder stock and swim step from solid ash and the blade from hardwood plywood, into which he had already cast lead while pouring his ballast bricks, reasoning that a design good enough for **[Matt Layden](/matt-layden/index.md)** was good enough for him.[11] He glued an eighth-inch skin of hardwood plywood to each face of the three-quarter-inch blade to build up a wing-shaped cross-section, checked the 30 percent balance against a one-inch-gridded photo before drilling the pivot hole, and set the pull-up rope hole as high as possible to keep it from vibrating.[12] The rudder stock pivots on a removable aluminium tube riding on Teflon washers rather than conventional pintles, a system he prefers because it lets the whole rudder assembly rise when it touches bottom or the boat is beached, so the hull takes the load rather than the eyebolts; a small wood stop keeps the rudder from swinging forward of vertical.[13] The support piece beneath it, which doubles as a swim step, is shaped like an airfoil section underwater and built strong enough to take the boat's weight when she is hauled up a beach.[14] The folding, rimmed hatch that doubles as a cooking platform is sealed watertight with a bicycle inner tube.[15] The twin aluminium masts are raked about four inches aft, a figure he settled on after testing how much the aluminium bent under sail tension and consulting his sailmaker friend Benoit Tremblay of Concept Air, who made the sails.[16]

## Build technique

Some of his technique carries over from boat to boat. While the side panels are still flat and easy to reach, he draws the waterline on them — trivial while flat, in his view, and a real headache once the sides are curved up — and glues an extra eighth-inch layer of plywood into the front four feet of each side panel, where impacts are most likely.[17] For ballast, he casts his own removable lead bricks: he collects old wheel weights, melts them over a propane camp stove (he found butane does not run hot enough), skims the floating iron clips off with a slotted spoon, and pours the molten lead into a mould while wearing heavy gloves, since the lead sets faster than the wooden mould can burn.[18] The bricks came out to a bit over 60 lb per side on Question, set into the bottom of the two side storage bins.[19] He padded the bricks with Ensolite foam so they cannot bang around and add a little flotation, and totalled the boat's ballast at roughly 30 kg (66 lb) of lead per side, plus a battery pack on one side, a spare anchor on the other, and water jugs on each side.[20] Asked for pre-build tips by a newcomer starting his own Paradox, he recommended hanging every tool on a wheeled cart kept beside the boat, for instant access from whichever side or end is being worked on, rather than repeatedly walking to a fixed bench.[39]

## Paradox, Enigma, and Gorfnik compared

He put Question through a deliberate capsize test soon after her first sail: he could not tip her sitting down, but standing and hiking out he laid her over until water briefly crossed the cabin window and still recovered; hiking out further, he fell out of the boat entirely and she flipped back upright on her own, and he climbed back aboard on the swim step and bailed her out with a bucket. He was clear that the Enigma, much smaller than the Paradox, does not share the Paradox's extreme, hard-to-capsize stability — on a Paradox, he notes, you can hang your whole weight out on the mast without coming anywhere close to going over.[21] Sailing the two boats together in 13 knots of wind, with an experienced racing friend aboard the Enigma and himself on the Paradox, he found the heavier, more stable Paradox could carry its full 130 square feet of sail while the more tender Enigma, leaned past 45 degrees, topped out around 66 square feet; the Enigma pointed at least as high as the Paradox, but the Paradox was faster, and sailing it at 13 knots felt relaxed enough that leaning the wrong way barely mattered, where the Enigma demanded constant attention.[22] After several multi-day sails and two camping trips in the Enigma — one in light air, one in a puffy 20 knots — sailed alongside a friend's Gorfnik, he judged the Enigma tender at first but stiffening as she heels, forgiving enough at 20 to 25 degrees that he was comfortable sailing her there; downwind the lighter Gorfnik keeps pace, but upwind the Enigma's pointing and hull shape leave the Gorfnik well behind. For camping he found the Gorfnik roomier and so stable that he could lean out and wash his hands while cooking, where the Enigma is stable enough to cook aboard but demands care not to move too fast; on safety, he rated the Gorfnik's nil secondary stability a real limit — leaned too far, or caught by a hard puff without releasing the sheet, she can flip instantly and without warning — against the Enigma, which he found would right herself from at least 50 degrees of heel, giving him time to react, and which he reckoned would spill him out before she filled with water; for beaching he preferred the Gorfnik, though the Enigma managed fine too. Paddling the Enigma disappointed him a little, making only about 2 knots at a normal pace against the Gorfnik's 1.75, a small enough margin that he found the Gorfnik's paddling position — leaning back against the cabin — more comfortable, and thought the Enigma's tenderness made her twitchier and harder to hold straight. His summary verdict was that the Gorfnik amounts to a square live-aboard canoe and the Enigma to more of a sailboat, while the Paradox remained, for him, the fastest and most relaxing of the three. The ketch rig itself left him unconvinced: once he had experienced the Paradox's infinite roller reefing, he found tying off reefing lines and shifting sail position between two masts a step backward, especially since the long chine runners make the boat comparatively insensitive to sail position anyway; the main reason he had chosen a ketch, freeing the cabin of an intrusive single mast, no longer felt necessary once the boat was built, and he considered reverting to a single lug sail with roller reefing.[23]

Still sailing both boats side by side, alone and with friends, four years on, he restated the comparison in 2025: the Enigma remains the tenderer, more demanding boat of the two, leaning away under him the moment he reaches over her side for something and needing more attention under sail generally, where the Paradox stays faster and more relaxing on every point of sail; her 180 lb of removable lead ballast, near the chines, still rights her from a knockdown by body movement alone. He rates the Enigma's own advantages as practical rather than nautical — launching without rigging in a minute or two against some fifteen minutes to rig the Paradox, and overland portage on her own wheels once the lead is out, an option the Paradox lacks entirely — worth more to him now that climbing aboard the Paradox to step the mast has grown harder with age. Full comparison: [matt-layden/enigma.md — a 2025 comparison](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2025-comparison-an-owners-enigma-and-paradox-sailed-side-by-side).[90] Answering a separate correspondent building an Enigma from photographs alone, the next day, he pointed him to his own public Facebook page (searchable as "Paradox Sailboat") for the full construction record, and restated the build-time figure once more — four and a half months, now specified as at least three hours a day, six days a week. Full exchange: [matt-layden/enigma.md — a 2025 continuation](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2025-continuation-reverse-engineering-from-photographs-and-the-george-van-sickle-blog-reconfirmed-live).[91]

## Seamanship shared with the group

He has also passed on practical advice drawn from his own trips. To help a Paradox come about cleanly in strong wind, he keeps two gallons of water and a heavy anchor in the far-forward locker, a trick that works up to about 25 knots, above which he simply circles the boat around the other way instead.[24] He notes that because the Paradox's mast sits off-centre, uneven steering between tacks can be balanced by shifting the halyard's position on the yard rather than by moving the mast itself.[25] On multi-day trips through Québec's frequent stretches of rain, he keeps a Paradox's cabin dry by gluing a foam layer to the inside roof to cut condensation, stepping out only under an umbrella (also useful for moving the boat with a yuloh in light drizzle), opening the hatch about 18 inches to pass in and out without letting rain in, leaving wet footwear on the rear deck, and going through a roll of shop towels a week.[26] Sailing both a Gorfnik and a Paradox into a Québec winter as ice began forming on the lakes, he described in some depth how each boat is kept warm and how each combustion heater is kept safe: aboard the Paradox, a propane heater with two independent automatic shutoffs — one for stale, oxygen-depleted air, the other for the heater tipping over — a safety margin he judges priceless, though the hatch still has to stay open to regulate the heat; the smaller Gorfnik cabin he judges too small even for that heater, relying instead on a simple candle lantern carried over from winter wilderness tent-camping, which gives little heat but cuts humidity substantially and doubles as a light once the flame itself is done. He also passed on a chimney-siting rule from the same tradition — the flue must extend above the cabin top for its draft to work, and a metal ring fitted where it crosses the cabin side (an old wall-tent term for the fitting is a "plate") keeps the surrounding wood from burning — plus a wider set of low-tech ways to stay warm while sailing carried over from two decades of winter wilderness travel: a bottle of boiling water at the feet, down booties, chemical toe warmers, a fur hat, wool layers, and a large breathable wind shell worn over the life vest. His standing rule throughout: never go to sleep with any combustion device still lit. See [cabin heating for cold-weather sailing — a November 2015 continuation](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-november-2015-continuation-further-heater-detail-and-a-foam-insulation-contrast).[46]

A rare Québec heatwave the following July put a different-weather test to the same practical-advice pattern: sailing and camping in briefly extreme 40°C (104°F) heat, relied on a silver **Birdiepal outdoor umbrella** as a portable sunshade strong enough to survive any wind, doubling as rain cover so sailing could continue with the hatch half open through a passing storm; repeated overboard dips, aided by the swim step, provided the only real relief. For the same trip's biting insects, fits self-adhesive female velcro (which sticks to nothing else) around the hatch and forward vent-hole openings to mate with male-velcro-edged insect netting, with a lengthwise zipper sewn into the hatch netting itself so it can be opened and closed without stripping and realigning the whole velcro seam every time it rains. A Fan-tastic Endless Breeze fan, hung over the vent hole and needed only for a night's first couple of hours, is recharged the next day by a 50-watt solar panel. See [components/vent-box.md — design rationale](/components/vent-box.md#design-rationale-ventilation-for-the-bahamas-and-cold-climate-adaptations).[70]

Helping a newcomer through his first-rig furling troubles that same November, confirmed a topping lift attached to the front only worked just as well for him; his own former topping lift, by contrast, had a real drawback — the sail could get hung up while flying low, exactly when the wind is strongest — and recalled that **Matt Layden**, asked once about a topping lift, had simply replied he did not think it a good idea. He did not rate a double pulley worth the trouble of the long extra rope it needs, preferring to slacken the halyard about 6 inches and pull with both hands on the furling rope to take up slack, even in tough winds. Asked for a photograph of his own front-rigged topping lift, answered that the boat was not to hand, but a drawing of it had already been posted to the group's "[Iota](/boats/ironsides.md)" photo album. See [furling system — a newcomer's rigging troubles, and a further compound halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-newcomers-rigging-troubles-and-a-further-compound-halyard-purchase-july-2015).[47][48] Nearly two years later, answering a different owner's furling-friction question, restated the same mechanism in fuller detail — an insufficiently tightened rope inside the furling drum's brass pipe leaves it sitting at an awkward angle that keeps the furler from rolling cleanly — and his own "half topping lift" fix. A day later, pushed back on overstating the difficulty: as designed, the gear works fine for a strong young man, and it is declining strength with age, not any fault in the mechanism, that makes it feel hard — furling from full down to about a third and back up again is easy, since the sail's full weight is not yet on the roller, and it is only the last third that grows heavy for a weaker sailor. At 64, having just completed a five-week cruise while healing a torn left shoulder, sailed the entire first three weeks using only one hand — alternating releasing the furling line about six inches and cleating it, then raising the sail about six inches and cleating that, repeated until the sail reached the top, and reversed to lower — the same technique making it possible to furl even the heavy last third using two hands. See [furling — a 2017 recurrence, and three further independent fixes](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2017-recurrence-and-three-further-independent-fixes).[59][60]

Answering a dissenting theory floated in February 2020 that credited most of a Paradox's lateral resistance to the rudder rather than the chine runners, restated his own firsthand conviction the other way: the runners have little effect sailed flat in light air, but once the boat is leaned hard they dig in and act "as a long keel right underneath the boat," making "a WORLD of difference" to pointing — "Yes the boat will sail without the chine runners, but it won't point nearly as high. Believe in the design! The harder I sail the boat, the more I believe." See [how chine runners work — a 2020 recurrence](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-2020-recurrence-a-dissenting-owner-rebuttals-and-further-testimony).[79]

In August 2017, replying to a new owner restoring a secondhand Paradox, described his own yard's end-hole and halyard technique: a pair of 1/4 in holes near each end for lines that pull the sail's head tight, and a halyard fastened with a stainless-steel strap wrapped around the yard and a shackle bolted through it, with a matching hole cut in the sail's own sleeve. Full account: [components/rig/index.md](/components/rig/index.md#yard-end-holes-purpose-and-a-rot-prevention-technique-for-drilling-them) and [boats/edge.md](/boats/edge.md#sold-to-deric-and-restoration-begins-2017).[61] Two months later, replying to a builder seeking a trailer design, described his own method for both a purpose-built and a converted trailer: two 2x4s tied crosswise about six feet apart, topped by two carpeted, eight-foot 2x8s laid lengthwise above the trailer's own frames, so the boat's weight bends the longitudinal boards down onto the frame and is carried along eight feet of hull on each side, where it is strongest — "not as fancy" as another builder's own purpose-built design, but effective. Full account: [operations/trailering.md — a 2017 recurrence](/operations/trailering.md#a-2017-recurrence-a-flatbed-conversion-a-restated-roller-design-and-a-carpeted-bunk-build).[62]

**Explaining the rope-steering loop's mechanics, 2016.** Answering a fellow owner puzzling over which way a newly rigged steering loop should turn the boat, set out the underlying mechanics directly: pulling front to back on the starboard rope turns the boat right, as if pulling the bow that way, while the port rope simultaneously runs back to front, pulling the stern around to the left for the same turn. See [components/steering.md](/components/steering.md#a-2016-recurrence-rope-steering-direction-confirmed-and-a-tiller-comparison).[49]

Comparing the cabin to two-person tent camping, he judged sleeping two aboard workable but cramped next to a tent's open staging area outside, and endorsed keeping a Paradox strictly for solo use with a second, larger boat for when family wants to come along; full comparison at [operations/crew-size-and-fit.md — a tent-camping comparison, and Mike Beebe's removable cabin top](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#a-tent-camping-comparison-and-mike-beebes-removable-cabin-top-2014).[38]

In his own experience the Paradox windows are perfect — great visibility all around, and never having come anywhere near hitting his head, something that has never even crossed his mind; the same holds for the Gorfnik, whose windows are similar. The side decks earn their keep too: the yuloh stows on one side and the sail on the other, and both stay out of the way enough to walk forward even with the yuloh in place. When beaching, he usually carries the yuloh along as well, using it to pole-vault off the front onto dry land and back aboard again without getting his feet wet. Full exchange at [components/pilothouse.md — trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin).[43] Sailing his own Paradox in Texas alongside [Michael Beebe](/people/michael-beebe.md)'s for six days of cold rain, recalled that Beebe's boat had been built with its side decks pushed out to the sheer line and a longer tiller for sitting sideways as on a conventional sailboat — a modification that cost Beebe the side decks' use for stowing the yuloh and mast and made climbing on and off the cabin top harder, and that Beebe eventually sold rather than reverted, building a second Paradox with the cabin to plan instead; read it as a sign the widening had not paid off as hoped, judging a genuinely good reason is needed before departing from the designed cabin width — though he never heard Beebe complain about visibility. Full exchange at [components/pilothouse.md — trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin).[44]

In January 2018, passed on a cam-cleat orientation fix credited to a fellow builder's boat: facing the halyard and furling-line cleats upward so the pull runs horizontal and straight to the rear, rather than downward, lets both be cleated and uncleated without leaving the seat, easing reefing considerably. See [components/rig/internal-rigging.md — cam cleat orientation, and reefing/cleating from the seat](/components/rig/internal-rigging.md#cam-cleat-orientation-cleating-uncleating-and-reefing-from-the-seat-2018).[68]

Agreeing in April 2020 with a fellow builder's own list of what to change on a second build — cleats reoriented for an easier pull, split locker covers, and assembling the vent box to the bulkhead ahead of the hull among them — noted he had already made each of those same changes on his own two boats.[81] Four months later, on a fresh "hatch" thread, gave the fuller reasoning for bolts over screws through a hull generally: water finding its way past a fastener eventually is almost impossible to prevent entirely however well maintained, but a bolt lets it drip down visibly inside the boat — an early warning, easy to fix before the wood rots — where a screw traps the same water invisibly until rot forces the whole piece to be replaced, and a screw, unlike a bolt, can also let go entirely at the worst moment. See [components/hatch.md — the outer rail's carriage bolts](/components/hatch.md#the-outer-rails-carriage-bolts).[82]

Answering a fellow owner's tiller-fatigue-under-sail question in August 2018, restated and extended his own forward-ballast tacking tip (above) with a fuller balance explanation: reaching or beating, either of his two Paradoxes sails long tacks with the ropes untouched entirely; running, by contrast, used to mean sweating and working hard until he learned to reduce sail instead, after which the boat holds the same speed downwind under far less canvas and handles more easily, digging in less at the bow; a heavy battery and spare anchors already sit forward on both boats alongside the water jugs, and the extra weight also cuts down on missed tacks. Tightening the steering ropes helps too, aided by a short elastic tied on to take up slack, and both boats carry a tightening clamp to hold the ropes temporarily for a break. Full exchange: [operations/sailing-characteristics.md — a 2018 recurrence](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-2018-recurrence-tiller-fatigue-running-and-two-rudder-position-ideas).[71]

Days later, passed on a tested self-rescue technique for climbing back aboard by a trailing line after going overboard: lying on the back while pulling on the line overhead keeps the face out of the water, favors lifting over diving, and lets the stronger arm muscles work through straight wrists rather than bent ones — arrived at, in his words, by testing it a lot, on the reasoning that testing beats surprises in a real emergency. See [operations/personal-safety-gear.md — a skeptical physics critique of the trailing rescue line, and a counter-anecdote](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#a-skeptical-physics-critique-of-the-trailing-rescue-line-and-a-counter-anecdote).[73]

## Coming to sailing, and his first Paradox: Ironsides (January 2013)

Bourbeau came to sailing relatively late, after a career as **professor emeritus at the University of Québec**, teaching adventure tourism and researching wilderness survival and outdoor risk management, and after building his own home and several birchbark canoes; by the time he found the list he owned a Siren 17 microcruiser and had taken two sailing-theory courses, but counted himself "nearly a complete beginner" at actually sailing.[27] Having lurked on the list for over two years without finding the courage to start a build of his own, his first Paradox was **[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)**, bought from Stephen Yahn in January 2013 from more than 5,000 km away in northern Québec — the purchase itself, the welcome it drew from Alastair Law, the trailer-hunting saga of collecting the boat that May, and a first shakedown sail at Black Lake are all recorded in full on that boat's own page.[27][28] In July 2013 he informed the group that Stephen Yahn, whom he had met picking up the boat, had died, crediting the archive with showing how many good ideas Yahn had brought to the list's discussions; see [people/stephen-yahn.md](/people/stephen-yahn.md#death-2013) for the fuller record.[29] In 2015 he cruised Ironsides for three weeks on Florida's St-Johns River, building himself a canoe-style seat that solved a long-standing sore-bum problem; full record at [boats/ironsides.md — a 2015 Florida cruise](/boats/ironsides.md#a-2015-florida-cruise-the-st-johns-river) and [components/interior.md — a built canoe-style seat](/components/interior.md#a-built-canoe-style-seat-ten-years-after-the-quintessential-seat-brief).[40] That same March he set out the boat's cross-country purchase and delivery in full for the first time, and reported a roller-furling arm Stephen Yahn had built failing outright in a hard Saguenay Fjord sail; ## Reaching out across the continent (September 2013)

## The "Great Loop": a considered but declined idea (November 2016)

Asked whether he had thought about doing America's inland-waterway "Great Loop" circuit in a Paradox, answered that he had, and was still considering it: the boat could certainly handle the trip, but his concerns lay elsewhere — a long time to be alone, and better places to go sailing than the Mississippi — though he allowed there was something to the idea of becoming "a looper," label or no label.[52] Days later, after weighing it further, came down against it: the only real reason to attempt the loop would be bragging rights, and at his age he no longer needed those, preferring instead to spend the same time and energy on North America's best cruising grounds — he singled out his own Paradox cruises on Florida's St-John's River, Ontario's Thousand Islands Park, and the Saguenay Fjord, with the Bahamas and the Sea of Cortez still ahead of him — while allowing there is something to be said for the adventure and the "torture" of completing a genuine challenge, drawing a parallel to why people put themselves through the Texas 200: not for pure enjoyment, but because suffering is healthy "in some strange way," as a survival expert would know.[53]

## A motorized boat for guests, and further yuloh advice (August 2018)

Explaining, to that same newly building owner, why he has never fitted a motor to either Paradox: doing so would cost the boat its charm and the welcome that comes with arriving under sail or oar alone, and if he wanted the convenience of a motor he would simply sail a bigger boat instead. For friends who want the option, he keeps a separate **26 ft Norwalk Island Sharpie** fitted with a motor as a social boat — in his own words, "it feels exactly a huge Paradox" — reserved for passenger-friends on a schedule, with the two Paradoxes kept engine-free.[72] Lending his second Paradox out to friends (see above) has its own learning curve: newcomers to the yuloh "just can't get anywhere" at first, so he fitted a stand-up paddle alongside the yuloh on the loaner boat as a backup, since mastering the stroke takes many days of practice even with instruction.[72] He passed on his established yuloh tips once more in the process — the handle-ward "overdrive" pivot hole and a rubber-hose pivot-retention trick — recorded in full at [components/yuloh.md](/components/yuloh.md#citations).[72]

Having previously mentioned teaching himself to scull left-handed (see [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#citations)), a concrete account of when it actually mattered followed years later: nursing a tennis-elbow injury on a five-week Florida cruise, he had to learn the stroke left-handed in earnest, complicated by the pivot sitting on the "wrong" side of the boat for a left-hander, and needed about twelve hours of practice before starting to feel comfortable — a skill he still keeps up, in his own words, "because a change is as good as a rest."[72]

In October 2018 confirmed running the continuous single-line halyard/reefing system on one of his two Paradoxes, with a plan to convert the other once its own line wore out, and restated his standing view — shared, in his account, with **Matt Layden** — that a full topping lift is best avoided in favor of the half-topping-lift he has long used at the front end only. Full account: [operations/trailering.md — removing the boat from the trailer entirely](/operations/trailering.md#removing-the-boat-from-the-trailer-entirely-for-storage-or-a-beach-launch-october-2018).[75]

## A canoe-poling technique for the yuloh in shallow water (October 2018)

Opened "Poling the Paradox," a new thread off the same day's grounding discussion, offering a technique carried over from years as an outdoor professional and canoe-poling instructor in rapids: the yuloh, though shorter than a dedicated 12 ft canoe pole and carrying a bend a canoe pole lacks, still serves well as a push-pole in shallow water, worked with the curve's convex face turned outward so it poles partly under the boat with the blade running parallel to the hull. The technique moves the boat upwind without the sail in shallow water and frees her from a grounding too, since poling backward works as well as forward; recommended practising along a beach in controlled conditions, learning to turn the boat left and right with the rudder fully up, ideally trying the same motion in a canoe first. Full technique, and a fellow owner's independent confirmation: [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md — poling](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#poling-a-canoe-poling-technique-for-shallow-water-groundings-and-going-upwind-without-sail).[76]

## The St-Lawrence: tides, a tanker wave, and a promotional ice float

Answering a newcomer's question about his own experience on the **St-Lawrence** river, said he had not yet sailed it extensively but had explored nearby waters — a stretch near **Tadoussac**, a passage around the south of **Île d'Orléans** in a Gorfnik, and a Paradox passage through **Thousand Islands National Park** — with fuller St-Lawrence cruising still ahead, and life "too short" to keep putting it off.[85] Warned that the river demands real homework, for its major currents, powerful tides, shoals, and heavy commercial traffic, from firsthand experience: standing in calm, knee-deep water off Île d'Orléans while boarding a Gorfnik, he was hit from behind without warning by a five-foot wave thrown up by a tanker passing on the river's far side toward Québec City — a lesson learned.[85] Separately recalled an earlier promotional stunt, his own idea: three friends floated down the St-Lawrence between Trois-Rivières and Québec City on a large piece of ice, drawing enough disruption to commercial traffic to make national headlines.[85]

## A 2021 case against fitting a motor at all

Set out the fullest case on record against fitting any motor to a Paradox at all: a motor's stink, weight, line tangles, fuel danger, and maintenance are exactly the burdens the boat's own small size is meant to leave behind, and an electric motor only substitutes a different burden — battery weight and upkeep for comparatively poor performance; avoiding a motor entirely was the main reason for choosing the Paradox in the first place, with a properly practised yuloh judged the sail's true complement. A second, larger boat — a 26 ft Norwalk Island Sharpie fitted with an engine — is kept purely for guests who must be back on a schedule, "that's what motors are for — big boats and schedules," leaving both Paradoxes engine-free. Travelling engine-free draws its own conversation-starter, "What, no motor? You came all this way without an engine?" — put a motor on the boat, in this view, and she becomes just another normal boat. On reliability, holds that a motor will always fail exactly when it is needed most, and that learning to do without one is the safer habit to build.[86]
