# Andres Espino

**Andres Espino** — posting as `ima_very_cool_cowboy` and signing almost every message "Andrew" — was among the most prolific contributors to the [Paradox builders mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md) between December 2008 and October 2011. He never built a Paradox, never ordered plans, and never owned one. His standing in the archive rests instead on comparative experience: he had built five boats by 2009 — three from published magazine designs, two from Glen-L plans — and owned and regularly beached a 26 ft sharpie of his own, a 21 ft Glen-L "Fancy Free" that a previous builder had stretched by 5 ft on the same beam.[3][5][7] He was 60 in February 2009, and his stated ambition throughout was a single-handed Atlantic crossing in a shallow-draft boat.[4][7]

What drew him to Paradox specifically was the one quality his own boat lacked. His sharpie heels to about 45 degrees, then tips to 90 and lies flat, and will not right itself without help; Paradox, by contrast, self-rights and self-rescues from a full capsize the way a kayak does, where most production sailboats recover only to about 90 degrees.[4][8] Combined with internal water ballast and steering from inside a closed cabin, that made her, in his reading, the right hull for a long shorthanded passage — if only she could be made bigger.[5]

Because he spoke from outside the design rather than from ownership, the archive's [source-authority order](/paradox.md) places an owner's account of Paradox above his on any Paradox-specific question. His value lies where he was strongest: in the wider small-boat literature, in sharpie type-knowledge, and in the arithmetic and structural consequences of changing a set of plans.

## Enlarging Paradox

Espino opened, sustained, and eventually closed the list's fullest examination of scaling the design up. He arrived asking simply what the "stretched version" was and where its drawings could be found,[1] then within two months posed the structural question the thread never fully answered: whether enlargement requires adding stations, whether a larger hull needs thicker plywood and heavier members such as a keelson, whether the interior would have to be redesigned outright, and what would happen to five or six feet of unsupported panel between bulkheads in rough water.[2] A personal motive surfaced alongside the technical one: being tall, he had looked at a stretched version partly for his own fit, and was pointed instead toward John Welsford's "Swaggie" as a design already built to that size.[6]

His answers came from the boatbuilding trade rather than from Paradox. He relayed Glen-L's own published guidance on altering stock plans — that most designs tolerate roughly ±10% in length, since length can be added by respacing a hull's stations while the centre of buoyancy stays put, but that changes to beam and depth alter a hull's hydrostatics outright and are far riskier.[3][33] He supplied the scaling arithmetic through Jim Michalak's published essay on enlarging the AF4 skiff: panel area grows as the square of the scale factor and enclosed volume, and therefore displacement, as its cube — a 44% and a 73% increase respectively at a factor of 1.2.[25] He returned repeatedly to the group's own Files-section enlargement document, treating its 20% figure as the practical ceiling.[27][28]

By March 2009 he had talked himself out of the project. Stretching past 20%, he concluded, would demand thicker and stronger materials, and those would shift buoyancy and centre of gravity enough to require redesigning the whole boat; a 29-footer would cost many times a 15-footer; on economics alone a 28 ft Paradox was not worth building.[12] The interest survived the decision. He went on distinguishing Paradox's monocoque construction from ordinary ply-over-frame sharpie practice — enlarge her too far and the bracing is simply inadequate, fixable only by adding ribs or bulkheads after the fact[28] — and in October 2011 gave the most concrete structural advice in the whole debate, to a builder already cutting panels for a +25% stretch: at that scale bulkhead spacing reaches about 3 ft 10 in against a recommended ocean-going maximum near 3 ft, and the fix is to notch the bulkheads and bend stringers around them — 1x2 stock, or 3/8 in ply strips double-laminated because 3/4 in will not take the curve — with drywall screws as temporary clamps.[32]

The full debate is narrated at [design/larger-paradox.md](/design/larger-paradox.md), the builder-side proposals at [design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md), and the reverse case — scaling down — at [design/ideas/scaled-down-idea.md](/design/ideas/scaled-down-idea.md).

## Rocker, chine runners, and the sharpie question

Espino supplied the archive's clearest account of why chine runners suit some hulls and not others, and the reason is rocker. His own Glen-L sharpie carries about 4 in of rocker at the centre of buoyancy — nearly flat, much like Paradox — against something closer to 24 in on a typical Bolger design. On a hull with that much curve a chine runner would trace a half-moon rather than a keel line and make drag rather than lift, which is why Bolger's own boats use leeboards and the Advanced Sharpie family uses centreboards.[10] He also offered a theory for why Matt Layden arrived at the device at all: a light micro-cruiser does not sit as deep as a larger boat, and the runners extend the chine downward roughly another 4 in to compensate.[11] He had himself misread the runners as running the full length of the chine, and corrected the error in public once a Yrvind article set him right.[9]

On the type question he was unambiguous, and his answer is the one the archive kept: sharpies began as fishing boats in the shallow coastal waters of the eastern United States, their flat bottoms suited to shoals and beaching; a sharpie is a kind of dory with less flare and a flatter bottom; and Paradox, like most of Matt Layden's and Bolger's boats, belongs to the family.[22][8] He wrote the list's notice of Phil Bolger's death in May 2009, judging that Bolger had probably influenced home boatbuilding more than anyone else and pioneered instant-boatbuilding with designs like the Micro and Martha Jane.[16]

See [chine-runners/how-they-work.md](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-2009-revisit-a-chine-runner-conversion-floated-for-a-bolger-as-29jesse-cooper-hybrid) and [design/sharpie-lineage.md](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#sharpies-fishing-boat-origins-restated-from-outside-experience-2010).

## Self-righting, and an unsettled figure

Self-righting was the quality that brought him to the design and the subject he returned to most often. He set out the ordinary sharpie and dory baseline — stable to roughly 35 degrees of heel, vertical topsides being less stable than flared ones — and against it placed Paradox as one of the few small boats that will roll completely over and come back up, probably without even losing her mast if the halyard is let go quickly.[8][17] In October 2010 he proposed making her self-rescue more certain still with a small bolt-on fin keel modelled on a vintage racing sharpie called "Missile," citing the San Francisco Great Pelican's external keel as precedent; an established owner rejected it flatly, on the ground that Paradox already exceeds Bolger's Micro on that count and a keel would only cost performance.[23]

The same exchange left a figure unresolved. Reading the group's own stability chart, Espino put the turnover point at around 60 to 70 degrees and was corrected to the documented 165 degrees; in August 2011, in an Atlantic-crossing thread, he restated the angle as roughly 120 degrees, which happens to match Don Elliott's own 1996 estimate but was not reconciled with the 165-degree figure on record.[23][30] The archive's own settled value is given at [design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md — angle of vanishing stability](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources); the 120-degree restatement should not be treated as settled.

## Blue water

He argued, against the list's prevailing coastal-cruiser verdict, that the practical objections to a long passage were dissolving. Provisioning was largely solved: a month of freeze-dried meals with snacks and desserts fitted a single 5-gallon bucket for under $80, and a hand-pump lifeboat watermaker covered the rest.[24][30] He pointed to the international "Around in Ten" competition and to Paul Fisher's 8 ft ocean-crossing design as evidence of how fast very small offshore boats were then developing,[30] and floated adapting the sealed-gearbox pedal drives of Phil Thiel's "Escargot" canal boat to run a watermaker.[31] The counter-arguments, and the owners' verdicts that met them, are at [design/blue-water-suitability.md](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2011-recurrence-an-atlantic-crossing-hypothetical-self-righting-restated-to-120-degrees-and-a-former-faith-owners-own-appraisal).

## Practical contributions

- **Plywood.** Reported that US marine-plywood standards had been lowered since 2000, with the standard by then permitting up to 20 fill plugs per side, and that many plans had followed by accepting exterior A-C grade; recommended buying uncoated stock so voids and fills can be spotted before purchase, and encapsulating in epoxy either way. For a stretched hull he favoured cold-molded bias lamination of thinner sheets over one thicker panel. See [construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2009-recurrence-doubling-thinner-sheets-instead-of-a-single-thick-panel-and-marine-versus-ac-grade-from-a-working-shipwrights-experience).[18] He later settled a metric-versus-imperial substitution question, confirming 19mm ply will not snap in place of the plans' 18mm bottom, and noting that US builders round to imperial stock as a matter of course.[29]
- **Fasteners.** Ordered bronze, brass, and 316 stainless from Duckworks, which sells home builders the small quantities that avoid buying a whole lot for a dozen pieces, and from Jamestown Distributors for what Duckworks lacked — his own local hardware store being pricier and the nearest big-box stores 100 miles off. See [construction/fastening-and-gluing.md](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#sourcing-fasteners-without-big-box-markup).[26]
- **Lightning.** His sharpie carries no lightning ground. On seeing thunderheads he dangles a chain from the standing rigging into the water, and has watched electricity run visibly up and down the mast stays at night without ever being struck; his advice is to stay clear of all rigging in a storm and to reef or set a storm jib well before the weather arrives. See [design/lightning-protection.md](/design/lightning-protection.md#skipping-the-system-entirely-and-a-jury-rigged-grounding-chain).[14]
- **Yuloh.** Recalled Chinese yulohs in South-East Asia resting in a notch cut into the transom rather than on a pin, suggested a leash so one that comes free can be hauled back aboard, and expected most users now fit a dedicated stern swivel to spare the transom. See [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#a-proposed-flexible-fishtail-mimicking-blade-tip-2010).[21]
- **Rig.** Explained why the design is not sloop-rigged: a jib cannot easily be furled, reefed, or set without leaving the cabin, so a sloop would work against the whole point of steering from inside. See [components/rig/index.md](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-rig-isnt-a-sloop).[19]
- **Ballast and towing.** Suggested the smaller deep-cycle batteries built for mobility scooters as better suited to a micro-cruiser than full-size units,[20] and answered a towing-weight objection to an enlarged Paradox with removable water ballast — filled by valve after launching and drained before the road, as on production Catalinas.[13]
- **Hull assembly.** Objected that nailing through the hull into a bulkhead's edge is not ordinary practice, taped-and-glued joints from inside being the norm, then retracted the objection on realising he had overlooked bulkhead 3's exception. See [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#epoxy-coating-the-bulkheads-and-side-panels-before-or-after-the-wet-assembly).[15]

He was also, for several years, the list's habitual answerer of newcomers — pointing them to microcruising.com for the plans and for Little Cruiser's record, and to the Files section for the enlargement figures. One first-time builder thanked him for having "answered alot of unasked questions," reporting himself finally content with his choice of boat.[16a]
