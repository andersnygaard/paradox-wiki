# Andres Espino

**Andres Espino** is a boatbuilder in the **United States** who has built five boats, three to published magazine designs and two to Glen-L plans.[1] His own boat, a 26 ft Glen-L sharpie, began as the stock 21 ft "Fancy Free" design, which he stretched 5 ft himself by inserting additional stations and re-fairing the hull; he has sailed her along the southern US coast and through the Caribbean.[2][3] He never built a Paradox himself. What drew him to the design was the one quality his own sharpie lacks: Paradox rolls fully over from a capsize and comes back up much like a kayak, where most production sailboats self-right only to about 90 degrees.[4] From outside the design, his knowledge of the wider small-boat literature and the arithmetic and structural consequences of altering a set of plans apply most directly to scaling Paradox up.

## Scaling Paradox up

Glen-L's own published guidance on altering stock plans holds that most designs tolerate roughly plus or minus 10% in length, since length can be added by respacing a hull's stations while the centre of buoyancy stays put, though changes to beam and depth are riskier, altering a hull's hydrostatics outright.[1] Jim Michalak's published essay on enlarging the AF4 skiff supplied the scaling arithmetic: panel area grows as the square of the scale factor and displacement as its cube.[5] He treats Matt Layden's own 20% sanction as the practical ceiling: stretching further would demand thicker, stronger materials that shift buoyancy and centre of gravity enough to require redesigning the whole boat, ruling out a 28-footer on cost alone.[6] For a builder already cutting panels for a 25% stretch, he worked out that bulkhead spacing at that scale reaches about 3 ft 10 in against a recommended ocean-going maximum near 3 ft and proposed notching the bulkheads and bending stringers or doubling laminated 3/8 in ply strips around them.[7] He also proposed removable water ballast, filled by valve after launching and drained before the road, to meet a towing-weight objection to an enlarged hull.[8] Full account: [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md), [Stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) and [a reverse case, scaling down](/design/ideas/scaled-down-idea.md).

## Sharpie type and self-righting

His own sharpie is the concrete case behind his account of why chine runners suit some hulls and not others: she carries only about 4 in of rocker at the centre of buoyancy, nearly flat like Paradox, against nearly 24 in on a typical Bolger design, so a chine runner on her would trace a half-moon rather than a keel line; Bolger's own boats use leeboards instead, and her swing keel tracks well fully raised.[9] Sharpies, in his account, began as shallow-draft fishing boats on the eastern US coast, related to dories but with less flare and a flatter bottom, a family Paradox and most of Matt Layden's and Bolger's boats belong to; the type is typically stable only to about 35 degrees of heel, with a beam-to-length ratio near 1/3 against roughly 1/4 on a comparable Bolger design, and his own boat heels to 45 degrees before tipping to 90 and lying flat.[3][10] He treats Paradox itself as fully self-righting and self-rescuing like Bolger's Micro, rolling back upright much like a kayak even from turned turtle once all tension is released from the sail.[11] Full account: [How chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md), [Glen-L's "Fancy Free"](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#glen-ls-fancy-free), [Sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md) and [Self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md).

## Blue water thinking

A month of freeze-dried meals, snacks and desserts included, fits a single 5-gallon bucket for under $80, and a hand-pump lifeboat watermaker covers the rest of what a long passage needs.[12] He read the international "Around in Ten" competition and Paul Fisher's 8 ft ocean-crossing design as evidence of how fast very small offshore boats were developing, and floated adapting the sealed-gearbox pedal drives of Phil Thiel's "Escargot" canal boat to run a watermaker instead.[13][14] Full account: [Blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) and [Auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md).

## Materials

US marine-plywood standards had been lowered since 2000, with the industry standard allowing up to 20 fill plugs per side; he recommended buying it uncoated so voids and fills can be spotted before purchase and encapsulating in epoxy either way. For a stretched hull he favoured cold-molded lamination of thinner sheets over a single thick panel.[15] For fasteners he sourced bronze, brass and 316 stainless from Duckworks, which sells home builders the small quantities that avoid buying a whole lot for a dozen pieces, and from Jamestown Distributors for what Duckworks lacked.[16] Full account: [Plywood grades and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md) and [Fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md).

## Rig, yuloh and self-steering

Sailing a coast prone to sudden thunderheads with no lightning-ground fitting of his own, he dangled a chain from the standing rigging into the water when he saw them coming and advised staying clear of all rigging in a storm.[17] He treats Paradox's rig as unsuited to a sloop conversion, since a jib cannot easily be furled, reefed or set without leaving the cabin.[18] Chinese yulohs he had seen used in South-East Asia rest in a notch cut into the transom rather than on a pin, with a leash as a simple safeguard for one that comes free.[19] For self-steering he preferred, in his own words, "simple wind-vane or other non mechanical means" over the newer electronic gear other builders were beginning to discuss.[20] Full account: [Lightning protection](/design/lightning-protection.md), [Rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md), [Sculling and yuloh technique](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md) and [Self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md).
