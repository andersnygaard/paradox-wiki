# Kjell Karlsson

**Kjell Karlsson**, of Sweden's Stockholm archipelago, built **"[Paraplastic](/boats/bris-paradox.md)"**, a [Paradox](/paradox.md) built in Sven Yrvind's "BRIS" foam-sandwich composite method rather than plywood, a project begun in 2003.[1] He has been a personal acquaintance of [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md) since the mid-1970s and obtained the Paradox plans directly from [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) after learning of the design through Yrvind.[1] The boat is now owned and sailed by [Johan Bloom](/people/johan-bloom.md) on Sweden's west coast as **"Tetris."**[2][3] Full build account: [boats/bris-paradox.md](/boats/bris-paradox.md).

## Coming to Paradox through Yrvind

Kjell had already spent a decade on a stalled dinghy project built to Yrvind's own "BRIS" hull concept when he learned of Layden's designs through Yrvind in 1998.[1] An introduction arranged for the following year, when his family holidayed in Florida, fell through when that season's wildfires sent them north instead, and the meeting he still regrets never took place.[1] He had also sketched his own two-person, scaled-up version of the boat; shown to Yrvind in person, it drew a gentle warning that the result would be "a 50% heavier and quite another boat," so he built to Layden's stock plans instead, keeping the BRIS composite material rather than switching to plywood, a choice made with Yrvind's blessing and, at one remove, Layden's too.[1]

## Building in Yrvind's method

Kjell frames his approach to building with five half-joking maxims: big boats bring big trouble and small boats small trouble; wood and water don't mix; aluminum and human flesh don't mix; plastic is fantastic; and water should stay outside the boat.[1] He describes himself as neither a boat constructor nor a designer and treats Yrvind, trained in chemistry, as his guru in the method.[4] What drew him to Paradox in the first place was less a wish to sail than a shared interest in Layden and Yrvind as free-thinking designers who publish open, non-proprietary solutions rather than patenting or selling their work.[5] During one shop visit, Yrvind inspected Kjell's completed bulkheads, pronounced them too heavy and left the verdict in coarse ink on the shop wall: **"TÄNK PÅ VIKTEN, Yrvind"** ("Think of the weight").[1] While puzzling over the plans he worked at an old office chair, turning to two CDs of Swedish birdsong once he found music too distracting to build by.[6] His own build, far more resource-intensive than a plywood Paradox built to the lowest possible cost, followed the opposite logic throughout, weight and durability over economy.[7] The material and construction changes the BRIS method brought, from the divinycell sandwich itself to a bronze-sheathed bottom carrying ballast in place of lead, are recorded in full at [boats/bris-paradox.md](/boats/bris-paradox.md#kjells-laws-of-nature-and-the-materialconstruction-changes-from-stock).

## Translating a friend's book

Meeting a standing wish for an English translation of Yrvind's new Swedish-language book, Kjell translated its Matt Layden/Paradox chapter and released it as "chapter9.doc," with the author's own permission and good wishes.[8] He called himself an unauthorized translator and invited corrections to any factual slips.[8] After a further conversation with Yrvind he circulated a revised translation, "Chapter9rev," correcting and clarifying his own earlier work.[9] A wider push to crowd-translate the whole book closed without a volunteer effort: Yrvind was willing to see the already-translated Paradox chapter offered to a publisher as a sample, but wanted a professional translator, not volunteers, for the rest.[10][11][12] Full saga at [references/yrvind-book.md](/references/yrvind-book.md).

## Sailing again, after twenty years

Kjell's first sail in the finished boat came after 21 years without having sailed anything at all, single-handed on a nearby lake.[13] He held a long-standing, half-serious ambition to sail to the Baltic states, particularly Estonia, tracing it to a failed attempt some thirty years earlier to reach Leningrad that Soviet authorities refused him permission for; before World War II, he recalled, the Estonian coast had been a popular Swedish beach-holiday destination, cut off entirely once the Iron Curtain fell.[14] A short sail one gusty weekend went worse than expected; afterward he joked he might take up stamp collecting instead.[15]
