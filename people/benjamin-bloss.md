# Benjamin Bloss

**Benjamin Bloss** is a builder **just north of Seattle, Washington**, on **Puget Sound**, constructing the [Paradox](/paradox.md) **"[Pathfinder](/boats/pathfinder.md)"** — begun with his first cuts in **December 2021** and still under way, with the bottom not yet on, in 2025.[1][2][16] He bought his plans from **Dave Bolduc** in September 2021 after his first email bounced, and it is his **second home-built sailboat**, following a Stevenson Weekender built in college in 2005 and still in use.[3][4] He documents the build in a **[series of video build logs](/references/sailing-pathfinder-videos.md)** and works from CAD, laying the plans out digitally in order to laser-cut a scale model before committing wood.[5][6]

He is unusual among the archive's builders in coming to the design from **professional sea time rather than from woodworking**. Bloss began his working life as a deck officer with the US **National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration**, standing navigation watches aboard the exploration ship **Okeanos Explorer** as a commissioning-crew member — the posting that brought him to Seattle — and later worked as a **senior electrical engineer** at a Seattle tech company before quitting in 2022.[17][18] The sabbatical that followed turned into a return to the water by a different route: sailing lessons and volunteering at Seattle's **Center for Wooden Boats**, a winter spent as an apprentice in a Seattle **sail loft** doing repair work, and then deckhand, narrator, and bartender on a local tour-boat fleet, where by 2025 he had accumulated some 300 sea days and held a **USCG Master's licence** (self-propelled vessels other than sail, under 100 gross tons, inland) with a near-coastal OUPV endorsement, and expected to begin captaining that season.[17][19][20]

That background shapes how he builds and how he writes about it. His repeated theme is **practice over preparation** — that reading has diminishing returns without doing, that "you have to suck before you're good at something," and that the frustration of being an inexpert woodworker is best answered by accepting that building things badly is what makes building them well possible.[21] He is explicit that he is not building a museum piece: what he wants is a boat that is structurally sound and that he intends to beat the crap out of on beaches, and he cites the reminder of a fellow Stevenson Weekender builder who spent, on carved ropework in the trim, the time it took Bloss to build his whole boat.[22] Part of the original inspiration was **Alex Zimmerman's *Becoming Coastal***, whose account of cruising Puget Sound and the BC coast showed him how much was reachable in his own backyard.[23]

## A table of contents for Don Elliott's manual

Bloss's most-used contribution to the community is not on his own boat. Working from [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s [*Building Paradox*](/references/building-paradox-series.md) series — which Elliott himself described as having grown from "obscure notes jotted down on a piece of wood scrap from Paradox" — he found the important material scattered across the parts and hard to reach while actually building, so he **wrote a table of contents for the whole manual** and published it to the group, paginated to the printed pages rather than the PDF reader's numbering, with the original spelling left intact so headings remain searchable.[7]

## The video build log

The **[Sailing-Pathfinder videos](/references/sailing-pathfinder-videos.md)** were begun in November 2021, before the first cut, in conscious imitation of **[Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md)**'s habit of short daily workshop videos — a model Bloss names in his first minute on camera and recommends to viewers.[24] Twelve videos carry the build from cleared shop to laminated chine logs. Their editorial character is set by a decision he states plainly: painful as it is to record work in progress, he shares it so that other builders have some idea of what was tried, on the grounds that he himself has got "a lot of mileage" out of photographs other builders took.[25] The result is a record of attempts that failed as well as those that worked, and of decisions weighed aloud before the outcome was known — a kind of source the archive otherwise lacks, since almost every other first-person Paradox account was written by someone who finished.

He is also candid about the limits of his own recollection. Filming the [stem](/components/stem.md) about a month after making it, he could recall only part of how he had done it, said so on camera, and drew from it the practical conclusion to record more often.[26]

## Plans, materials, and method

He reads the plans closely enough to have posted the archive's clearest worked example of **interpreting their non-perpendicular dimension lines** — and then, characteristically, found and published his own error before anyone answered: the figure in question is the **hypotenuse along the side of the bulkhead**, so heights marked below the drawing are measured from the baseline down, with the above-baseline measurement added.[6] He also flagged an apparent contradiction in Elliott's manual, which advises that with exterior plywood the **knot holes should end up on the outside** of the side panels, against the more usual instinct to hide them inside where foam and sealing will cover them.[8]

For materials he used his local yard's **ABX marine plywood** for the main panels and experimented with **laminating 1/4" Sandee for the deck beams**, and he put the cost question about the [stem](/components/stem.md) squarely: at 2022 prices a clear vertical-grain Douglas fir 2×8 ran $150–200 against roughly a tenth of that for #2 construction lumber, for a part that is "a bloody important part of the boat that'll take a lot of punishment."[9][10] His **cleat-gluing routine** — dry-fit, wire nails as locating pins, both faces precoated with unthickened epoxy, thickened epoxy (fumed silica) on one side only, ring nails driven, clamp, clean up with a spreader — is written out step by step, along with the two things he found hardest: judging the quantity so the joint is not "a gooey, sticky mess," and seating ring nails flush rather than slightly proud (see [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md)).[11]

Two of his methods are recorded nowhere else in the archive and are drawn out on the pages they belong to: the **half-width cardboard pattern and foam-poster-board template** by which he shaped the transom cleat and [vent baffle](/components/vent-baffle.md) after battens and corrugated cardboard both failed him, and his **trigonometric derivation of the bulkhead cleat bevels** from a scale print of the plans — which he pronounced a mistake six months later, concluding that the angles are better taken off the boat as it comes together (see [hull assembly sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#fit-at-trial-assembly-rather-than-pre-cutting-a-perfect-bevel)).[27][28]

## Setbacks, and the things that stopped work

When his **sheer log cracked partially between bulkheads 2 and 3** after being nailed solidly in place, he laid out his options publicly — cut the section and patch in new wood, or open the crack with an oscillating tool and fill it with epoxy — rather than quietly bodging it.[12] Two larger interruptions had nothing to do with the boat's construction. The first was **cold**: working in a rented, shared garage whose air leaks could not practically be sealed, and wanting 50 °F held for a full day after each pour, he lost the winter of 2021–22 in part and the whole winter of 2022–23.[29] The second was a **move** — his partner bought a house — which cost most of two seasons and, worse, cost him his stock of lumber and plywood, stored in an existing shed where it grew **mould and mildew** and had to be written off, the two 3/4 in sheets for the bottom being the expensive part.[16]

His most-repeated piece of advice to other builders comes out of the first of those: get the hull **into three dimensions before a winter layup** if you possibly can, because walking past something that looks like a boat is an inspiring sight rather than a discouraging one, and he doubts the project would have survived a second winter of flat panels.[29]

## Two questions put to the group

Two of Bloss's questions raise design points the archive had not otherwise recorded from a builder. The first concerns the **vent-trunk drain**: a commenter on his videos reported that Matt Layden had told **Sven Yrvind** he would not fit the drain again, because in a knockdown, pitchpole or roll the tube **lets air out of the cabin and lets water in faster** through the pressure release. Bloss could find no documentation of it and the commenter never followed up, and he was left tempted simply to leave the tube plugged; a fellow builder replied that Yrvind had told him the same story, and had led his own mast-tube drain into a forward compartment instead.[13][30] Bloss **resolved it in favour of the plans** six weeks later, on camera: having asked other builders and found nothing documented, he judged the boat too stable for the failure mode to be a realistic worry — he has watched people almost climb the mast of a properly ballasted Paradox without tipping it — and reasoned that bunging the hole is an available remedy if he ever is worried, adding that the hole was drilled already, "that's the hard part." The claim itself remains an **unsourced second-hand report**, not Layden's recorded position (see [vent box](/components/vent-box.md)).[31]

The second is **battery bank as ballast**: a ham-radio operator, he asked whether anyone had replaced the lead with a large AGM or similar bank charged at home between outings, which would put the mass low and make it useful.[14] He has also asked whether anyone has combined an **HF antenna** with a Paradox.[15] The two interests converged in a winter project he showed on camera in 2025 — a self-contained, portable navigation system built into a droid head, carrying GPS, an AIS receiver, an Iridium satellite modem, a software-defined radio, an environmental sensor board, and a camera, made so that he would not have to build a fixed navigation computer into every boat he sails.[32]
