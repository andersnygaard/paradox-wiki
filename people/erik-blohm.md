# Erik Blohm

**Erik Blohm** sails out of **northeast Ohio**, USA, on **Lake Erie**, where he spent a decade cruising small trailerable boats, Precision, Potter and Com-Pac models among them, before taking on a 2005 Montgomery 17. He started his own [Paradox](/paradox.md) in February 2023, later naming her **"Rebel."**[1][2] He first met the design in 2016, weighing three capsize-recovery modifications against the stock plans: lead let into the gap between the chine runner and the hull side, a removable 10-12 kg bolt-on lead bulb keel and as buoyant a mast as practical. Persuaded the stock design's own righting margin made them unnecessary, he ordered plans and Don Elliott's building manual, both delivered as e-mailed PDFs within a day, then set the project aside.[3] Six years later, unable to find that original set, he ordered a fresh copy from Dave Bolduc and settled on Paradox over a Wharram Tiki 21, reasoning a first build was demanding enough with one hull rather than two.[1]

## Coming to Paradox

Studying the plans closely for the first time, not a boat designer himself, he judged them **"VERY well built"** and was hesitant to make major changes. The one departure he weighed, doubling the bottom to two 12mm sheets, each skinned in glass and Kevlar, rather than the plans' single 18mm panel, sits among other builders' own bottom-thickness choices at [plywood grades and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2022-recurrence-a-two-layer-bottom-from-a-live-build-and-a-doubled-kevlar-alternative).[4] Construction began that February in half of a two-car garage; at the same time, he weighed a carbon-fibre tube against the plans' wooden mast, inspired by another small-boat builder's own spar, a comparison recorded in full at [mast: a 2023 carbon-fibre inquiry](/components/rig/mast.md#a-2023-straight-carbon-fibre-mast-inquiry-sourcing-a-stiffness-caution-and-the-lightning-conductivity-risk).[5]

## Building Rebel

He builds by research first. Reading back through every earlier account of deck-beam lamination after coming across a caution about plywood delaminating, he split his own beam plan by structural position, laminating plywood at the four bulkheads with support behind them and solid mahogany at the two that stand unsupported.[6] Materials for the beams and bulkhead cleats followed within days, a friend's table saw enlisted to rip the first strips: **"It's only taken me 9 years to get to this point!!!!"**[7] Full account: [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#a-2023-continuation-mixed-materials-by-bulkhead-a-cold-water-bending-method-and-settling-on-a-beam-species).

The build has taken its setbacks in stride. A water-heater repair gone wrong flooded the basement that same February and cost four days; back at it, he bought a dedicated table saw and found the beam strip length he needed directly, with a batten sprung around the boat's tightest curve rather than trusting a rule of thumb.[8] A pair of nitrile gloves from an unopened box more than ten years old tore from dry rot mid-glue-up soon after, so a deck beam was finished bare-handed rather than restarted.[9] Six months in, by August 2023, all the bulkheads were complete and the side panels scarfed, with a junk rig, an external tiller, self-steering and a different hatch all weighed without commitment.[10] After a year without an update he returned in August 2024 to glue the bulkheads in, working out the sequence with [Andreas Westerberg](/people/andreas-westerberg.md) and giving the hull her name, **"Paradox Rebel,"** for the first time.[2]
