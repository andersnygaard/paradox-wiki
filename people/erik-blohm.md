# Erik Blohm

**Erik Blohm** sails out of **northeast Ohio**, on the shores of **Lake Erie**, and spent a decade aboard a succession of small trailerable boats — a Com-Pac 23, then a 2005 Montgomery 17 — before starting his own [Paradox](/paradox.md) in **February 2023**. He first wrote to the mailing list in **2016** with a set of capsize-recovery modification ideas, none of them adopted, and returned six and a half years later with plans re-ordered to begin construction in earnest; no name for the hull is yet on record.

## A first approach to the design, declined (2016)

Introducing himself in February 2016 as new to the group and not yet fully sold on a keel-less boat's offshore stability, he proposed three modifications aimed at improving capsize recovery, in order of preference: lead added into the gap between the chine runner and the hull side, glassed over, to lengthen the righting-moment arm, though doubted to help a full 180-degree roll; a removable, bolt-on lead bulb keel of 10-12 kg near the aft end of the keel, for offshore passages only; and as buoyant a mast as practical, or a tube run through or alongside it fitted with an inflatable bladder at the masthead and a small compressed-air cylinder to refill it after repeated knockdowns. All three were judged unnecessary against the stock design's own recovery margin.[1] Persuaded, he emailed Dave Bolduc for a set of plans and Don Elliott's widow for the building manual, describing himself as an amateur carpenter and asking after a tool and materials inventory, and planned to start building that fall.[2][3] Both arrived as e-mailed PDFs within 24 hours of ordering; he revised his own timeline to mid-summer, once an existing Com-Pac 23 was back in the water, and asked whether a table saw was worth the expense over a jigsaw.[4] No wood was cut that year.

## Returning to build (2022)

Erik resurfaced in November 2022, "re-inspired to build": the plans he had ordered some seven years earlier could no longer be found, so he re-ordered them from Dave Bolduc along with a fresh copy of Don Elliott's manual. By then he had spent a decade sailing a succession of popular trailer-sailors — Precision, Potter, and Com-Pac models — on Lake Erie and inland lakes, and had recently taken on a 2005 Montgomery 17; he had done basic repairs on his own boats but never built one from scratch. He had been weighing a Wharram Tiki 21 or 26 against the Paradox, but building two hulls at once felt overwhelming for a first project, and he settled on the Paradox, "decided to start building one ASAP."[5] Studying the plans closely for the first time, he judged them "VERY well built" and, not being a boat designer himself, was hesitant to make major changes — the one modification he considered was doubling the bottom panel to 12 mm plywood skinned in glass/Kevlar/glass rather than the plans' single 18 mm sheet.[6][7]

## Starting construction, and a wide-ranging mast question (2023)

By early February 2023 he had set up a workshop in half of a two-car garage, warmed with two electric heaters, and built a jig to laminate the deck beams from five strips of 25 mm x 9 mm plywood per beam — sourcing Meranti plywood marked to the BS1088 standard from a locally reputed seller.[8][9] The same message that announced the build under way also raised a question that ran well beyond his own boat: inspired by a YouTube builder's carbon-fibre mast on a 14 ft "Ocean Capable Small Sailboat," he asked what other builders thought of a carbon-fibre mast for the Paradox — opening the archive's fullest modern exchange on carbon-fibre and other alternative mast materials, covering sourcing, a stiffness caution, and a lightning-conductivity risk. Full account: [mast — a 2023 straight carbon-fibre mast inquiry](/components/rig/mast.md#a-2023-straight-carbon-fibre-mast-inquiry-sourcing-a-stiffness-caution-and-the-lightning-conductivity-risk).[8]

Reading back through the archive's whole deck-beam thread after hitting a caution about plywood delaminating, he split his own beam plan by structural position — laminated plywood at the four bulkheads with support behind them, solid laminated mahogany strips at the two that stand unsupported — and, over the following two days, bought a mahogany board and white oak for beam laminates and bulkhead cleats, with a sheet of Lloyd's-certified okoume plywood as a fallback if the oak would not bend; a boatbuilding reference recommending white oak against the plans' own caution against oak prompted the guess that the plans meant red oak specifically, which is oilier and harder to glue. A friend's table saw was lined up to rip the first beam-laminate strips that weekend — "It's only taken me 9 years to get to this point!!!!" Full technical detail on the beam materials and the bending and lamination methods discussed: [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#a-2023-continuation-mixed-materials-by-bulkhead-a-cold-water-bending-method-and-settling-on-a-beam-species).[10][11]

## A flooded basement, and the first actual cuts (February 2023)

A water-heater replacement gone wrong — a knocked sump-pump line flooding the basement — burned four days of build time later that month before he could call 25 February his first official day of construction. The oak strips ripped at the friend's shop two weeks earlier were set aside unused: opinion on whether white oak holds up reliably to an epoxy-laminated bond varied too widely to risk it. A SawStop portable table saw, bought specifically to re-rip the beam strips, replaced the borrowed one; the working strip length was found directly with a batten sprung around bulkhead #4's curve, the longest on the boat — 55 in gives 2.5 in of extra length on each side, enough to cut every beam to one working length and trim each afterward. His wife's price for taking over the garage: a kitchen remodel, "now that I have all the tools."[12]

## Deck beams finished, and bulkhead #4's cutout (March 2023)

By early March 2023 the first laminated deck beam — cut on a table-saw jig that produced 25 x 45 mm strips "spot on" — was nearly ready to come out of its mold; assembling it, a pair of nitrile gloves pulled from an unopened box more than ten years old tore from dry rot, and the beam was finished bare-handed rather than start the glue-up over, an old-stock-glove lesson he passed on to future builders. A curved template cut from 6 mm plywood, made to trace bulkhead outlines onto the remaining sheets, followed the same week.[13] By 15 March all six deck beams were complete and bulkheads 1 through 3 were cut; unable to find a record of bulkhead 4's own side curve, he asked the group directly, and used a 140 mm circle for the bottom of bulkhead 3's own passage cutout, a dimension the plans do not fix (see [bulkhead side curves](/construction/panel-layout.md#bulkhead-side-curves)).[14] He planned a 6 in Lewmar hatch at bulkhead 1's opening and was weighing the same at bulkhead 4.[15] A week later, ready to assemble bulkhead #1 itself, he asked whether its cleats and deck beam are nailed on before or after the epoxy coat and whether the plans' 19 mm floor timber has to be matched exactly; general technique for nailing bulkhead cleats and a deck beam together is at [fastening and gluing — assembling bulkhead cleats and deck beams](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#assembling-bulkhead-cleats-and-deck-beams-pre-drill-from-the-back-then-nail).[16]

## Six months on: bulkheads and vent box complete, mods weighed, and a rigging-diameter question (August 2023)

By mid-August 2023, six months into the build, all bulkheads were complete, the mast-step/vent box assembly about 90% finished, and the side panels scarfed and surface-epoxied except where the bulkheads would mount; the bulkheads' initial bevels were fine-tuned but expected to need further adjustment once dry-fitted. Four modifications were under consideration without commitment: a junk rig, an external tiller, sheet-to-tiller or windvane self-steering, and a Lewmar rectangular hatch in place of the stock sliding hatch. Wanting cam-cleats and turning blocks for inside the vent box, and unable to settle on the halyard, reef, and topping-lift line diameters — the plans give one figure, though some builders upsize by a couple of millimetres — he raised the question to the group; the plans' own reading of the sheet and reef diameter, and the general run of rope and block dimensions, are gathered at [running rigging](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rigging-dimensions-the-complete-record).[17]

## Gluing in the bulkheads, and the boat's name (August-September 2024)

A year after the last recorded update, Erik returned with a bulkhead-gluing question: locator blocks fixed with hot glue and two large roof nails as lineup pins were ready at the top and bottom of each bulkhead, along with ring nails pre-fitted to their holes, though he was weighing silicone/bronze screws as an alternative; unsure of the order to work in beyond starting at bulkhead 3, and whether the hull needs to be disassembled and reassembled for the glue-up or whether the sides can simply be spread apart and each bulkhead glued into the resulting gap, he planned to do the whole job in one session.[18] The answer he drew — assemble and align everything but the stem first without glue, drill alignment holes for wooden locating plugs, then disassemble and remount starting at bulkhead 3 and working outward, pulling the sides in with ratchet straps and holding each bulkhead with temporary screws until the epoxy cures — is given in full at [hull assembly sequence — assembling bulkheads out of sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#assembling-bulkheads-out-of-sequence-and-squaring-the-resulting-box).[19] He signed the message "Erik, Paradox 'Rebel'" — the first record of a name for his hull — and confirmed a few days later that he intended the same sequence, waiting for a day around 15°C so the epoxy would not set too quickly.[20]

## Disambiguation

A possible family connection exists to [Christian Blohm](/people/christian-blohm.md), a fellow builder in the same north-east Ohio/Lake Erie area whose own Facebook debut opens in near-identical terms two days after Erik's "Re-inspired to build" message — the same surname, the same region, the same "~7 years ago" plans purchase, and the same Tiki-versus-Paradox deliberation. A further point of overlap: Erik signs one 2023 message "Erik (Christian — my middle name on FBk)," so his own Facebook presence itself carries the name "Christian." Even so, no message links the two accounts directly, so they are treated as two different people rather than merged.[5][17]

A far stronger point of overlap surfaced in 2024. Christian named his own hull "Rebel" in a Facebook post dated 25 August 2024, resuming work after roughly a year away from the build; six days later, on 31 August 2024, Erik signed his own mailing-list message "Erik, Paradox 'Rebel'" — the first record of a name for his hull, the identical name, at almost the identical moment, after both accounts had likewise gone quiet on the build for about a year.[18][20] Taken together with the surname, the region, the matching plans-purchase timeline, and the "Christian — my middle name on FBk" signature already on record, this is now a very strong case for one person behind both accounts. No single message states the connection outright, so the two pages remain unmerged pending a dedicated review, but the identity question should be treated as close to settled rather than merely possible.
