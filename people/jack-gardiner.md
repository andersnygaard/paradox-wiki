# Jack Gardiner

**Jack Gardiner** is a Rhode Island carpenter and tradesman, a self-described "mr fix it type" who had already designed and built his own diving boat in six weeks before buying a set of [Paradox](/paradox.md) plans in December 2003.[1] Wanting the boat for coastal cruising rather than an ocean crossing, he built the stitch-and-glue Paradox **"[Jill](/boats/jill.md)"**, launched at **Gaspee Point, Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island**, on 11 September 2004, the historic site where the Providence sloop *Hannah* lured and grounded HMS *Gaspee* in 1772.[2] Full build, launch and sailing record: [boats/jill.md](/boats/jill.md).

## Steel, wood and a welder's question

Gardiner's welding and ornamental-ironwork background, with a Lincoln 110 wire-feed welder already in his shop, led him to weigh a steel hull against plywood before he began building.[3] A commercial fabrication background, including work he compared to far more complex shipyard jobs (submarine steam-loop piping, confined-space boiler-tube welding, spiral-staircase handrails), grounded his judgment that steel chine runners pose no real difficulty to fabricate at any hull size; see [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#fabricating-chine-runners-in-steel-if-the-whole-hull-is-steel).[4] He settled on building a stock Paradox in wood first, judging the stock hull's size suited steel less well than a stretched hull would. No steel-hulled Paradox has been built, by him or anyone else, and the idea remains speculative. Full account: [design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#jack-gardiners-own-resolution-a-stock-wood-paradox-first-steel-later).[5]

## Building "Jill"

He built Jill stitch-and-glue rather than to the plans' nailed construction, distrusting how flexing works a nail loose over time and lets water into a plywood's end grain; tabbing and filleting the joints sidestepped the concern entirely.[6] As a recreational diver with a larger dive boat of his own, he weighed a stretched, roughly 19 ft Paradox against the stock hull for dive-tank storage before building to the stock plans, and kept towing a small inflatable as a dive tender instead.[7]

## Approach

The tack-strut and boom-axle pivot joint he fabricated for Jill's rig was later adopted for [Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)'s own rig.[8] When the Mk2 sail appeared, he went straight to it, reasoning that Matt Layden had already worked the lessons of the original sail into the revision.[9]

His practical judgment extended past his own boat to material choices other builders faced too. He set a rudder's pivot-bolt washers flush and bedded them in epoxy, a fix that held up under years of hard motoring and motor-sailing without wear;[10] he paired plywood grade with epoxy quality, buying the best affordable of both rather than economizing on either;[11] and he treated a rudder gudgeon's material, wood and epoxy against bronze or welded steel, as a question of a builder's skill and budget rather than one right answer.[12] Full accounts: [rudder pivot-bolt fixing](/components/rudder.md#the-pivot-bolt-fixing-and-its-record-under-grounding-loads), [choosing a plywood grade](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2006-recurrence-budget-exterior-ply-and-why-green-plywood-should-be-avoided) and [rudder gudgeon materials](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#casting-a-glass-lower-rudder-gudgeon-technique).
