# Jack Gardiner

**Jack Gardiner** (`alex29@...`, Yahoo profile `tidybowlmann`) is a Rhode Island carpenter and tradesman — a self-described "mr fix it type" who had already designed and built his own diving boat in six weeks before buying a set of [Paradox](/paradox.md) plans in December 2003. He wanted the boat for coastal cruising rather than ocean crossing, joining the list at a point when six Paradoxes had already been launched. He built a stitch-and-glue Paradox of his own, **"Jill,"** and remained an active correspondent on construction and rig questions for years afterward.[1][2]

## Building "Jill"

Jack began building a stock, plans-as-drawn Paradox in wood in January 2004[3] and launched her, stitch-and-glue, as **"Jill"** at Gaspee Point, Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island, on 11 September 2004 — a deliberately chosen 9/11 anniversary date at the historic site where the Providence sloop *Hannah* lured and grounded HMS *Gaspee* in 1772.[4] He reported the boat essentially complete that September, passing the news to a UK Paradox-site maintainer keeping a running worldwide build count (see [references/paradox-uk-website.md](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#a-uk-and-worldwide-build-count)), and filmed her under sail the following summer, circulating the footage to the group.[5][6] Full build, launch, and sailing record: [boats/jill.md](/boats/jill.md).

## The steel-hull debate

Jack's distinctive contribution was opening and driving the archive's fullest debate on building the whole Paradox hull in steel rather than plywood, over several days in late December 2003 — first floating a narrower steel-hull-*bottom* idea for combined ballast and impact protection, then pressing the case for an all-steel build. He proposed adjustable, bolt-on or sliding chine runners built from lead-filled steel tube or angle iron; argued that since ballast is unneeded at the boat's ends, a steel floor need only run under the cabin; and floated using that floor to support the mast step, open up the mast bulkhead for more room, or enlarge the cockpit and cabin top. He brought practical fabrication credibility to the idea — he owned a Lincoln 110 wire-feed welder and had prior ornamental-ironwork experience — citing a finding that steel runs roughly 30-40% stronger than wood for its weight, alongside a case for fast priming/painting and spray-foam condensation control, and flagged remote repair as steel's real weak point, proposing epoxy as a stopgap and spare bolts and washers for a mechanical patch. See [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — whole-hull steel construction](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#whole-hull-steel-construction-gauge-framing-and-damage-tolerance).

Continuing the exchange over the following days, Jack settled on roughly 3/16 in plate as a target-weight compromise, framing his own priority as matching wood's *loaded* weight and center of gravity rather than bare hull weight, and proposed a fully weldable, gasketed water-tank system. He described his own welding-stress technique — tacking small sections and building up incrementally rather than heating a large area at once, since thin steel dissipates heat fast enough to avoid the stress thick-plate welding develops — and later suggested round thin-wall tubing as an alternative to square tubing for bending the compound-curved chine logs and sheer clamps. See [gauge, weight, and the case for steel over wood generally](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#gauge-weight-and-the-case-for-steel-over-wood-generally), [composite wood/steel hulls, and steel's fabrication practicalities](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#composite-woodsteel-hulls-and-steels-fabrication-practicalities), and [fabricating steel chine logs and sheer clamps](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#fabricating-steel-chine-logs-and-sheer-clamps-two-curves-at-once).

The debate drew sustained pushback on his construction choices; he held to a stated intent to keep the exchange civil, defended his fabrication answers point by point, and framed his own draw to unconventional construction as consistent with Matt Layden's own rule-breaking approach to designing Paradox in the first place. The thread closed after the archive's fullest rebuttal of the whole steel-Paradox idea, which Jack answered point by point before taking the subject private. See [answering the objection: fog, precedents, and fabrication specifics](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#answering-the-objection-fog-precedents-and-fabrication-specifics) and [Smith's rebuttal: why a steel Paradox would be "a horrible mistake"](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#smiths-rebuttal-why-a-steel-paradox-would-be-a-horrible-mistake).

Jack settled his own resolution to the whole question: build a stock, plans-as-drawn Paradox in wood first and sail it, continuing toward steel only if he liked the boat enough to justify it — reasoning that the stock hull suits steel less well than a stretched hull would, and that his winter workshop ruled out steel fabrication for the time being despite having welder friends and shop access. No steel-hulled Paradox, by Jack or anyone else, was ever built; the idea remains speculative. When the question recurred in August 2006, he summed up his standing view: the topic had been well beaten already, the stock 13'10" length is questionable for a steel build, but a larger, stretched hull would work out better.[7] See [Jack Gardiner's own resolution: a stock wood Paradox first, steel later](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#jack-gardiners-own-resolution-a-stock-wood-paradox-first-steel-later) and [a second 2006 recurrence, briefly answered](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#a-second-2006-recurrence-briefly-answered).

## Chine runners, and Jill as a reference build

Jack took the lead in answering a question about whether the chine runners' upward-curving outer edge serves a real hydrodynamic function, suggesting fabrication approaches for a metal bottom and noting that the runner's outward dimension matters more than its exact shape.[8] See [chine runners — how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#the-chine-runner-curve-cosmetic-fabrication-choice-or-hydrodynamic-feature). Drawing on a professional fabrication background that included shipyard work near Groton, CT and Quonset Point, RI, he argued a steel-hulled Paradox's chine runners posed no real difficulty to fabricate at any hull size, credited modern, affordable tools with putting steel fabrication within a backyard builder's reach, and countered a corrosion/pinhole concern directly: epoxy coating over a weld, a known backyard steel-hull practice, fills pinholes before priming and painting, with ordinary maintenance preventing the feared corrosion.[9][10] See [chine runner construction — fabricating chine runners in steel](/chine-runners/construction.md#fabricating-chine-runners-in-steel-if-the-whole-hull-is-steel).

Jill's own construction became a frequently cited reference point for later builders: stitch-and-glue with fir chine logs, tabbed and filleted rather than nailed, doubled 2 oz glass over exterior-grade fir plywood, and 12 oz biaxial cloth (rather than the plans' specified woven roving) on the bottom and chine runners — a build that took repeated beach and dock-piling groundings with no damage.[11][12][13][14] See [chine log vs. taped-seam construction — chine-log thickness](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md#chine-log-thickness-why-38-mm-rather-than-19-mm), [chine runner construction — Method 5](/chine-runners/construction.md#method-5-cardboard-patterns-and-a-block-built-wall), and [epoxy pre-coating technique](/construction/epoxy-coating-technique.md#a-builders-own-polyester-to-epoxy-conversion-and-the-case-against-poly-on-a-strip-planked-deck).

## Later contributions and advice to other builders

Jack weighed several departures from the stock cabin layout — extending the cabin forward, raising the aft hatch area, or fitting a domed hatch over the forward stateroom — before concluding that a lower floor, rather than a raised roof, better preserves the mast's ability to stow low for trailering, and settled on a secure, weathertight hatch over the forward stateroom, reachable from outside the hull, for stowing items not often needed.[15][16][17] He judged Paradox workable for an experienced amateur builder but not an ideal first project for someone without prior woodworking experience.[18] A recreational diver, he collected lost lead sinkers and weight belts locally and used the recovered lead as his own ballast, judging sand a workable but bulkier alternative, and — answering a fellow diver's question — judged a stretched, roughly 19 ft Paradox would better suit dive-tank storage, discussing the tradeoffs of towing a small inflatable as a dive tender.[19][20]

Over the following years Jack answered fellow builders on rudder pivot-bolt wear (none, after heavy motoring, with dragging over rocks rather than sand judged the real risk to the blade), plywood grades and epoxy's advantage over aging polyester, and bronze fittings as a viable upgrade over the plans' wood-and-epoxy rudder gudgeon wherever skill and budget allow.[21][22][23] See [rudder — the pivot-bolt fixing](/components/rudder.md#the-pivot-bolt-fixing-and-its-record-under-grounding-loads), [plywood grade and foam flotation debate — a 2008 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2008-recurrence-the-cost-gap-between-a-top-job-and-an-economy-build), and [rudder-bearing-materials.md](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#casting-a-glass-lower-rudder-gudgeon-technique). Answering a newcomer's ballast question in 2008, Jack judged gold would beat lead outright but any material works in a pinch, at a cost in stowage space, and recalled trimming a boat down onto her lines mid-cruise once by filling heavy plastic bags brought along with beach sand after grounding — a Paradox must be down on her lines to sail correctly.[24] See [weight, ballast, and displacement — improvised and exotic ballast materials](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#improvised-and-exotic-ballast-materials-gym-weights-sandbags-and-tungsten).

He also developed his own tack-strut/boom-axle pivot-joint fabrication method, drawn on later for the "Enuf" rig.[25] See [boats/enuf.md — the tack strut and boom axle's pivot joint](/boats/enuf.md#the-tack-strut-and-boom-axles-pivot-joint). On rig handling in a blow, carrying too much sail in a higher wind makes a Paradox want to round up sharply into the wind, corrected simply by reefing.[26] See [sailing characteristics and rig balance](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#weather-helm-strengthens-with-wind-and-sail-carried-and-reefing-is-the-standard-fix). He once backed his truck's drive wheels over Jill's own rudder while freeing her from a beach, crushing it, and simply rebuilt it stronger.[27]

Looking back after four years of building and sailing Jill, Jack judged her stitch-and-glue, all-fiberglass construction had held up well, and added a boltrope to the sail's leading edge to improve its trim; full account at [boats/jill.md](/boats/jill.md#a-four-year-durability-report-and-the-sails-boltrope-revisited).[28]
