# Matthew E. Noonan

**Matthew E. Noonan** is a builder from **Ithaca, New York**, in the Finger Lakes country south of Lake Ontario.[1] He began building a [Paradox](/paradox.md) from scratch in June 2006, after already completing two Yost-designed folding kayaks and a PDRacer, the most complex of the four builds by his own reckoning.[2] He moved that fall to a house on Cayuga Lake with little room to build there, and treated the summer as a race to get as much of the hull done beforehand, with two brothers due to help in July.[2] He kept a public daily build log through his first week, an unusually granular record for a stage most builders compress into a sentence: see [Matthew E. Noonan's Paradox build log](/references/matthew-noonan-build-log.md).

## A week logged day by day

His build log runs day by day from the plans' arrival on 6 June 2006 through Day 7 on 16 June, covering the build's earliest days in more detail than survives anywhere else.[3] The plans came from [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc.md) with a CD of photographs, hints and material lists, and six gallons of epoxy were ordered the same day from a New Hampshire supplier chosen on price.[3] Plywood and lumber were delivered free three days later by a lumberyard at Groton, New York, and the first day's work was as much shopping as building, a circular saw, jigsaw blades stout enough for the thick plywood and sawhorses doing double duty as a kayak rack, before bulkhead 1 was cut and bulkhead 4 drawn; the remaining bulkheads, the transom and the side panels followed within two more days.[3] Laying out that sheet produced the build's one real puzzle: a side panel, the transom and bulkhead 2 would not fit as drawn, and the plans' own alternate layout for the sheet did not resolve it either, solved instead by moving bulkhead 2's butt joint, the same fix [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s construction booklet had already recommended. Full account at [panel layout, tight spots builders hit](/construction/panel-layout.md#tight-spots-builders-hit).[4] The rest of the week went into deck-beam stock: mahogany ripped freehand into strips a couple of millimetres thick for the laminated beams, a bending jig built to curve two beams at once, and the side-panel scarphs cut by a stepped method, sawing to each glue line then chiselling and sanding the waste away. Full account at [scarf joints, a 2006 stepped scarph](/construction/scarf-joints.md#a-2006-stepped-scarph-cut-to-each-glue-line-and-chiselled-back).[5]

## Building the hull

By late July 2006, with his two brothers' help, the bottom panel was wired on and glued, its scarf joint coming out cleaner than the side panels', which had been done earlier on a flat work surface, and fitting out the interior and the vent trunk followed. A laminated panel's own bonding area so far exceeds a scarf or butt joint's that the joint itself barely matters for strength, a technique documented in full at [plywood grades and foam flotation, a double-layer technique](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-double-layer-plywood-technique-for-an-enlarged-paradox-and-testing-an-unconventional-mast-wood).[6] With the chine runners underway that August, the placement of the copper lightning-grounding flashing in the layup was resolved, with turning the hull over next; full account, including copper's bonus as an antifoulant, at [lightning protection, where the copper flashing sits in the layup](/design/lightning-protection.md#where-the-copper-flashing-sits-in-the-layup-and-coppers-bonus-as-an-antifoulant).[7] Building to the stock plans, he was recorded still building at Ithaca in an October 2014 census of Paradox builders and owners.[8]
