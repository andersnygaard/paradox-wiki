# Matthew E. Noonan

**Matthew E. Noonan** (Yahoo handle `twitch_816`, signing his messages "Matt") is a Paradox builder from the Ithaca, New York area, on Cayuga Lake, who introduced himself to the list in June 2006 as a "long-time lurker" finally starting to build, about two weeks before a fellow builder launched his own Paradox, ["Enuf"](/boats/enuf.md).[1][2] He had already completed three other small boats — two Yost-designed folding kayaks and a PDRacer — and judged Paradox by far the most complex of the four.[2] Moving to a house on Cayuga Lake that fall, with limited room to build at the new property, he treated the summer of 2006 as a race to get as much of the hull built as possible beforehand, with two brothers due to help in July.[2] He was in the area for graduate studies in mathematics at Cornell, in no particular rush to graduate, in a part of central New York shaped by retreating glaciers into gorges, waterfalls, and deep lakes, about two hours south of Lake Ontario and on the shore of the 40-mile-long Cayuga Lake.[3] He took his doctorate there in 2010.[11] He kept a public daily build log through his first week — see [Matthew E. Noonan's Paradox build log](/references/matthew-noonan-build-log.md) — alongside a page describing his three prior boats.[2]

The "long-time lurker" description was accurate but not quite complete: he had already posted to the list once, in July 2005, answering a question about BitTorrent clients on a Mac — eleven months before he said a word about building a boat.[12]

## The first week

His own build log covers 6–16 June 2006 day by day, four of those entries falling before his first message to the list.[13] The **plans arrived on 6 June** from [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc.md), together with a CD carrying photographs, hints and material lists, and six gallons of epoxy were ordered the same day from a New Hampshire supplier chosen on price.[13] **Plywood and lumber were delivered free** three days later by a lumberyard at Groton, New York, and the first day's work was as much shopping as building — a circular saw, jigsaw blades stout enough for the thick plywood, and sawhorses that doubled as a kayak rack — before bulkhead #1 was cut out and bulkhead #4 drawn.[13]

**All four bulkheads, the transom and the side panels were cut within three days.** Laying them out produced the one problem of the week: the sheet carrying a side panel, the transom and bulkhead #2 would not fit as drawn, and the alternate layout supplied on the plans CD would not work either — resolved by moving bulkhead #2's butt joint, a fix he found afterwards that [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s construction booklet had already recommended. Full detail at [panel layout and nesting](/construction/panel-layout.md#tight-spots-builders-hit).[14]

The rest of the week went on **deck-beam stock and scarphs**. Mahogany left over from an older project was ripped freehand into strips only a couple of millimetres thick — six of them making up the beam for bulkhead #1 — a cut he judged impossible without the saw's laser guide, having been unable to buy a rip guide with it.[13] A **bending jig** for the beams followed, built of plywood blocks epoxied to a board and made to bend two beams at once; the board was the rudder blade's own, to be chiselled clear and sanded back to plywood afterwards, a shortcut he recorded at the time as possibly more hassle than it was worth.[13] The side-panel scarphs were cut by the stepped method described at [scarf joints](/construction/scarf-joints.md#a-2006-stepped-scarph-cut-to-each-glue-line-and-chiselled-back), after a first attempt with a badly made jig came out poorly.[15] By **Day 7 the beams were clamped up in the jig**, most of the side-bin cleats were on the bulkheads, and the vent-trunk cleats were in place.[13]

## Building the hull

By late July 2006, with his two brothers' help, he had wired on and glued his hull's bottom panel — its scarf joint came out cleaner than the side panels' had, done earlier on a flat work surface — and moved on to fitting out the interior and the vent trunk.[4] A laminated, double-layer panel's own bonding area so far exceeds a scarf or butt joint's that the joint itself barely matters for strength; full technical detail at [a double-layer plywood technique for an enlarged Paradox](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-double-layer-plywood-technique-for-an-enlarged-paradox-and-testing-an-unconventional-mast-wood).[4] In early August 2006, with the chine runners underway, the placement of the copper lightning-grounding flashing in the hull's layup was resolved, exposed copper's antifouling benefit was confirmed, and turning the hull over was named as his next milestone — full detail at [lightning protection — where the copper flashing sits in the layup, and copper's bonus as an antifoulant](/design/lightning-protection.md#where-the-copper-flashing-sits-in-the-layup-and-coppers-bonus-as-an-antifoulant).[5][6][7]

## No recorded outcome

The **rollover named as his next step in August 2006 is the last thing recorded of the build.** His message of 16 August 2006 is his final one in the archive; nothing further from him appears in the remaining nineteen years of the list.[10] The build log stops at Day 7 and was never continued. When [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md) compiled his worldwide census in October 2014, the entry was still "In build," with no boat name — the last fixed point on record.[16] Whether the boat was ever finished is not documented anywhere in the archive.

## Contributions and stories

- **Pigment color and UV protection.** Asking whether the pigment in his hull's epoxy protected it from UV on its own, or whether paint or varnish was needed over it as well — and half in jest, why so many green-hulled, red-sailed Paradoxen came from the UK side of the Atlantic — he drew out durable Paradox lore: a supplier's pigment alone was said to prevent UV degradation with no paint needed over it (see [Enuf's paint scheme](/boats/enuf.md#paint-scheme-three-tone-green-with-a-yellow-bottom) for the fuller account and its later complication), "forest green" was corrected to **British Racing Green**, and the red sail was explained as a deliberate high-visibility choice — a boat easy to spot in genuine trouble at sea, but inconspicuous with the sail down while creeping up rivers.[3][8]
- **A PDRacer as a quicker alternative.** He suggested building a PDRacer as a quicker alternative to a proposed scaled-down "mini" Paradox. See [a scaled-down "mini" Paradox (speculative)](/design/ideas/scaled-down-idea.md#reception-a-comparison-to-the-bolger-brick-and-a-cheaper-and-quicker-alternative).[9]
- **Sail sleeves versus lashings.** He relayed a question about why the Paradox sail uses sleeves rather than lashings on the yard and boom. See [why the sail uses sleeves rather than lashings, and a first sail of the 2006 season](/boats/jill.md#why-the-sail-uses-sleeves-rather-than-lashings-and-a-first-sail-of-the-2006-season).[10]
- **What he was reading.** His log records, in the same week, which Paradox sources a newcomer of mid-2006 actually had in front of him: [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s construction booklet, [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s newly published [Duckworks](/references/duckworks-magazine.md) article on building "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)," [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own Enigma build pages, and the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) photographs — the last of these supplying the photograph of Paradox and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) that heads his log.[15][13]
