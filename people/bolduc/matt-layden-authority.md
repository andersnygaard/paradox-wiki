# The Bolducs: Matt Layden firsthand record

*Part of the [Bolduc](/people/bolduc.md) record; see also [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md) and [Mindy Bolduc](/people/bolduc/mindy.md).*

The Bolducs have **one of the longest and closest personal relationships with Matt Layden recorded in the archive**. Dave met Matt in **1992**, bought a Layden boat that same year, and the couple went on to own three of his designs and to cruise the Bahamas in his company for weeks at a time: eating aboard his Paradox, baking bread on its stove, sculling it under his coaching and discussing each of his boats with the designer himself. Where others met Matt once or sailed a single day alongside him, the Bolducs' firsthand knowledge of the man, his boats and his methods runs across two decades, and much of what is known of Matt Layden's own boatbuilding and cruising practice comes from them.

## How they found him: two magazine photographs, and a 1,300-mile drive

The relationship began with a **caption**. Two black-and-white magazine photographs of a miniature yacht (fifteen feet, lug-rigged, with a large pilothouse) caught the couple's attention in **February 1991**; the caption named the boat's owner as Matt Layden of Connecticut and noted he had spent the fall working his way slowly up to Cape Cod for the winter. Intrigued enough by the design to make contact, they then drove **1,300 miles round trip** to meet him.[1]

What they learned on that visit is the earliest firsthand picture the archive holds of Matt Layden's life afloat, and by Dave's own account: Matt had by then spent **three years cruising aboard the flat-bottomed sharpie he called home**; he had sailed as far north as the **Bay of Fundy**, with its forty-foot tides; and **on an earlier boat he had survived a capsize in the Gulf Stream** while bound for the Bahamas, an incident recorded nowhere else in the archive. All of it had been done, by the same account, **entirely under sail and oar**, and he took pride in covering **up to 50 miles a day** without trouble in what was, as he told it, the **third such boat he had built**. Close to the Bolducs in age and sharing many of their interests, he corresponded with them over the following year; the 1992 trip north was to collect the boat itself.[1] See [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md).

The sequence carries a dating wrinkle. A first meeting after the February 1991 photographs, followed by a year of correspondence and then the 1992 purchase trip, implies the couple first met Matt Layden in **1991** rather than 1992; their own website says plainly "I met Matt Layden in 1992."[2] Both are first-party accounts by the same author, the later one written more than twenty years after the fact; the discrepancy is recorded rather than resolved, and the archive's established 1992 dating is left standing below.

## Meeting Matt Layden, 1992

Dave met Matt Layden in 1992, struck by how far outside convention he lived: "he seemed really out there... cruising full-time in these little dinky home-built boats, and he was having a blast doing it."[2] That meeting turned Dave toward micro-cruising for good; he bought [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), a Layden design "very similar to Paradox," that same year.[2] Having examined most of Matt's boats in person and talked with the designer at length, Dave became one of the few firsthand authorities on Layden's whole body of work rather than the Paradox alone.[3]

## First time aboard the original Paradox

Dave and Mindy's first time aboard [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own original Paradox came in Beaufort, NC, a stop consistent with Matt's early passage south down the Intracoastal Waterway from Connecticut (see [original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md)), and left the couple "duly impressed," predating even their own 1994 joint Bahamas cruise alongside Matt in his boat.[4]

## Owning three Layden designs

Over the following two decades all three of the Bolducs' own Layden boats came from Matt personally, in sequence: [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) in 1992, [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) (which Dave rebuilt, rated the best-sailing of all Matt's designs[3][5]) and, later still, Matt's own [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md). The one cost figure here that is actually about Matt rather than about the Bolducs' own boats is his original Paradox: about **$2,000** in materials and three months of full-time labor, the benchmark the couple's other purchases were measured against.[5] Full ownership history, cost figures and voyages for each of the three hulls, including the Bolducs' own hardest passage aboard Little Cruiser, a 1993 overnight gale, and Dave's 2013 solo Bahamas crossing aboard Enigma, now live on their own pages.

## Cruising the Bahamas in Matt's company

The Bolducs made roughly seven winter Bahamas crossings aboard Little Cruiser, averaging about **800 miles** over two-and-a-half-month cruises, several of them sailed in company with Matt Layden himself.[6] On the **1994** joint cruise, with Matt sailing the original Paradox alongside Little Cruiser, the two boats kept company through the Exumas; at a Warderick Wells potluck **Mindy and Matt baked two loaves of bread on Paradox's tiny kerosene stove**.[7] Cruising together, the group cooked aboard Little Cruiser and all three (Dave, Mindy and Matt) ate aboard Paradox: a Layden cabin holds "a few close friends aboard for short stints," the space "similar to that of being in a tent."[8] The Bolducs' photographs from these cruises capture Matt's own life afloat: Matt and Mindy aboard Paradox, and Matt with a then-companion sailing Paradox in the Bahamas.[8][9] Across some two months sailing alongside Matt, Dave learned the tides, wind shifts and trim of the cruising grounds directly from him.[10] A particularly windy day during the 1994 Bimini leg of that cruise (judged from the state of the trees in the Bolducs' own photographs) is preserved in two surviving microcruising.com images: Paradox under sail, and the two boats together in the Bahamas.[11]

## Colorblind in a shipping lane - the 1994 night crossing

On the 1994 joint cruise, following Matt's boat after dark on a crossing to the Bahamas, Little Cruiser found herself between **two ships converging from opposite directions**. Remembering mid-crossing that Matt was colorblind and could not read the ships' navigation lights, Dave broke off from following him and held station instead; the two ships passed close enough that their sides were visible from the cockpit, in what the couple called the most frightened they have ever been on the water. Their own verdict afterward was that they had been wrong to second-guess a far more experienced sailor and should have followed him regardless.

By the couple's own account, Matt Layden is colorblind and cannot tell red from green: a fact that surfaces nowhere else in the archive, and one they learned only because it mattered at sea that night. It stands as the archive's only account of a physical limitation of the designer's affecting how his boats were sailed.[12]

## Techniques and figures carried back from Matt

Because they sailed with Matt and handled his boats, the Bolducs relayed a great deal of the designer's own practice for other builders:

- **Sculling technique.** Visiting Matt in spring 2003, Dave sculled the original Paradox himself under Matt's coaching, which emphasized whole-body technique. Paradox's new sculling oar (straighter and wider-bladed than the plans') was more powerful but demanded so much more wrist action that it hurt; Matt's own wrist had hurt for the first few days of the Everglades Challenge with the new oar.[13] See [sculling and yuloh technique](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md).
- **Quantified chine-runner sea trial.** Matt Layden's detailed VMG/VMGW sea-trial data for a modified chine runner reached the microcruising list and from there the builders' group, giving quantified windward numbers for the design.[14] See [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md).
- **The February 2003 sail.** Having sailed the original Paradox himself, Dave gave the only outside verdict on the new sail Matt cut in February 2003: it "sets very nicely."[15] See [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md).
- **Wintering in the ice.** Dave confirmed Matt's story of freezing Little Cruiser into a Cape Cod saltwater pond, finding (years later, under stripped paint) bow-wave-shaped cuts up forward that showed Matt had sailed her out through the ice; he also adopted Matt's foam-in-the-windows trick, which keeps the boat cooler when hot, warmer when cold and cuts condensation.[16] See [cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md).
- **Sculling endurance.** The clearest measure of the gap between the designer and everyone else: Matt could **scull eight, nine or ten hours** at a stretch, where the Bolducs (still calling themselves beginners at it after years of practice) could move Little Cruiser comfortably at about 2 knots but not all day. Matt's WaterTribe racing is offered as the proof that a small boat can be sculled efficiently as primary propulsion; the lesson drawn for everyone else is more modest, that an ordinary sailor should at least be able to scull a boat a few hundred yards clear of danger.[17][18] See [sculling and yuloh technique](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md).
- **Entering an inlet at night by ear.** Sailing with Matt taught the Bolducs to make a night landfall **by listening rather than looking**: picking out where the seas are breaking along the shore and steering for the gap where they are not, which is the channel. The technique was used in earnest on the 2006 Outer Banks cruise, when a jump of sixty-odd miles ran out of daylight and the couple felt their way into an opening perhaps thirty feet across, aiming for the quietest water; it is the kind of thing, they judged, one would never attempt in a larger boat in water that shallow.[19]
- **Build history**, told in full at [the build and launch of the original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md).[3]

## Matt Layden as the Bolducs knew him

The Bolducs are also among the steadiest witnesses to Matt Layden's character. They record that Matt stayed **private and would not join the mailing list**, leaving the couple to relay his views by proxy.[20] Matt was thought of as "a modern-day Thoreau," someone who "quit his job, built a small boat, and went sailing off into the sunset to find Paradise," a characterization of the designer's ethos rather than a biographical fact.[21] And, having been asked several times over the years to draw a larger sharpie without success, the Bolducs nonetheless kept faith in him: "Still you never know... Matt's the kind of person who accomplishes amazing things quickly when he is in the mood."[22]

Asked to describe the man on the air in 2006, the couple settled on **"a very quiet and unpretentious type of fellow"** who is nonetheless "extremely knowledgeable" and "an excellent seaman," and, more plainly still, "our hero and the ultimate sailor." Two months in his company on the 1994 cruise, they said, brought them up to speed both in general sailing skill and in how to handle this particular kind of boat.[23][24] The privacy already on record is qualified in the same interview: Matt is, in the couple's words, a genuinely **solitary person who enjoys his time alone**, yet still values the camaraderie of the other competitors enough to keep entering WaterTribe races.[25] His stamina under way is described from the outside exactly as his own race tactics describe it: he takes **short catnaps and can then go steady for a very long time**.[24]

Two of Matt's positions put him at odds with ordinary practice. He shared the Bolducs' long reluctance to carry an **EPIRB**, the two having discussed at length whether it is worth risking a rescuer's life to answer another sailor's own foolhardiness or bad judgment; neither carried one for years, and the Bolducs relented only later and only because they had each other's lives to answer for, Dave's stated view being that a sailor voyaging alone is entitled to make the opposite choice.[26] And Matt's boats drew official attention that theirs did not: by Dave's recollection, he was **stopped by the Coast Guard several times**, particularly on the voyage made in the twelve-foot [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md), while the Bolducs in a fifteen-footer were taken for day sailors and never questioned.[27]

## The engine question, and a photograph sent to Sven Yrvind

The sharpest illustration of Matt Layden's engine ethos in the Bolducs' record does not come from Matt at all: it comes from what **[Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md)** said when Dave sent him a photograph of [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md). Yrvind's reply approved of everything in the picture except one thing: **"I like everything except for that motor back there. You're not sending a good message."** Dave took the point (these boats are meant to be driven by human or wind power) and put a version of it to Matt, observing that sailors who tow their boats to Florida behind cars are burning fossil fuel to go sailing either way. Matt, who by Dave's account **admired much of Yrvind's work**, afterward accepted a lift to the start of the race but **sailed [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) home** at the end of it under her own rig. Dave was careful to disclaim any causation ("I don't know if it had any influence on him") while adding that the choice was typical of Matt regardless: offered any way of doing something under human or wind power, he much prefers it to a combustion engine, believing in "a more holistic way to live."[28] See [Matt Layden, "motors are fine as long as..."](/matt-layden/biography.md#motors-are-fine-as-long-as-matts-engine-philosophy). The photograph itself, and what it says about Little Cruiser's own equipment, is at [Little Cruiser: systems and fit-out](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md#systems-and-fit-out).
