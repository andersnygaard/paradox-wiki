# Tony Barlow

**Tony Barlow** built and launched **"[Miko](/boats/miko.md)"** at **Phuket, Thailand**, in **March 2024** — the archive's only [Paradox](/paradox.md) built and sailed in tropical Asia.[1] He is also the archive's plainest cautionary case on epoxy: during the build he developed a **severe epoxy allergy** and can never use the material again, a fact that has shaped every plan he has made since.[2]

## The build, and the allergy

Miko was built to the stock design with a handful of tropical adaptations, and launched with the wry note that there would be no second epoxy boat: "Next time Aluminium, me and epoxy can't be in the same town."[1] Aluminium proved expensive to import into Thailand, so when he began costing a **20 ft next boat** he canvassed the group for a low-cost hull material usable **without epoxy at all**, weighing **vinylester and polyester resin** and settling on the view that his answer is **foam rather than plywood**, since foam is compatible with both — his doubt about polyester being not the resin itself, which builds most of the world's fibreglass boats, but its adhesion to plywood.[2] He has separately said he would like to build a **second Paradox 20% larger, in aluminium**, and follows [Alex McGavin](/people/alex-mcgavin.md)'s welded hulls closely for exactly that reason.[3]

His one recorded construction shortcut is worth keeping: rather than bend a long timber for the **chine runners**, he shaped **sections about a foot long**, epoxied them in place and faired them with a grinder and sander — the same problem other builders solved by lamination or by cutting plywood to shape (see [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md)).[4]

## Fitting her out for Phuket

Miko is powered as much as sailed, and Barlow says so without apology: light winds are the Phuket norm, so she carries **two outboards** — a working motor and a back-up for longer trips — "Would sail the entire way if I could."[5] Earlier he built an **electric trolling-motor installation** copied from another owner's boat: the motor's shaft cut down, its control head relocated **under the tiller** with the wiring led through a hose in a transom hole, an outboard bracket modified by removing the springs and reversing two arms so it lifts and drops smoothly, and a **100 Ah lithium battery** housed between bulkhead 4 and the transom — installed with the explicit intention of removing it again if his sailing and yuloh skills made it unnecessary.[6] The same set of photographs introduced a **stainless-steel rudder gudgeon** (his epoxy casting having come out full of bubbles) and an alternative to the sliding hatch that frees deck area for **more solar panels**.[6]

For the tropics he has fitted **air conditioning** — a Cybertake S1 Pro running about **100 W in eco mode**[7] — and has worked on a **seat that stays comfortable when the boat is heeled**, a recurring small-boat complaint he put to the group for better ideas.[8] He also raised a plans question nobody else in the archive had asked directly: why the **boom sits to port of the mast** when the mast is already offset to port, and whether it could go to starboard instead.[9]
