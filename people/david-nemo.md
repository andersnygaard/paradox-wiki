# David Nemo

**David Nemo** is a UK builder who built an unnamed **28 ft by 8 ft live-aboard Paradox** by translating every measurement on Matt Layden's stock plans to double scale, against the stock 13 ft 10 in.[1] By January 2009 the hull, decks and sides were already built and glassed, with some £8,000 spent on materials and tools.[1] She was complete and sailing by December 2009, having crossed the English Channel and set off, under electric power, via France's Canal du Midi for the Mediterranean.[2] The design question his build raised, whether Paradox's qualities survive enlargement at all, is treated in full at [design/larger-paradox.md](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-far-larger-unsanctioned-build-doubling-every-stock-dimension-for-a-live-aboard), including the "do not scale" doctrine it ran against; the full technical account of the boat itself is at [boats/david-nemos-paradox.md](/boats/david-nemos-paradox.md).

## Building at double scale

Her construction departed from the stock plans throughout. Divinycell foam core replaced plywood for the sides and decks, with a thin bonded Divinycell lining under the plywood bottom for insulation and extra flotation. The framing was built from reclaimed hardwood salvaged from a church, joined with peg-and-hole, mortise-and-tenon and dovetail joints, then double-bonded with fibreglass and resin, and the chine runners were hand-carved from solid oak, hollowed and filled with cast lead ballast before being glassed into the hull, combining ballast and lateral plane in one member.[1] The hull kept the stock skeg variation, with a small open cockpit added aft, the one departure visible from outside and the detail by which she was later recognised on the water as a scaled-up Paradox.[1][2] The full build account is at [boats/david-nemos-paradox.md](/boats/david-nemos-paradox.md).

## Wintering aboard mid-build

For months in early 2009 he lived in a van in an old yard far from anywhere, with little contact with the outside world. He sold the van to raise cash for the build and moved aboard the half-finished hull itself, sheltering under insulated canvas through a difficult winter while construction continued.[3]
