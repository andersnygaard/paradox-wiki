# David Nemo

**David Nemo** is a UK builder who built the largest Paradox scale-up documented anywhere in the archive: a **28 ft x 8 ft live-aboard**, arrived at by translating every measurement on Matt Layden's stock plans to double scale, against the stock 13'10".[1] He introduced himself to the group in January 2009 with the hull already built, decks and sides glassed, and roughly **£8,000** spent on materials and tools — not as a proposal but as a report on work substantially done.[1] By December 2009 the boat was confirmed finished and under way, having crossed the English Channel and headed for the Mediterranean via France's Canal du Midi.[6] The boat is never named anywhere in the record.

The design question his build raised — whether Paradox's qualities survive enlargement at all, and the standing "do not scale" doctrine it ran against — is treated in full at [design/larger-paradox.md](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-far-larger-unsanctioned-build-doubling-every-stock-dimension-for-a-live-aboard). This page records the build itself.

## The build

The boat follows the stock plans' skeg variation, with a small open cockpit added aft — the one departure visible from outside, and the detail by which she was later recognized on the water as a scaled-up Paradox.[1][6] Her construction is not the plans' construction at all:[1]

- **Sides and decks** are **Divinycell foam core** rather than plywood.
- The **bottom** stays plywood, but carries a thin bonded Divinycell inner lining for insulation and extra flotation.
- **Framing** is reclaimed **church hardwood**, worked with peg-and-hole, mortise-and-tenon, and dovetail joints, then double-bonded with fibreglass and resin.
- The **chine runners** are hand-carved solid oak, hollowed and filled with cast lead ballast before being glassed into the hull — ballast and lateral plane combined in one member.

Nemo put the design question to the group himself: whether doubling the design's size would let her float and sail as intended, and whether she would retain any of the stock boat's qualities.[1]

## The answers it drew

The reply that engaged most closely with the numbers came from a correspondent working through what scaling actually does to a hull: sail balance (a lug sail overlapping the mast by about a quarter rather than a fifth of its foot) and rudder balance (a leading edge set 3-4 in ahead of the pivot line) would both ease tiller and mainsheet load at this size; structural loads scale with the **cube** of a linear dimension rather than its square, making a doubled hull effectively about four times the original in the dimensions that matter for masts and spars — an argument for a tabernacle-mounted, counterbalanced mast. Low length-to-beam-ratio hulls can whip port to starboard at large sizes, a problem Paradox's short length avoids regardless of scale, though hand-holds inside an open cabin are still worth having; a way back aboard given the freeboard, and a plan for propulsion in a calm since rowing a boat this size is impractical, both needed answers, and the added windage and mass called for strong ground tackle and strong fenders.[3] See [similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#phil-bolgers-black-skimmer-and-martha-jane) for the Bolger AS29 comparison the same reply drew on.

A second reply endorsed proceeding, the hull being already finished, while noting a narrower hull — around 6 ft — would have sailed better, and predicting the chine runners would need more ballast to get down far enough to be effective at this size.[2] A third gave the practical launching method: start with a great deal of ballast aboard for the first sail and reduce it progressively until she performs well — "that is the way of the Paradox," and the more so at large scale.[4] See [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast) for that method stated generally.

## Building through a winter aboard

Returning to the group in March 2009 after a silence, Nemo explained he had been living in a van in an old yard far from anywhere, with virtually no contact with technology. He had since sold the van to raise cash for the build and moved onto the half-finished boat itself, sheltering under insulated canvas through difficult winter weather. The build was going very well despite the conditions, and he expected to answer the outstanding design questions once he had a new laptop and, hopefully, a digital camera.[5]

## Completed, and bound for the Mediterranean

In December 2009 a fellow sailor who had just crossed the English Channel aboard the finished boat gave the archive its confirmation that she was complete and sailing. He described the completed hull as looking exactly like a scaled-up Paradox with a rear cockpit added, and the build as "a real miracle." She was under way — slowly, on electric power — bound via France's Canal du Midi for the Mediterranean.[6] This is the archive's last word on the boat; no name, photographs, or specifications were ever posted.
