# Stephen Barwell

**Stephen Barwell** (Yahoo handle `surfgarden`) is building a Paradox, "**[SaSa](/boats/sasa.md)**," on North Stradbroke Island, Queensland, Australia, begun 9 June 2013 — his first piece of woodwork since a school shoe rack made 47 years earlier.[1] By early August 2013 he had shared photographs of the boat's progress, including a curved transom and a planned double-tiller self-steering arrangement.[1][2] Full build record: [SaSa](/boats/sasa.md).

## Hosting "the Jollybod," a shallow-water sailing event

Barwell also runs **"the Jollybod,"** a biannual (winter and summer) sailing, paddling, and "stealth" event on North Stradbroke Island open to any vessel able to start and finish in three feet of water, with a raft-up the day before the main event. In April 2016 he invited a fellow Paradox builder, then musing about a winter cruise north toward Queensland, to the sixth running that July, offering to make him and his boat welcome if he made it.[3] The invitation was warmly received, with a promise of a sail aboard the visiting boat in return if the trip came off.[4]

## Speculative junk-rig sail shapes

Floated two untried ideas for reconciling a junk rig's aft-leading sheets with Paradox's own far-aft sail position: a sail cut wider at the head than the foot, with the boom shorter than the yard, and, separately, a sail cut narrow at both boom and yard but widest across the middle, the kind of shape that gives some junk rigs their curved leech. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#answers-to-the-heeling-objection-leeway-without-heel-but-a-sheeting-conflict-remains).[5][6] Returning to the same thread the next day, floated two further untried ideas — lazyjacks used for reefing instead of roller furling, to eliminate the creases furling causes, and a way of laying the whole dropped rig over to lie alongside the cabin, cutting windage at rest — and promised to raise the windage question with dedicated junk-rig forums for a wider opinion.[7] Restated the case for the conversion on cost grounds days later: a plain jib for a 14 ft dinghy costs around AUD 600 by comparison, and two thousand years of continuous use as a working rig was offered as its own kind of proof, with the standing lug's own furling and chafe troubles pointed to as the design's persistent weak point. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#further-sheeting-fixes-a-trimarans-cockpit-only-rig-and-the-cost-case-for-conversion).[8] Named Arne Kverneland — the same designer behind THU's own cambered-sail method — as a well-known champion of the cambered junk rig within the wider Junk Rig Association community, recalling a skeptic's jibe that improving junk-sail performance is "like putting a spoiler on a tractor," and Kverneland's own reply as demolishing it.[9] Noticing Eerik Mee's own design questions being answered on the Junk Rig Association's technical forum, in a thread Mee had titled "Building a junkrigged Matt Leyden's Paradox," remarked it was no wonder Mee was on top of the junk rig's problems — "wonderful stuff."[10]
