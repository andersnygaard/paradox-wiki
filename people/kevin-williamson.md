# Kevin Williamson

**Kevin Williamson** is a Paradox owner in **Perth, Western Australia**, and the second owner of the junk-rigged [Paradox](/paradox.md) **"[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)"** — bought from her builder **[Roselt Croeser](/people/roselt-croeser.md)** in July 2023 and renamed **"Skye."**[1] He had intended to build his own: he ordered plans after a first visit to Croeser, then went back for a second look, "and a delightful couple of hours sailing we struck a deal and I became the new custodian of his Paradox."[1] What he has done with her since makes him the archive's practical authority on the awkward part of a [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md) on a **trailered** boat.

## Making a junk rig trailerable

Williamson likes the junk rig — it is "really easy to use" — but named its real cost plainly: the lowered sail is far bulkier than a rolled lug sail, and if the mast has to come out for bridges there are "a lot of lines to disconnect and keep tidy whilst trying to lift out the mast. Then re-rig it all afterwards. Trying to do it on the water could be a nightmare."[2] His answer was a **folding mast**, which drops the whole rig onto the cabin top and raises it again **without unrigging anything**; he only has to slack the aft lazy jacks so the aft end of the sail stack can settle and the pressure comes off the lower pin. He notes the lower mast section could be **epoxied in place and sealed at the deck**, removing a water-ingress point.[2]

The unintended consequence is the more useful finding. Because the folding mast can simply be left rigged, **trailer to sailing now takes only a few minutes — quicker than most conventionally rigged boats**, which inverts the usual objection that a junk rig is too fiddly for a trailer-sailer.[3]

## The mast extension

In **November 2023** he **extended the mast** from **3.72 m to 4.43 m above deck** — an added **86 cm**, of which **22.5 cm** sleeves over the old masthead, after shaving a couple of millimetres off the top to fit. Moving the halyard to a **quicklink on the crane**, instead of a line tied 10 cm below the old masthead, gained a further 150 mm. The result leaves about **30 cm clear above the cabin top** for forward visibility and lets him carry **full sail**; Croeser measured the enlarged sail at about **8.3 m²**.[4] On the first proper sail he found the difference obvious in photographs even when it was hard to judge close up, with a little bagginess remaining in the bottom panel — probably curable by easing the rear lazy jacks — and a **top speed of 4.8 knots** on a downwind run along the beach.[5] He credits **John Hyndman** and **Graeme Kenyon** for the input and ideas behind the modification.[4]

## Cruising the Swan River

In **May 2024** Williamson made his **first overnight cruise**, from the furthest upstream ramp at **Guildford down the Swan River to Fremantle and back** — about 19 miles each way, planned to ride the easterlies downstream and the afternoon sea breeze back up, with the option of continuing through the port south to Rockingham for another 16–17 miles. He launched in the afternoon, used the **trolling motor** to pass a road bridge, a railway bridge and a low power line, and tied up for the night **between two fallen tree limbs** on a 60 m-wide reach of river, cooking on a **metho burner** with collapsible cookware, a fire extinguisher to hand, and the fold-out table in use for the first time. He had to lower the mast twice more before the city centre. Calm and then persistent westerlies ended the attempt — he drifted at under half a knot and turned back — but he counted the time well spent for what it taught him about **camping aboard**.[6][7]

## Practical fittings and advice

Two recommendations recur in his advice to builders still working. He would fit a **deck hatch forward of the mast** and a **6" screw-in inspection port** ahead of bulkhead 1, because the forward compartment is very hard to reach from inside through the small access hole, and planned to add both to his own boat.[8] And on **non-skid**: "On a Paradox you just cant have too much non slip" — the whole topside needs it, since the deck is tiny and the boat is at its least stable exactly when your weight is furthest off centre, heeling a long way without tipping over, which is "quite unsettling until you get used to it."[9]
