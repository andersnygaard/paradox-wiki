# Glen C. Maxwell — Matt Layden eyewitness record

*Part of the [Glen C. Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md) record.*

**Glen Maxwell** is a firsthand eyewitness of designer **Matt Layden** under sail: the one builder to sail his own Paradox side by side with Matt's. Maxwell sailed two Paradoxes together with Matt at Ft. Desoto in the days before the 2003 Everglades Challenge, the first time two Paradoxes had ever sailed in company[1], met him again in person at the 2003 Cedar Key Messabout,[2] watched him win that race, and in 2005 sailed a second of Matt's boats, Enigma, alongside Matt and his wife Karen, preserving Matt's own techniques and words along the way.[3] (For the deepest and longest-running firsthand relationship with Matt Layden, meeting him in 1992, owning his boats, and cruising the Bahamas in his company, see the [Bolducs' Matt Layden firsthand record](/people/bolduc/matt-layden-authority.md). For Maxwell's own Atlantic crossing and later voyaging, which is not part of the Layden record, see [Glen C. Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md).)

## Sailing alongside Matt Layden at Ft. Desoto and Cedar Key

In March 2003, days before the Everglades Challenge, Maxwell sailed "Zoë" alongside Matt Layden's own original Paradox at Ft. Desoto, the first time two Paradoxes had ever sailed together, "a first for Matt as well." Matt's windward ability proved far superior: on the return beat he delivered "a butt kicking to windward that I won't soon forget."[1] Matt noticed Glen's own sail (cut too flat, its yard bowing and needing about 2½ in of crescent at the top) and, sailing his own sails cut and re-cut by hand, suggested Glen do the same rather than order a replacement.[4] He also sketched out the haul-out roller rig Matt used to beach his boat single-handed: two sets of 6 in x 2 in boat rollers on trestles, stowed in the starboard bilge, each trailing a cleated retrieval line so it would not drift away in the surf. Full detail at [boats/original-paradox.md](/boats/original-paradox.md).[5] Comparing notes years later on why "Zoë" herself was re-rigged, Glen recalled that same sail alongside Matt's boat pulling away to windward the instant both turned up into the wind, leaving him "so disappointed I wanted to vomit" at the gap; the fuller verdict on that re-rig, and on "Zoë"'s own sail history, is at [boats/zoe.md § Rig and sail](/boats/zoe.md#rig-and-sail).[6]

The two met again in person weeks later at the 2003 Cedar Key Messabout, where Glen, having watched Matt "make it look easy," asked directly how he furled and hoisted without a topping lift: foot pressure on the furling line while hoisting, and on the halyard while furling, freeing both hands, an open question since December 2001.[2] Asked afterward whether he had noticed how Matt reefed his sail, Glen admitted he had not and "could kick myself for it"; photographs show Matt's sail furling counter-clockwise from the cockpit, the opposite of the more common convention.[7][8] A photograph of Matt's own bronze-tipped rudder and yuloh once suggested he had changed his sheeting arrangement; in fact he had not: the picture simply showed the prototype, where the plans version runs a two-to-one purchase; Matt's own fix for the mainsheet catching on the rudder head, yuloh mount and cleats was, in his own words, "very simple and works well."[9] The rudder blade's single-bolt pivot fixing has a strong service record: Matt told Glen his own original Paradox had logged roughly 10,000 miles on that exact configuration without giving him any trouble.[10]

### Matt Layden's eye for yard curvature, demonstrated on another builder's sail

Comparing a sail cut too flat (built by a sailmaker inexperienced with lug rigs, who had not accounted for the bend in the yard) against Maxwell's own Dabbler-built Mark II sail, the fix is to measure the yard's own curve directly: support each end on saw horses or chair backs, suspend a 50 lb weight from the center, then measure the gap between a taut string run end-to-end and the yard itself at the 25%, 50%, and 75% points along its length. Those measurements give the curvature that needs to be built into the sail. While sailing together, Matt Layden judged the flatter sail needed about **2 in of curvature** worked into it, purely by eye; measured against the yard afterward, the true figure came out at exactly 2 in, "underscoring Matt eye for detail."[11]

### Matt Layden's own transom port opens from inside

Seeing Matt Layden's own Paradox in person, rather than only in photographs, showed a detail invisible from a distance: his transom ventilation port was fitted so the cover could be removed **from the inside**.[12] See [construction/transom-deck-plate.md](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#a-2006-revisiting-an-inward-opening-cover-a-handle-and-lanyard-or-twin-back-to-back-ports) for the fuller discussion this observation informed.

## Watching Matt Layden win the 2003 Everglades Challenge

Matt won the 2003 Everglades Challenge outright. His progress is preserved through a relayed Checkpoint 4 comment, radioed in from Flamingo at 273 miles: "Spectacular sailing: Surfing at 6 knots with dolphins on the bow off Pavillion Key… Onward!"[13] The only near-catamaran entry that year, to Maxwell's knowledge, was a tacking proa built from a Hobie 18 hull, raced two-up as "Weed Warrior," which finished second overall.[14] At the race's rough beach start, Matt's boat was hauled out into large waves slamming into the stern, sending a good deal of water into the cabin; the bilge was worked dry by hand, with many pump strokes, before the job was finished off with a sponge, a case on its own for carrying a bigger bilge pump.[15]

That spring, Matt Layden's first message of his own arrived (a full sailmaking recipe for the sail he had cut for the original Paradox), and Glen was among the first to answer, keeping a copy on hand for later builders approaching a sail order or sewing their own.[16]

## Sailing aboard Enigma, and comparing two chine-runner designs

In 2005 Maxwell sailed a second of Matt Layden's own boats: **Enigma**, then discussed under the working name "Mini Paradox" before microcruising.com settled its name. Sailing her alongside Matt and his wife **Karen**, who judged she "sails like a witch" two-up and very roomy for her size, Maxwell confirmed firsthand that Enigma does carry chine runners, though they are hard to see in photographs.[3] Her runners sit farther aft and end farther forward than on a stock Paradox, an effect he attributed to end-plate effect rather than the chine's curvature. On a hypothetical catamaran, he judged chine runners would only be fitted to the outboard side of each hull.[17][18] Her speed he credited mainly to being sailed by someone who knows small sharpies well, and to her very light weight (a photo-based estimate of 130 lb, under a third of Paradox's own crewed displacement) rather than to the slight V tortured into her bow.[19]

Pressed years later on how a different designer's chine runners compare with Layden's, the direct firsthand verdict was that the two are "much different." Chris Morejohn's runners, fitted to his own larger "Hogfish Maximus" and "Jubilee," work from the flow at the chine/hull junction rather than Layden's lifting-body system, and ride wetter than Layden's, confirmed firsthand on an Atlantic crossing aboard "Hogfish Maximus" (see [Glen C. Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md#an-atlantic-crossing-aboard-hogfish-maximus)), because they do not extend as far aft; they are also less aerodynamically shaped and narrower than they would be if scaled directly from a Paradox. Full technical discussion at [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#narrow-wing-runners-on-a-chris-morejohn-sharpie-and-a-centerboard-plus-runner-combination).[20]

## Matt Layden's own words on scaling the design

Maxwell had worked out, and kept, a full set of 20% scale-up figures for an enlarged Paradox around 2003, resurfacing them a decade later, in his own words as their author, to confirm that Matt Layden had given his blessing to a scaled-up version needing no new set of drawings, since every dimension required is already on the stock plans as drawn.[21] Recalling the conversation directly, Matt's own words on the question were that Paradox was **"infinitely scale-able,"** with the caveat of having the knowledge to address the myriad problems that come with an increase in scale, a judgment Maxwell felt qualified to make himself as a former professional boat builder, used to moving bulkheads, adding structural support and recalculating sail area and displacement to keep a design properly balanced at any size.[22] See [Building Paradox larger: how far the designer's sanction actually reaches](/design/larger-paradox.md#how-far-the-designers-sanction-actually-reaches-and-what-the-record-does-not-settle) for the fuller sanction-provenance record and the worked 20% figures in full.
