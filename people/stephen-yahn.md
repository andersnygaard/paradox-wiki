# Stephen Yahn

**Stephen Yahn** was a builder in **Manchester, Washington**, on Puget Sound. Before Paradox he sailed a Picaroon, which in his own words was "not a trailer kindly boat"; a smaller, trailerable design, he reasoned, would let him travel far and wide behind a car instead.[1] He started **"Ironsides"** in Manchester in July 2006 and launched her on Puget Sound that October, two years on, going on to cruise the Sound in her for four years.[2][3] Answering builders' technical questions across some 290 messages, he was the second-most active correspondent of his own 2006-2009 span, after only [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md), and the sixth-most active across the whole archive's ten-year history. Diagnosed with cancer, he sold Ironsides in October 2012 and died in July 2013.[4][5]

## Building and launching Ironsides

"Ironsides" was begun in Manchester in July 2006 and launched on Puget Sound that October two years later, after a long run of detailed pre-build questions on rigging, ballast and wiring.[2][3] The full build and cruising record - a steel-reinforced third bulkhead, a side-mounted outboard he built to spare his hands, a glued-2x4 mast and four years of cruising Puget Sound - is told in full at [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md).

## A father's boatyard, and Puget Sound

His path to boats ran through his father's trade: head painter at Built Right Boats in Santa Monica, California, in the 1950s and 60s, building boats of plywood and polyester resin over fiberglass.[6] He learned his own fiberglassing technique there, favoring a paintbrush over a squeegee so the resin could soak in properly, and he remembered crushed walnut shells - sold industrially for sandblasting delicate machine parts, later repurposed as pet-lizard bedding - used as non-skid decking around his father's yard.[7] Bioluminescence belongs to the same seam of memory: as a small boy he watched it glow in the waves breaking on a Santa Monica beach, and later, riding with his father in a 16 ft runabout doing perhaps 25 knots, he watched the bow spray glow and fish dart away from the hull in streaks of light beneath the surface, "a wonder."[8] Decades on, at anchor one summer night near the Tacoma Narrows south of Gig Harbor, he found it again: rocking Ironsides by hand, he watched her chine runners churn the water into a whorl of glowing blue "snowflakes" streaming away aft, unprompted and unlooked for.[9]

He lived in Manchester, on Puget Sound, with a boat launch not far from his own house and Seattle's skyline visible to the east, glass towers that sometimes caught the summer sunset and threw back a show of "golden diamonds" - a view he mostly saw from his own kayak, since a neighbor's trees screened it from home, the same trees that spared his property the worst of the region's winter storms.[10] He sailed at night by preference, finding the Sound emptier and easier to navigate by lighthouse than in the hazy daytime view, and reckoned moonlight sailing "transcendental-ish."[11]

## A restless, generative mind

Yahn kept starting things. He was first to suggest modeling Paradox parts in Google Sketchup, the archive's earliest mention of the program and an idea other builders later took up.[12] He floated fitting a Paradox deck, cabin, chine runners and rig onto a Bolger Brick hull instead of scaling the Paradox hull itself,[13] and separately proposed starting from a Phil Bolger sharpie with chine runners fitted in place of Bolger's own leeway devices.[14] He fused Tom Yost's folding-kayak construction method, an aluminum-tubing skeleton under a vinyl-canvas skin, with Matt Layden's Elusion design at her own roughly 9 by 3 ft dimensions, imagining a boat foldable enough to fly with and keep in a car trunk.[15] A recurring daydream of a human-pedaled propeller for Ironsides drew on Philip Thiel's "dorycycle" designs,[16] and he took it far enough to hang a salvaged bronze propeller from a rope off Ironsides' own transom, gauging the idea's proportions against her tiny stern.[17]

Studying the design patiently rather than taking it at face value, he judged Paradox's rig, hull, cabin, chine runners, rudder, roller reefing and control lines too interconnected to change piecemeal: changing any one of them, in his view, would be a mistake only patient study would reveal. He put the same thought more plainly to a newcomer, that she looks quite small from the outside, drawing comment from everyone he is out with, yet proves surprisingly big and roomy within.[18] In April 2010 he began a second, unrelated small-boat project of his own, "Victoria Patagonica," with his wife's interest too.[19]

## Sharing what he knew

Across roughly 290 messages between 2006 and 2012, Yahn answered technical questions spanning nearly every part of the boat. A long personal essay weighed bronze, hot-dip galvanized steel and copper fasteners by era and application, judging screws mechanically superior to nails and steering builders away from brass in saltwater.[20] Asked how to check an old or secondhand Paradox's glassed-plywood hull for rot, he recommended the tap test: a light hammer or a screwdriver's plastic handle, tapping and listening for the tone, noting that a hull built well and never wetted through can last well beyond 30 years.[21] He was just as ready to help with a boat other than his own: answering a fellow builder's question about a vent-trunk drain for "Johanna," he explained the difference between US pipe and tube sizing and the Schedule system that sets a pipe's actual wall thickness.[22] The rest of his technical range, from hull materials to self-steering, ground tackle and epoxy technique, is folded into the boat and topic pages it built, cited there rather than repeated here.

## After his death

[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md), who had bought Ironsides from him the year before, announced that Yahn had died, calling him someone who had brought many good ideas to the community; his sympathies went to Yahn's family and friends.[5] [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md) called him an innovative, excellent craftsman in both metal and wood.[23] [Nels Arvidson](/people/nels-arvidson.md) wrote that the news had shocked and saddened him, sending his condolences to Yahn's family and "toward all of us who have valued knowing him."[24]

Bourbeau himself had met Yahn only once, in the course of collecting the boat: Yahn, already seriously unwell, had only limited energy to walk him through her in person.[25] He later remembered him simply as "a really nice guy."[26] Paul Raymond recalled once congratulating him on his own tack-strop hardware as "a work of art."[27]
