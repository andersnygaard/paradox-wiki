# David LeBlanc

**David LeBlanc**, of Seattle, Washington, was active in the Paradox community from 2002 to 2004 and became its most prolific outside contributor, cited across 38 bundle pages, more than any other non-building contributor on record. A long-time amateur radio enthusiast and a former US Navy radioman, he brought a wide reader's range to the community: plywood grading, antifouling chemistry, wooden mast construction and a running skepticism toward Kevlar and whole-hull steel. His own boat interest ran alongside the Paradox community rather than through it. He ordered plans for a **Golant Gaffer**, Roger Dongray's gaff-rigged design, taken with her interior space and looks from the start, and later, finding Paradox too small for his own purposes, weighed [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s larger Little Cruiser and a stretched [Paradox](/paradox.md) sized to come in just under Washington State's 16-foot boat-registration threshold.

## A Golant Gaffer, and a boat just under the line

He arrived with plans for a Golant Gaffer already ordered and checked before writing about it that a non-Paradox build was welcome talk.[1] It was: off-topic discussion of his own project drew no objection.[2] The Golant Gaffer's own designer, Roger Dongray, had mentioned one cruising Europe's canals as far as Turkey, and LeBlanc praised her interior space and looks on that account alone.[3] He extended the same origin story a few days later with a magazine research trail through *Classic Boat* and *Watercraft*, and a line that became one of his best-known: "i'm very impressed with the huge amount of well organized space below in an under 20' boat. It doesn't hurt either that she is utterly gorgeous (forgive me, i'm in luuuuv)."[4]

Paradox itself he eventually judged too small for his purposes, turning toward [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s larger Little Cruiser or something only marginally bigger, in the region of 15 feet 11 inches. The routes he weighed ran from Little Cruiser plans, if any ever became available, to a Layden-style sharpie designed from scratch, to a scaled-up Paradox, though two commercial designers had told him that going up by more than about 10 percent "throws the boat's equations hopelessly out of kilter."[5] The 15'11" figure was not arbitrary: he wanted to build as inexpensively as possible and stay just under Washington State's 16-foot threshold for boat registration, which added a fee for any qualifying boat that size or larger.[6] He put the constraint more memorably a few months earlier, joking that his home state "would require regi$$$tering a bathtub if it was used as a pleasure craft."[7] No Little Cruiser plans in fact exist; the fuller exchange, including a recommended 10-20 percent Paradox stretch with concrete dimensions, is at [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).

## Radio, and a rare piece of encouragement

He was, by his own account, a long-time amateur radio enthusiast who had come close to a license more than once without ever taking it, and separately a former radioman in the US Navy at the end of the 1960s: he had the theory but not the code.[8] That disclosure surfaced in a 2003 exchange about Little Cruiser's own antenna, which had moved from an Outbacker to a Buddipole by mid-year; the technical detail belongs at [radio and electronics](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md#a-permanent-hf-antenna-using-the-masts-lightning-protection-wire), but the exchange that drew the disclosure out of him is his own.[9]

His most substantial single contribution to the community was not technical at all. It remains a rare piece of build-motivation writing on record: keep a build log, develop transferable skills such as navigation, weather reading, safety courses, ham radio and one-burner cooking, reward yourself with the occasional piece of gear, research destinations, and above all, work on the boat at least once a week.[10] Later that year he answered a call for volunteer moderators he had nearly missed, closing with a line that has stuck: "Here's to keeping the pointy side up!"[11] Smaller instances of the same civic habit recur throughout his correspondence: an Atlantic-and-Pacific microcruising account, shared alike with two further small-boat communities,[12] a legal pointer confirming that simple bucket sanitation is federally legal,[13] and a forwarded commercial toilet-bag product with his own Seattle chandlery tips attached.[14]

## Books, and a few good jokes

He was a patient, occasionally very funny reader. A years-long hunt for the "Lugworm" series ended when the SHRIMPY books turned out to be out of print entirely: working through AbeBooks, he found *Shrimpy* priced well outside what he judged reasonable for what amounted to a cult classic, but the first *Lugworm* volume turned up cheap from a UK seller and he ordered it on the spot.[15][16][17] An aside on woodworking vocabulary produced one of the best-remembered lines on the page: "Scarf is something you do to food, or something worn on your head or around your shoulders! Scarph is the joining together of two pieces of wood! So, you'll excuse me while I scarf my dinner, put a scarf on my head and go and scarph some wood!" He closed with "Cabin feverish in Seattle... It is NOT supposed to snow here in the lowlands in MARCH!"[18] He is also the source of the community's best-known joke, coined the following year: "I believe a pair of paradoxes are a paradoxen;-) Paradoxically, more than 2 paradoxes would still be a paradoxen;-)"[19] and, wondering aloud whether a pet might satisfy an old two-up cruising standard, "I wonder how convincing a Lovebird named 'Rover' would be?"[20] His reading ran to literary asides as well as jokes: when David Beard described a hard week's sailing, LeBlanc asked, "Did you happen to name your boat after Hal Roth's?"[21]

## Materials, method and the case he made

His most substantial technical legacy is what remains the archive's fullest general account of building a wooden mast from scratch, a method drawn from Lin and Larry Pardey's *Seraffyn* books: an interior buildup at each fitting point, scarphs cut at 12:1 or steeper, careful tapering and rounding, and a crumpled-aluminum-foil radar reflector run up the mast's own interior. For an advanced builder he described the alternative birdsmouth method, a staved hollow mast he called "the pinnacle of the mast maker's art," and named Bud McIntosh's book as a further source.[22] `components/rig/mast.md` carries that method in full because this page promised it; his own wood-species follow-up, supplied a day later, is compressed here to a link at [mast](/components/rig/mast.md#wood-species-for-a-scratch-built-wooden-mast).[23]

He was a persistent skeptic of Kevlar for a Paradox bottom, on both workability and cost grounds: it is hard to wet out, hard to cut and effectively unsandable.[24] He backed the caution with figures, pointing builders to raka.com and comparing Kevlar against fiberglass by the yard at roughly four times the cost.[25] He also covered refit basics for a damp secondhand hull: drying wet plywood can take weeks, a tapping test and a dew-pattern check both reveal a hidden void, and moisture-barrier coatings are worth considering; that guidance, together with a separate pointer to West Marine and the West System Epoxy website for the same moisture-barrier question, is compressed here to a link at [rot inspection](/construction/rot-inspection-ice-pick.md).[24][26] He also directed builders to a West System Epoxyworks magazine article on building a wood-and-epoxy sharpie, mentioning as an aside what he took to be the largest wood-and-epoxy ship ever built, a 213-foot barque.[27]

Everywhere else his range shows as a scatter of clauses rather than full accounts, since the fuller versions already live on the pages he pointed to. He gave what remains the archive's most specific plywood recommendation, Meranti to the BS1088 standard over Okoume, with Brunzeel named as the top choice for US east-coast builders; see [plywood grades and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md).[28] He argued against amateur epoxy-lined water tanks in favor of jugs and jerry cans; see [water tank lining](/construction/water-tank-lining.md).[29] His antifouling advice, the archive's fullest breakdown of the selection factors involved (water temperature, hull material, salinity and haul-out frequency), recommended a copolymer ablative for a wood-hulled trailer sailor and warned against DIY cuprous-oxide shortcuts; see [bottom painting](/construction/bottom-painting.md).[30][31] A related anecdote of his own, four days of unprotected Tampa salt water fouling a hull badly enough to take real work to clear, is told in full there too.[32] He flagged that pressure-treated wood's preservative contains arsenic and believed at the time that an EPA ban on it was imminent, a fact folded into [plywood grades and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2006-recurrence-budget-exterior-ply-and-why-green-plywood-should-be-avoided) alongside that page's own pressure-treated guidance.[33] He recalled a Pardey book describing a Delrin gudgeon bearing and liner; see [rudder bearing materials](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md).[34] He was a repeated advocate for cheaper off-brand epoxies, Raka and Fiberglass Epoxy Products, over West System, citing a multi-gallon kit running roughly a third of West's price; see the same page.[35][36]

He argued against whole-hull steel construction on weight grounds and suggested a layer or two of Kevlar as a lighter answer to rock-strike or abrasion concerns instead, then endorsed a design-philosophy case against steel outright, pointing builders to `www.fairmetalboats.com`; see [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative)](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md).[37][38] He held that some modern window plastics are literally stronger than steel, so that a stove-in window more often reflects poor fastening than weak plastic, and described a storm-repair technique for a stove-in porthole using a diagonally cut plywood blank and a strip of old bicycle inner tube; see the same page.[39] He set out the standard checklist distinguishing a coastal cruiser from a genuine blue-water boat, illustrated with Ottavio Testa's *500 Days*, and separately argued that the differences between [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s and Sven Yrvind's designs are a matter of materials rather than design philosophy, disputing the double-ender's buoyancy rationale on hydrostatic grounds; see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md).[40][41]

He was also the community's sharpest defender of a beginner-friendly tool list. When a full Build Manual tool inventory drew the objection that it would deter first-time builders, he proposed a tiered minimum, nice-to-have and luxury structure instead, and reinforced the minimalist case with a 21-foot boat built using a single power tool and the observation that a full stationary-tool shop mainly benefits production-scale builders; see [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md#the-build-manuals-preparations-installment-build-site-and-a-full-tool-inventory).[42][43][44] He proposed a second Sven Yrvind acolyte, a Swedish microcruiser builder known online as "Sebastian," as deserving fuller coverage or even his own book in any future reprint of [William Longyard](/people/william-longyard.md#a-speck-on-the-sea)'s *A Speck on the Sea*, and pointed to two of Sebastian's own web pages of pictures and project updates.[45] He preferred kerosene over LED navigation lights, arguing they did little to reduce the need for a proper lookout in any case, while rating an anchor light as the more important fixture; see [running lights](/operations/running-lights.md).[46] And in the [BRIS Paradox](/boats/bris-paradox.md)'s own laminate-schedule discussion, he made the passing point that epoxy is available with varying moduli of elasticity and need not be stiff or brittle.[47]

## Two debates, held and yielded

Twice he took a firm technical position in public and showed both sides of how he held it. He objected to what he read as an endorsement of lee helm, arguing that a boat's helm should stay neutral toward weather to avoid a knockdown risk, and guessed that the catamaran story offered against him really turned on mizzenmast weight in the cockpit rather than helm balance itself.[48] Shown the fuller case the next day, he yielded outright: "I must defer to your experience."[49]

Six months later he took the opposite tack and held his ground. Asked how heavy a fully loaded and ballasted Paradox really is, he offered "1400# I think I've heard mentioned here" as his own opening figure,[50] then worked the numbers from a roughly 600 lb hull, a 250 lb crew member and 250 lb of stores to argue that a loaded weight near 1,000 lb "isn't so far off the mark."[51] Told this was silly, he did not back down: "Oh right, silly me - of course a pound of lead will displace as much water as a pound of foam! HAH! I stand by my previous post."[52] He pressed the underlying point with a concrete illustration of his own: a pound of lead and a pound of foam do not displace the same volume, since most of the foam floats above the water, so displacement is the weight of water actually displaced rather than the weight of whatever is doing the displacing.[53] The fuller physics of the claim, and where it needs qualifying, are at [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).

## Disambiguation

**Not to be confused with [David Beard](/people/david-beard.md).** The two correspondents are easy to conflate in the raw archive, since Beard's own Paradox is named "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" and LeBlanc's own list account is confusable with that name for unrelated reasons of its own.
