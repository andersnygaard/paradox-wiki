# David LeBlanc

**David LeBlanc**, of Seattle, Washington, was a longtime amateur radio enthusiast and a former US Navy radioman, a wide reader with a particular grounding in materials, refit and rig.[1] He had already ordered plans for a Golant Gaffer, Roger Dongray's gaff-rigged design, drawn to her interior space: "utterly gorgeous (forgive me, i'm in luuuuv)."[2] Finding Paradox too small for his own purposes, he later weighed Matt Layden's larger Little Cruiser and a stretched Paradox sized to come in under Washington State's 16-foot boat-registration threshold; the fuller exchange is at [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).[3]

## Materials and refit

He recommended Meranti plywood to the BS1088 standard over Okoume, naming Brunzeel as the top choice for US east-coast builders; see [plywood grades and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md).[4] He was wary of Kevlar for a Paradox bottom: hard to wet out, hard to cut and effectively unsandable. For a damp secondhand hull he described a tapping test and a dew-pattern check for a hidden void and recommended moisture-barrier coatings; drying wet plywood, he noted, can take weeks.[5] His antifouling advice weighed water temperature, hull material, salinity and haul-out frequency, recommending a copolymer ablative paint for a wood-hulled trailer sailor and warning against DIY cuprous-oxide shortcuts; see [bottom painting](/construction/bottom-painting.md).[6] He favored jugs, cubes and jerry cans over amateur epoxy-lined water tanks and found a Raka epoxy kit running at roughly a third of West System's price; see [water tank lining](/construction/water-tank-lining.md) and [rudder bearing materials](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md).[7][8]

A patient and often funny reader of small-boat literature, he once distinguished two easily confused words in his own way: "Scarph is the joining together of two pieces of wood!"[9]

## Mast, rig and lights

He described the box-girder method for a wooden mast built from scratch, drawn from Lin and Larry Pardey's *Seraffyn* books, with staved birdsmouth construction as an alternative for a more advanced builder and a crumpled aluminum-foil radar reflector run up the mast's own interior; see [mast](/components/rig/mast.md#box-girder-mast-construction-technique-and-the-birdsmouth-alternative).[10] He preferred kerosene running lights over LED, judging they did little to reduce the need for a proper lookout, rating an anchor light the more important fixture; see [running lights](/operations/running-lights.md).[11]

## Design and workshop

He opposed whole-hull steel construction on weight grounds, suggesting a layer or two of Kevlar as a lighter answer to rock-strike or abrasion instead; see [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative)](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md).[12] He set out the checklist distinguishing a coastal cruiser from a genuine blue-water boat, illustrated with Ottavio Testa's *500 Days*; see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md).[13] He proposed a tiered minimum, nice-to-have and luxury tool list as a friendlier alternative to a full workshop inventory, illustrated with a 21-foot boat built using a single power tool; see [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md#the-build-manuals-preparations-installment-build-site-and-a-full-tool-inventory).[14] He also gave practical advice for staying motivated through a long build: keep a log, develop transferable skills and work on the boat at least once a week.[15]
