# Dave Skaife

**Dave Skaife** is an American small-boat sailor who built a [Paradox](/paradox.md) in Hawaii and sailed it, motorless, over four seasons between 2014 and 2018 — across Hawaii, the Gulf of Mexico, the Bahamas twice, and Cuba. He documented the whole of it on a personal blog, `daveskaife.wordpress.com`, which is the archive's longest continuous first-person record of a single Paradox, second only to [another builder's cruise log](/people/bill-serjeant.md). His blog is preserved as a first-class source at [references/dave-skaife-blog.md](/references/dave-skaife-blog.md); this page draws the person and the voyage out of it.

His boat carried **no name** anywhere in the blog — he called it only "my Paradox" or "my microcruiser." A direct inquiry away from the blog later drew out the name he actually used on paperwork: "**Viking funeral**."[25]

## Why he built a Paradox

Dave came to the design moving *down*, not up. He had a 36 ft monohull that he almost always sailed solo, and found himself maintaining and pushing around far more boat than he needed; he wanted something small, simple, and trailerable. Looking through many designs, he found [a widely circulated sketch](/people/don-elliott.md) of a Paradox and was, in his words, "pretty much hooked" — it was the kind of sailing he wanted, which he learned was called microcruising. Not wanting to build from scratch but finding it the only way to get one, he talked it over with his boatbuilding mentor and ordered the plans through [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md).[1]

He judged the plans "jam packed with details and information, almost a sensory overload," warned that a Paradox takes much longer to build than a normal fourteen-footer because of everything built into it, and called [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) "an evil genius when it comes to boat designs — he thinks so far outside the box that you forget there was a box." The boat, he wrote soon after launch, had already exceeded his expectations.[1]

## Building the boat in Hawaii

Dave built the boat from scratch in Hawaii, blogging the tail end of construction from December 2013. His first post is characteristic of how he built: a tiny bamboo drip-guide fitted under the cabin windows to catch condensation and lead it to a sponge in the corner — an easy upgrade to do before the windows and paint went on, and, he cheerfully admitted, "probably one of those times when I over think something."[2] Through the build and first season he documented his electrical system, fender and anchor storage, dual-purpose running lights, a hatch lock, and multipurpose water-storage buckets — the small, considered outfitting choices that carry through the rest of the log. He was also corresponding with the Paradox builders mailing list during construction: in February 2010, already mid-build, he singled out a fellow builder's own consolidated list of departures from the stock plans — see [Little Jim — a full accounting of departures from the stock plans](/boats/little-jim.md#a-full-accounting-of-departures-from-the-stock-plans) — as worth resurfacing, said he would fold its tips into his own build, and invited other owners to share tips of their own.[8] The same month he reported the hull's panels and bulkheads tacked together into a rough three-dimensional shape for the first time, and switched to building a trailerable Paradox after Hawaii changed its mooring-permit fee structure, having already found most of the features he wanted for fishing built into the design; he judged the enclosed, all-weather inside steering station and the boat's smaller, simpler systems well suited to fishing underway compared with a larger boat, planned to simplify his own gear back toward the handline basics he had used from an earlier trimaran, and sourced a cheap trailer and hardware early on eBay to spread the build's cost.[9] By March 2011 the bare hull sat on its trailer while a house renovation took priority, construction paused until the drywall and tile were finished.[10] Once a fellow builder answered his hatch and rudder-hole watertightness questions (see [Small Fry — heavy-weather watertightness](/boats/small-fry.md#future-plans-and-heavy-weather-watertightness-in-geoff-daviss-own-words)), Dave weighed his own plans against sailing in warmer waters than most: a 12V fan fitted inside the vent box for when the hatch must stay closed, and a lip along the top of the baffle to stop water shooting off it and soaking the stern compartment (see [vent box](/components/vent-box.md) and [vent baffle](/components/vent-baffle.md)) — informed by an earlier, non-Paradox small boat of his own, sailed in big water with such low freeboard that the hatch had to stay closed most of the time regardless, letting in an ounce of water at a time through the deck that got old after a couple of days; Paradox, by contrast, he judged would prove a much better all-weather boat.[11] He remained active on the mailing list through the gap before his own blog began: in May 2013 he gave a fellow owner setting out on his own first Puget Sound cruise detailed, tide-specific route advice for the Tacoma Narrows.[12] The following week he reported the project back in build mode after a long break for other projects — a house and a trimaran rebuild — at the stage of tackling the chine runners, vent trunk, and tiller baffle, and having started work on the rudder stock; weighing a scavenged length of old mast tubing of similar dimensions to the plans' wooden mast, he decided to give an aluminum mast a try, judged his own chine-runner work "not very pretty" though workable, and reported the rudder stock glued up and fitting the boat, needing only a few hours of final shaping.[13][14] The same month he began collecting other parts early for good Craigslist, eBay, and Amazon deals, and asked after other builders' anchor choices and lead-ballast quantities, uneasy at the prospect of buying and handling a couple hundred kilos of lead and wondering whether water ballast might substitute if there proved to be room for it.[15] Weighing the replies, he leaned toward a 5 kg Bruce over a 2 kg one, kept a couple of Danforth-types in reserve while still short one anchor, and settled on loading up on chain in place of some of the lead ballast, reasoning that chain, unlike dead lead, still earns its keep as usable ground tackle — see [ground tackle](/operations/ground-tackle.md#anchors-builders-favor) for the fuller anchor-choice discussion this and other builders' replies fed into. The same week he reported shaping the yard, the first part of the boat, in his own assessment, that did not look "way overbuilt."[16] That August, on the recurring "Don Elliot's book?" thread, he reported leaning on Don Elliott's construction manual at every phase of the build, alongside pictures from a few builder blogs — "every little bit of info helps" — and found Don's own drawings of the boat in use inspiring, even if that felt "strange" to admit.[17] By November, nearing completion, he had reached the stage of needing to think about getting a sail made.[18] For the mainsheet, he settled on a deck-entry point like the one built into "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" to accommodate her wind vane, needing only to fabricate a turning block for it — a route chosen after fitting the plans' transom entry showed it would chafe running downwind; he also planned to try a wind scoop paired with a fan, suited to his own warm climate.[19] He announced the finished boat and its new Hawaii-cruise blog on the mailing list that July, promising further posts on the boat's gear and interior details to follow.[20]

## Four seasons under sail and oar

The boat was **motorless** throughout — Dave sculled and sailed it, through locks and calms as well as open water — which makes the blog a sustained real-world account of engine-free Paradox cruising over an unusually wide range:

- **[Hawaii (2014)](/merits/skaife-hawaii-cruise.md).** A roughly 415-nautical-mile inter-island cruise of the main chain — Hilo on the Big Island up through Lanai, Molokai, and Oahu to Kauai, with two four-day unbroken passages — including reef-damage repair and the tiller-pilot he rated his most valuable single piece of gear.
- **The Gulf ICW (2015).** He shipped the boat from Hawaii to Texas and sailed on across the Gulf of Mexico's [Intracoastal Waterway](/merits/inland-waterway-and-canal-touring.md) from Texas to Florida — starting out from **Port Isabel**, per a mailing-list sighting the same month that called it an impressive voyage[21] — blogging it almost daily through the Louisiana and Mississippi marshes, the Chandeleur and Ship Islands, and Florida's Big Bend, and closing the season with a damage-assessment post recording what the long passage had actually broken.
- **The Bahamas, twice (2016 and 2017).** A Pensacola shakedown and Gulf Stream crossing to Bimini and the Exumas in 2016, then George Town, the Jumentos, and Ragged Island in 2017.
- **[Cuba (2017)](/merits/skaife-cuba-voyage.md), and the end.** A 17-hour passage from the southern Bahamas took him to Puerto de Vita on Cuba's north coast; he cruised west to Cayo Guillermo and inland to Morón — check-in, money, and internet all documented — then made an engine-free Gulf Stream return to Florida, closing out the four seasons through hurricane season and the Everglades.[3]

## "Comfortable microcruising"

Between seasons Dave wrote a deliberate twelve-post **"comfortable microcruising"** series — his own thinking on how to make a fourteen-foot Paradox genuinely liveable: ventilation, the padded "cell" interior, dressing for the conditions, window covers, an "ultimate personal environment management system," and the balance of sentiment against minimalism aboard a very small boat. Read as one owner's outfitting philosophy rather than as documentation of the plans, it is the densest original design thinking on the blog and its most useful material for topic pages; alongside it he ran a `gear-i-like` review series (Bebi anchor light, JetBoil, Streamlight spotlight).[4]

## The boat's later life

The single fullest description of the boat is Dave's 2017 for-sale post, written after two Bahamas seasons: a "14 foot motorless sailboat," registered in Hawaii with a hull identification number (he registered it there because proving ownership for the paperwork trail was simplest, and most states want residency), and equipped with an electronic autopilot, a personal locator beacon, a submersible handheld VHF, four anchors, cabin fans, and a full kit of cruising gear — "pretty much turn-key."[5] He posted the same news to the mailing list itself the next day, naming a price for the first time — turn-key at **$3,500**, "I already regret that price!" — and directing questions to a comment on the blog's own for-sale post rather than the list, since he "seldom visit[s] here."[26] The post drew a one-line reaction calling it "a steal" and predicting "somebody is going to get a good deal."[27] He offered it for sale in Pensacola, Florida from 2017 as he moved on to overland travel in Southeast Asia, and it sold in March 2018 — to "a new excited owner," with the proceeds earmarked for further adventures.[6][7][28][29] Once the sale went through, he began looking for a lighter secondhand [Elusion](/matt-layden/elusion.md) or [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) to replace it, and said he would be willing to pay someone else to do much of the finishing work.[30]

The new owner surfaced on the mailing list himself the following month, confirming the boat had moved from Hawaii to the US Gulf coast and asking, not yet sure which sail had come with the boat, after a sailmaker who could supply one — signed off "Steve C (Paw Paw)," in the same name-plus-boat-name convention used elsewhere on the list, suggesting the boat was renamed "Paw Paw" under her new ownership.[31] Full record of the boat's life and the sail question's eventual answer under her new name: [boats/paw-paw.md](/boats/paw-paw.md); full record of her new owner: [people/steve-collins.md](/people/steve-collins.md).

A January 2017 registry-thread exchange after "the name of Dave's Hawaiian boat" that had cruised the Gulf ICW pointed back to the blog itself[22] and, once he was reached directly, supplied the archive's only record of her Hawaii-registered hull identification number — **HA1081K**.[23][24] A direct reply from him days later settled the naming question left open there: for paperwork purposes he called her "Viking funeral."[25]
