# Pete Hodges

**Pete Hodges**, 6'3", was based in Florida. A secondhand Paradox, owned by Bob Archibald, was for sale near Steinhatchee in September 2001; [David Beard](/people/david-beard.md) later bought that boat and named her "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)".[1] Rather than buy her, he worked out a 10 percent enlargement of his own, "Paradox 1.1", sized to his frame, spending the months to May 2002 gathering materials for the build.

## The "Paradox 1.1" enlargement

His height ruled out the stock cabin's roughly 36 in of rear headroom; 39 in, three inches more, was his target.[2] A uniform 5 percent enlargement was weighed and set aside as impractical, since the bulkheads and stem are not vertical and would need lofting rather than a simple percentage bump.[2] He settled instead on a uniform 10 percent scale-up, named "Paradox 1.1," in December 2001, and worked out the practical ceiling on any larger enlargement: beyond 10 percent the side panels need an extra scarf; the bottom panel, with chines added, exceeds a standard 4 ft sheet.[3][4] Planning ran through the following May: a tent shelter, bronze ring nails, epoxy and stem and beam molds, pending a tax refund. "I am busy screwing up my nerve to get going on my 1.1 version."[5] The full engineering, including the bottom-panel layout options and the scaling problem for sail area and angled parts, is at [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md) and [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#pete-hodgess-10-stretch-paradox-11); the headroom fixes he weighed and set aside are at [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#pete-hodges-proposes-concrete-modifications-for-a-taller-sailor).

## Chine runners, rig and hull materials

He weighed whether a chine-runner pattern could be pre-cut to the hull's horizontal curve, leaving only a vertical bend for installation, and whether the runners, backed by a heavy inside fillet, could substitute for an internal chine log, a known rot-start point.[6][7] He looked for a way to seal the boom's narrow, deep tack-line holes against rot without filling them solid.[8] He remained doubtful of the furling system's reefing mechanism, the one part of the design he saw as unique to Paradox.[9] A roughly 1 sq ft Lexan bottom window, doubling as interior lighting in shallow water, was his own proposal.[10] Building with RAKA epoxy through a Florida winter, he found 60°F the threshold below which curing could take days.[11] Pressure-treated wood, at roughly twice the weight of untreated stock, was his proposal for the bottom, chine logs and chine runners; his hammer test of an epoxied joint against a screwed one in the same material found the glued joint held while the screwed one pulled through, detailed further at [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#pete-hodgess-10-stretch-paradox-11).[12][13] Full account: [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md), [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md), [boom-end tack line holes](/construction/boom-end-tack-line-holes.md), [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md), [glass-bottom Paradox](/design/ideas/glass-bottom-idea.md), [epoxy brands and chemistry](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md), [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md), [interior bins and hinged floor](/components/interior.md), [head and sanitation arrangements](/operations/head-and-sanitation.md), [mast rake](/components/rig/mast.md#mast-rake--where-it-comes-from-and-how-its-built), [rudder and chine experiments](/components/rudder.md), [checking a used Paradox for rot](/construction/rot-inspection-ice-pick.md) and [lower rudder fitting](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md).
