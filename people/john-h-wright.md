# John H. Wright

**John H. Wright**, of **central Texas**, entered WaterTribe's Everglades Challenge six times between 2010 and 2016 under the name **Karank**, finishing in 2012 crewed with Gary Blankenship.[1] He built a working roller boom to Matt Layden's own principle by his own means and methods, and on the Thursday evening before the 2010 Ultimate Florida Challenge he inspected Layden's newest boat, **[Elusion](/matt-layden/elusion.md)**, in person, the same week he raced the Everglades Challenge himself.[2]

## A WaterTribe racing career, as Karank

Racing as Karank, he entered the Everglades Challenge's Class 4 (monohull sailboats) division six times: 2010, 2011, 2012, 2014, 2015 and 2016.[1] Five of the six attempts were sailed solo and did not finish, withdrawn on the first day in 2010, 2011 and 2014 and stopped after the first checkpoint in 2015 and 2016. The sixth, in 2012, finished: sailed double handed with Gary Blankenship as Lugnut and Karank, Class 4 Double Male, the 300-mile course completed in 5 days 17 hours 31 minutes.[1] That race's overnight anchoring decision, taken as a gale built toward morning, gave the tribe its own shorthand for Layden's reputation on the course; the full account is at [matt-layden/biography.md](/matt-layden/biography.md#racing-beyond-paradox).[3]

## Never second guess Matt

His standing position on modifying the design held to two lines: "Rule No. 1 Never second guess Matt. Rule No. 2 Refer to rule #1."[4] He tested the principle on the boom itself, building a working roller boom to Layden's own function by his own means, at a cost, by his own reckoning, of a couple of weeks he would not have lost building to the stock design; his advice to other builders was to leave the fitting exactly as drawn, since only using it reveals the nuance of all its functions.[5][6]

The same trust in the design carried into chine runners, which he treated not as a standalone foil like a centerboard or leeboard but as an extension of the chine itself, effective at any depth so long as the chine and the lifting-body hull behind it run deep enough to matter.[7] By his own comparison across Layden's boats, Sand Flea carried a leeboard rather than chine runners, while Elusion's own runners were short but, proportionately, about the same as Paradox's, Elusion being a heavy boat for her size once her crew and gear were stowed.[8]

The recommended method for launching an unusually large scale-up was a great deal of ballast for the first sail, reduced progressively until the boat performed well, a practice he held to be fundamental to the design and more pronounced still at large scale.[9] For windward sailing in a strong wind, the sail needed to be relatively flat, with some luffing as the boat pitches forward, and the boat kept moving at good speed to keep the chine runners effective.[10]

## A firsthand look at Elusion

He kept his dockside visit to about ten minutes so as not to intrude: the hull was finished to a high standard, assumed to be 6 mm marine plywood glassed on the exterior, with Layden still weighing how much lead-shot ballast to add on top of the gear already stowed.[2] From the pre-race photographs alone he had already picked out a small end plate on the rudder's cheek plate, the same reboarding step Paradox carries on her own rudder.[11] Watching Layden letter the boat's name onto her transom at the boatyard put him in mind of one of the late Don Elliott's own cartoons and of how much this kind of boat had been Elliott's love.[12]

Once the Ultimate Florida Challenge was under way, his own knowledge of the Florida coast supplied a fellow racer's reading of the tracker: the St. Marys River runs to the Atlantic, so the fleet paddled upriver against the current before the forty-mile portage carried it to the Suwannee for a run downstream, and Layden's boat was making about four knots or better running downwind. Full account: [merits/ultimate-florida-challenge-2010.md](/merits/ultimate-florida-challenge-2010.md).[13]

He had started the shorter Everglades Challenge himself that same week and withdrew from it on the first day. The Gulf had put him close to the edge for an hour or two, one small steering error from cold water: "Steering down wind in those conditions is like running bare foot on a stack of boards with nails in them...for hours."[14]

## Local knowledge

Thousands of miles of his own Gulf of Mexico sailing, including a cruise from Mexico to the Bahamas and back over one summer, sit behind a hot-weather cabin-comfort routine: an air scoop with mosquito netting led as far forward as practical, netting over every other opening besides, the cabin sealed up entirely from sunset in some anchorages and, on a hot still night, an air mattress filled with sea water and pumped out again the next morning, cooler than body heat and quick to cool a sleeper. Full account: [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md#a-hot-weather-cabin-cooling-routine-air-scoop-mosquito-netting-and-a-wet-mattress-trick).[15]
