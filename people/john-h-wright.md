# John H. Wright

**John H. Wright**, who lived in **Bastrop, Texas**, was a recurring voice among Paradox builders from 2006 to 2018 and a small-boat racer in his own right, entering WaterTribe's Everglades Challenge six times under the name **Karank**. No Paradox of his own is on record, but he built a working roller boom to Matt Layden's own principle by his own means, and his standing advice on the design distills to two lines: never second-guess Matt, and refer to rule one. In March 2010, on the eve of the Ultimate Florida Challenge, he inspected Layden's newest boat, **Elusion**, in person, the only such inspection of her the archive records, then narrated the race itself from a competitor's own knowledge of the course, having started on the same beach that week before withdrawing on the first day.

## "Never second guess Matt"

His standing position on modifying the design fit in two lines: "Rule No. 1 Never second guess Matt. Rule No. 2 Refer to rule #1."[1] He held to it in practice as well as in principle, advising a builder against messing with Matt's roller boom at all, on the grounds that only using it reveals the nuance of all its functions.[2] His own attempt at the fitting is the reason the advice carries weight: he built a roller boom functionally like Layden's using his own means and methods, and reckoned it cost him a couple of weeks he would not have lost building to the stock design.[3]

The same trust in the design ran through his other technical answers. To a builder launching an unusually large scale-up, he gave the method rather than a figure: launch with a great deal of ballast aboard for the first sail, then reduce it progressively until she performs well, "that is the way of the Paradox," and the more so at large scale.[4] Asked how to rig the sail for windward ability, he gave the archive's most direct answer on sail shape: in a strong wind the sail needs to be relatively flat, with some luffing, about 5-10% of the time in that kind of seaway as the boat pitches forward, tied straight back to the hull, since the boat must maintain good forward speed for the chine runners to be effective at all.[5]

Chine runners drew his fullest technical range. In a December 2009 exchange over fitting them to other hulls, he synthesized the conclusion it had been circling toward: chine runners work only with help from the hull itself, plus an oversized rudder, which pushes the center of effort farther aft than normal practice.[6] Restated two years later, answering a newcomer's question about building the hull in foam instead of plywood: the runners are not a stand-alone device like a centerboard or leeboard but an extension of the chine itself, effective at any depth so long as the chine, and the lifting-body hull behind it, is deep enough to matter, "a boat is a sum of the parts."[7] On whether shorter, wider runners would improve on Paradox's own shape, he reduced the whole idea to a reductio: raise a runner's aspect ratio far enough and it becomes a bilgeboard, then an obtrusive appendage sticking out of the hull, until the logical endpoint is simply moving it to the centerline and making it retractable, "the circle is then complete."[8] Working through that same history across Matt Layden's own boats, he placed a leeboard, not chine runners, on Sand Flea, and judged Elusion's own runners rather short but, proportionately, about the same as Paradox's, Elusion being a heavy boat for her size once her own crew and gear were stowed. He meant to ask Matt the question himself, at the next Everglades Challenge.[9]

## Elusion and the Ultimate Florida Challenge

From the pre-race photographs alone, in February 2010, he had already picked out a small end plate on the rudder's cheek plate, reasoning it could only be on the port side, opposite the swing-up rudder, the same reboarding step Paradox carries on her own rudder.[10] He saw the boat itself the following month, on the Thursday evening before the Ultimate Florida Challenge start, keeping the visit to about ten minutes so as not to intrude: "Awesome, that Matt. I worship the man as a designer and tried to kiss his foot but he is so unassuming, he would not let me." The hull was finished to a high standard, assumed to be 6 mm marine plywood glassed on the exterior, with construction and testing still being completed close to race day, and Matt was weighing how much lead-shot ballast to add on top of the gear already stowed.[11] Speculation that "Elusion" echoed the late [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s own unbuilt "Illusion" drew a personal reply: he recalled watching Matt letter the boat's own name onto her transom at "the park," a sight that put him in mind of one of Elliott's cartoons and of how much this kind of boat had been Elliott's own love.[12]

Once the race itself was under way, he became one of its two main narrators, supplying the local knowledge a tracker alone could not: that the St. Marys River flows to the Atlantic, so the fleet would be paddling upriver against the current until the roughly forty-mile portage carried them to the Suwannee and a downstream run,[13] speeds of about four knots or better running downwind and as high as 2.4 knots paddling against the river current, from his own watch on the first leg's Saturday start,[14] and the portage's own waypoints, Traders Hill to Fargo, when the route itself came into question.[15] Watching Matt sail left him with a verdict he repeated more than once: "Matt is the most efficient sailer I have ever seen. I could pass him but every time I started screwing around and not paying attention....I would look up and there he was...again." Of the race's outcome, whatever the order: "Anyone that finished wins, in my book. Two days or two minutes don't mean nothing. They are all heros to me."[16] Reading another competitor's own fast SPOT readings, he judged they had come on the face of a wave, timed just before Wizard reached Venice Inlet: "that last hour was scary... I was very aware that a slight steering error on the face of a wave could capsize my boat....in that cold water!"[17]

He knew the feeling firsthand. He had started the shorter Everglades Challenge himself that same week, and withdrew from it on the first day, in his own words: "I chickened out and can list excuses. The boat worked well....I did not." The first day in the Gulf had put him close to the edge for an hour or two, one small steering error from that cold water: "Steering down wind in those conditions is like running bare foot on a stack of boards with nails in them...for hours." It was fun for a while, he said, "but built to scary at some point."[18]

## A WaterTribe racing career, as Karank

Under the name **Karank**, he entered WaterTribe's Everglades Challenge six times between 2010 and 2016, finishing once: double-handed in 2012, crewed with Gary Blankenship as "Lugnut and Karank," Class 4 Double Male, 300 miles in 5 days 17 hours 31 minutes. The other five attempts, sailed solo, did not finish, withdrawn on the first day in 2010, 2011 and 2014, and stopped after the first checkpoint in 2015 and 2016.[19]

That 2012 finish supplied the tribe's own shorthand for its most celebrated competitor. Preparing overnight for a wind shift that reached gale force by morning, Blankenship found a good anchorage at 11 pm, when the crew's own judgment was already failing, and the shelter it bought them mattered more, in retrospect, than the boat or either man's sailing talent, which was the whole of what "**What would Wizard do?**" was ever asking, Matt Layden being, in Wright's own words, "the Wizard of the course, not counting his other talents."[20]

At the same start that year, he watched Matt sail past on Sand Flea rather than Elusion, unchanged except for a new dodger, and did not try to speak to him this time: "I did not even try to talk to him.....he is a rock star...in this venue." Two years on from his own dockside visit, his impression, watching the preparation the day before, was that Elusion herself had had little if any test sailing before that first race began.[21][22]

## Local knowledge, and introducing a fellow builder

He signed himself **"johninbastrop,"** for the small Texas town he called home. The earliest thing on record from him, in 2006, defended the Gulf of Mexico against the suggestion it was easy water: a 35 mph wind turns it into a "salt water pond," rarely calm except in a hurricane.[23] The claim held up over thousands of miles of his own Gulf sailing, including a cruise from Mexico to the Bahamas and back over one summer, and it stands behind the fullest hot-weather cabin-comfort routine on record for the design: an air scoop with mosquito netting led as far forward as practical, netting over every other opening besides, the cabin sealed up entirely from sunset in some anchorages, and, on a hot still night, an air mattress filled with sea water and pumped out again the next morning, cooler than body heat and quick to cool a sleeper.[24]

In July 2015 he introduced a fellow builder's cruising blog, unprompted, with open admiration: "The second thing I do each morning is check on this guy... I am so impressed with his guts sailing abilities and communication skills. He has a great boat for this coast. I want to shake his hand one day and say thanks for allowing me to be with him.... vicariously." The blog was [Dave Skaife](/people/dave-skaife.md)'s, covered in full at [references/dave-skaife-blog.md](/references/dave-skaife-blog.md).[25] Two builders responded the same day, one reading the whole thing start to finish in one sitting and left waiting on the next installment, and [Rob Kellock](/people/rob-kellock.md) calling its author "a Paradox hero," checking the blog every time he fired up his computer.[26][27]

In his last years of technical give-and-take, he kept raising the same close, particular questions: whether a stalled boat's own inertia ought to carry it through a tack anyway,[28] whether a furled boom and gaff really are meant to lie flush on the side deck,[29] and whether a properly ballasted Paradox can capsize at all outside a wave breaking clean over her.[30] A practical note on beach-launching closes what is on record from him: at the start of a race requiring exactly this kind of launch, competitors typically help each other get boats off their trailers, but getting the loaded boat from there into the water alone afterward, he judged, is the problem that actually needs solving, since a Paradox ballasted properly to the plans is very heavy. The record of his participation runs from 2006 to 2018.[31]
