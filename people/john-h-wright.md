# John H. Wright ("johninbastrop")

**John H. Wright**, posting as **`jhargrovewright2`** and signing "johninbastrop" — for Bastrop, Texas, where he lived — was a recurring commentator on the `paradoxbuilders` list from 2006 to 2010.[1][2] No Paradox of his own is confirmed anywhere in the record, but he is a small-boat sailor rather than an observer: he built a roller boom functionally like Matt Layden's by his own means and methods,[9] entered a WaterTribe race himself in 2010,[16] and watched Matt Layden sail at close quarters over several days.[15] His deepest subject by far is the **roller reefing and furling gear**, where he supplied both the mechanical explanation of the "Y" fitting and the argument for leaving the stock design alone.

## "Never second guess Matt"

His standing position on modifying the design fits in two lines, and became something of a catchphrase on the list: *"Rule No. 1 Never second guess Matt. Rule No. 2 Refer to rule #1."*[3] See [design/modifying-paradox.md](/design/modifying-paradox.md). He held to it in practice as well as in principle — advising a builder against messing with Matt's roller boom at all, on the grounds that only using it reveals the nuance of all its functions.[8]

## Roller reefing: the "Y" fitting, and a boom built the hard way

Wright's own attempt at the fitting is the reason his advice carries weight. He had built a roller boom functionally like Layden's using his own means and methods, and reckoned it cost him a couple of weeks he would not have lost building to the stock design; he pointed the list to a 2008 *Duckworks Magazine* how-to article on the fitting.[9] See [references/duckworks-magazine.md](/references/duckworks-magazine.md).

On the mechanics, he explained what the split **"Y"** actually achieves: it lets the boom keep moving fore-and-aft under sail while restricting side-to-side — "wrist" — movement at the joint, so the boom stands up on its tube and reaches the boat's side.[10] See [components/rig/furling.md — the "Y" fitting's purpose confirmed](/components/rig/furling.md#the-y-fittings-purpose-confirmed-and-the-plans-own-may-1996-revision). Separately, on the furling line itself, he worked through the trade-off between line size and line quantity — the roller furler's spool line can be much smaller because of the large mechanical advantage, but a great deal more of it is needed — and observed the line need not be continuous with the halyard but could be, saving some clutter, along with a tip for preventing tangles.[7] See [components/rig/furling.md — a continuous halyard/furling-line innovation](/components/rig/furling.md#alastairs-fuller-walkthrough-and-a-continuous-halyardfurling-line-innovation).

He also confirmed from use that sailing with the halyard slack and the boom down on the deck or cabin makes tacking and gybing awkward,[11] and pressed a fellow correspondent to clarify what removing the tack strop entirely would demonstrate, beyond releasing the whole rig assembly from the deck.[12] See [components/rig/furling.md — what removing the tack strop entirely would demonstrate](/components/rig/furling.md#a-follow-up-what-removing-the-tack-strop-entirely-would-demonstrate).

## Rig trim for windward ability in a seaway

Asked how to rig the sail for windward ability, Wright gave the archive's most direct answer on sail shape: in a strong wind the sail needs to be relatively **flat**, and should show some luffing — about 5-10% of the time in that kind of seaway, as the boat pitches forward. He tied the trim question straight back to the hull, since the boat must maintain good forward speed for the chine runners to be effective at all, and asked the question that usually settles such cases: whether the boat is being sailed **heeled, as it should be**, or flat.[13] See [components/rig/index.md — sheeting position and sail flatness](/components/rig/index.md#sheeting-position-and-sail-flatness-for-windward-sailing-in-a-seaway) and [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — why heavy displacement and heel are essential](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#why-heavy-displacement-and-heel-are-essential).

## Chine runners: what they need to work

In the December 2009 debate over fitting chine runners to other hulls, Wright synthesized the conclusion the thread had been circling: chine runners work only with help from the hull itself, plus an oversized rudder — which pushes the center of effort farther aft than normal practice. On the specific proposal to fit them to a PDRacer he judged them simply the wrong fitting, adding that the point was moot regardless, since the class's own rules bar horizontal foils whatever their effectiveness.[14] See [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a December 2009 debate](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-december-2009-debate-the-triloboat-16s-negative-result-and-matts-own-explanation-restated). Restated the point again in 2011, answering a newcomer's question about building the hull in foam instead of plywood: the runners are not a stand-alone device like a centerboard or leeboard, but an extension of the chine itself, effective at any depth so long as the chine — and the lifting-body hull behind it — is deep enough to matter; "a boat is a sum of the parts." See [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a 2011 restatement](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#why-heavy-displacement-and-heel-are-essential).[22] On whether shorter, wider runners would improve on Paradox's own shape, he reduced the whole idea to a reductio: raising a runner's aspect ratio for efficiency eventually turns it into a bilgeboard, "then the unobtrusive chine runner becomes an appendage...then you gotta deal with that thing sticking out of the bottom/side of the boat," at which point it might as well be moved to the centerline and made retractable — "the circle is then complete."[24] See [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — shorter, wider runners versus longer, narrower ones](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#shorter-wider-runners-versus-longer-narrower-ones-a-2013-recurrence). Working through that same leeway-prevention history across Matt Layden's own boats, he placed a leeboard, not chine runners, on Sand Flea — Matt's own Everglades Challenge boat — and judged Elusion's own runners rather short but, proportionately, about the same as Paradox's; Elusion herself was a heavy boat for her size once her own crew and gear were stowed.[25]

## Ballast: "the way of the Paradox"

To a builder launching an unusually large scale-up, Wright gave the method rather than a figure: launch with a great deal of ballast aboard for the first sail, then reduce it progressively until she performs well — "that is the way of the Paradox," and the more so at large scale.[5] See [design/weight-and-ballast.md — ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast) and [people/david-nemo.md](/people/david-nemo.md), the build that drew it.

## Headroom: a pop-up top

On whether Paradox suits a taller sailor, he made the distinction that reframes the question — sitting headroom is only a constraint **while actually sailing** — and proposed a "pop up" top over the cabin's centre section for the rest of the time, needing only one stanchion in the middle.[6] See [operations/crew-size-and-fit.md](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#further-replies-a-yrvind-pointer-a-recovered-measurement-a-pop-up-top-idea-and-a-standing-room-objection-february-2009).

## Elusion, and the 2010 Ultimate Florida Challenge

Wright is the archive's only correspondent to have **inspected Matt Layden's Elusion in person**. Keeping the visit to about ten minutes on the Thursday evening before the race start so as not to intrude, he found the hull finished to a high standard — assumed to be 6 mm marine plywood glassed on the exterior — with construction and testing still being completed close to race day, and Matt weighing how much lead-shot ballast to add on top of the gear already stowed, "a fine line between too much and too little in such a little skinny boat." The two pipes protruding from the rear of the cabin were larger than he expected and appeared to be tied into a mainsheet drainage channel running beneath the deck.[15] Earlier, from the pre-race photographs alone, he had picked out a small end plate on the rudder cheek plate — reasoning it could only be on the port side, opposite the swing-up rudder — which proved to be the same reboarding step Paradox carries on her own rudder.[4] See [matt-layden/elusion.md — first archive sighting](/matt-layden/elusion.md#first-archive-sighting-february-2010).

Once the race was under way he became one of its two main narrators, supplying local knowledge the tracker could not: that the St. Marys River flows to the Atlantic, so the fleet would be paddling upriver against the current until the roughly forty-mile portage carried them to the Suwannee and a downstream run,[17] and the portage's own ChallengeMapper waypoints — Traders Hill CP to Fargo CP — when the route itself came into question.[19] On speed he could speak firsthand, having watched the first leg's Saturday start: about 4 knots or better running downwind, with readings as high as 2.4 knots paddling against the river current.[18] The full day-by-day account is at [merits/ultimate-florida-challenge-2010.md](/merits/ultimate-florida-challenge-2010.md).

He had entered a WaterTribe event himself that same season and withdrew on its first day in the Gulf, citing the difficulty of steering downwind in the conditions and how easily a small steering error could have put him into cold water — an experience he drew on directly when reading another competitor's speed data, recalling that comparable readings on his own race had come in the approach to Venice Inlet.[16] Watching Matt sail left him with a firsthand verdict he repeated more than once: *"Matt is the most efficient sailer I have ever seen. I could pass him but every time I started screwing around and not paying attention... I would look up and there he was... again."*[20] Of the race's outcome he took the same view throughout: "Anyone that finished wins, in my book. Two days or two minutes don't mean nothing. They are all heros to me."[20]

His last word on the boat was a personal one. Replying to speculation that "Elusion" might be a nod to the late [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s own unbuilt "Illusion," he recalled having watched Matt letter the boat's name onto her transom in person, at "the park" — a sight that reminded him of one of Don's cartoons, and of how much this kind of boat had been Don's love.[21]

## Watertribe wisdom: "What would Wizard do?"

Recalling a Watertribe race crewed alongside a companion known as Gary "Lugnut," Wright distilled the tribe's own shorthand mantra into its plainest statement yet: **"What would Wizard do?"** — Matt Layden being "the Wizard of the course, not counting his other talents." Weather on the Gulf coast in early March is a roll of the dice, from 35 degrees and raining to 80 and sunny, so the only sound plan is to prepare for the worse case, cold and rain. A race is finished by its crew as much as its boat — "the boat will not go farther than the crew" — and Gary "Lugnut" proved the point directly: knowing the course well enough to find a good anchorage at 11 pm, when the crew's own judgment was already failing, gave them shelter from a wind shift that reached gale force overnight, so they were rested and ready to go again at daybreak once the front had passed — "that in retrospect was more important than the boat or sailing talent."[23]

**Disambiguation.** On this same March 2012 thread, John Gilbert (`gilberj55`, see [John Gilbert](/people/john-gilbert.md)) also posted, opening his own reply "hi Nels" — a different account, handle, and person sharing only a first name with Wright.

## Local knowledge and pointers

- **The Gulf of Mexico.** His earliest recorded post defended the difficulty of a Gulf of Mexico passage leg against the suggestion it was easy water: a 35 mph wind "salt water pond," rarely calm except in a hurricane.[1]
- **Where to get plans.** He pointed a prospective builder to the paradox-uk.co.uk site's own "building" section for the current distributor's address, and separately suggested buying Don Elliott's book.[2] See [references/building-plans.md](/references/building-plans.md#source-and-cost).
- **Unrecoverable replies.** Five further replies survive in the archive as headers only, with no body text of any kind, and — unlike citations [22][24][25] above — no later message quotes any of them back, so none could be recovered: one on the "Paradox on Jim Michalak's web site" thread,[26] one on "Scout -inside and out!",[28] one on a 2014 "Width" thread,[29] one on a further 2014 "Re: Longer Cruises" thread,[30] and one on a still later 2014 "Re: Longer Cruises" thread.[31]
- **Staying cool.** Sailing the Gulf of Mexico for thousands of miles, and having cruised Mexico to the Bahamas and back over a summer, he gave the fullest hot-weather cabin-comfort routine on record — full detail at [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md#a-hot-weather-cabin-cooling-routine-air-scoop-mosquito-netting-and-a-wet-mattress-trick).[27]
