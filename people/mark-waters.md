# Mark Waters

**Mark Waters** is a Paradox builder who introduced himself to the group in February 2006 from Los Angeles, California, registering his boat with a UK build registry; his Paradox — later named **"Raffles"** — was already 90% complete at the time, built strictly to the plans, with completion expected that summer. He had previously built a Selway-Fisher 50/50 sailing canoe.[1] Full detail of the registry entry is at [references/paradox-uk-website.md](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#a-2006-database-renewal-and-a-new-los-angeles-builder).

A 2014 community census lists his location as Portland, Oregon, and his boat's name as "Raffles" — consistent with his relocation there by October 2008, six years before the census, rather than any conflict with his 2006 Los Angeles self-identification.[2][3]

His finished interior became well regarded within the group, and a 2014 retrospective called his Paradox "one of the best built."[4]

## The self-steering wind vane

With "Raffles" otherwise finished, Waters' one remaining project was a self-steering wind vane, and he asked after good design sources, whether his own thinking resembled the vane built for "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," and whether a sheet-to-tiller arrangement might be worth trying — prompted partly by finding "Raffles" tracked so well under sail that a simpler, old-fashioned direct vane, without a servo-pendulum linkage, seemed like it might suffice. See [self-steering — a further owner's tracking observation, and interest in a simpler vane](/operations/self-steering.md#a-further-owners-tracking-observation-and-interest-in-a-simpler-vane) for the general answer this drew.[5] He later passed along a free PDF copy of *Self-Steering Under Sail*, judging it strong on theory and useful for understanding commercial self-steering systems from a "reverse engineering" perspective, though offering no step-by-step building instructions of its own.[6]

Separately, Waters asked after the dimensions behind "Zoë"'s own vane gear, drawing detailed servo-blade proportions in reply and a recommendation to study the commercially built Cape Horn gear; a follow-up question about whether the Cape Horn oar swings freely, and about a Rudder-Head-Mount (RHM) design combining a trim tab's and a pendulum's advantages, drew a fuller explanation of how a rudder-head-mount gear differs in principle from a conventional servo-pendulum. See [self-steering — quantified servo-blade proportions, and the rudder-head-mount principle explained](/operations/self-steering.md#quantified-servo-blade-proportions-and-the-rudder-head-mount-principle-explained) for the full technical exchange.[7]

Having built a model of an RHM gear over a weekend and found "the penny finally dropped," Waters asked four detailed practical questions about putting one into practice — whether the rudder tiller is left to swing completely free, whether the pendulum swings out if not secured, whether the securing line around the pendulum shaft causes noticeable friction or would call for a bearing, and where the vane setting lines are best secured, including whether they can be reached safely from the cockpit in a seaway. See [self-steering — practical rudder-head-mount building questions, answered](/operations/self-steering.md#practical-rudder-head-mount-building-questions-answered) for the answers this drew.[8]

Three weeks later, with two rival models under way at once — an upside-down design and a conventional one, to compare which performed best — Waters asked specifically about the Walt Murray-inspired system's own **5 in x 32 in x 9 in** vane-blade dimensions, having read of light-air trouble with a vane built to that size, and floated switching between a larger and a smaller vane blade to suit conditions rather than settling on one size. See [self-steering — progress on two competing vane builds, and a question of blade size](/operations/self-steering.md#progress-on-two-competing-vane-builds-and-a-question-of-blade-size) for the cross-linked technique.[9]

Nearly two years after starting, Waters reported "Raffles"' self-steering wind vane finally finished — five times longer than first expected — and immediately ran into a mounting problem: set far enough aft to clear the boom, the vane was awkward to reach for routine adjustment without risking "an unplanned swim," prompting him to consider a mount that could bring it inboard when needed. See [self-steering — the vane gear finished at last, and a mounting-adjustment problem](/operations/self-steering.md#the-vane-gear-finished-at-last-and-a-mounting-adjustment-problem) for the fuller technical thread.[10]

## A full owner's assessment: performance, modifications, and a caution for larger sailors

Personal circumstances limited how often Waters got "Raffles" out over 2007–2008, but every outing exceeded his expectations — sailing her especially in cold, rainy weather that keeps other boats at the dock, and finding her easy to rig, launch, and sail. Built in Los Angeles, she had by December 2008 traveled by road to Nashville and back to the West Coast at Portland, with a further move to the Bay Area expected soon. Modifications included removing the built-in water tanks, doing away with the rudder's [swim step](/components/rudder.md), and building a raised seat that brought his seated position level with the deck without compromising the doghouse with a companionway, preserving the cooling breeze on hot days. Looking back, he would make the same bulkhead #2 adjustment other builders have made, would either remove the side bins or build in cabinets, and would make the battery compartment easier to reach, having found lifting a fifty-pound battery into that tight space on his back "quite a feat of agility." He fitted an outboard, finding the sculling oar effective but difficult to use except in a near-dead calm.[11] Despite prior boatbuilding experience, the Paradox's construction proved more complex and detailed than expected, taking the better part of a year. At **5'10" and 160 lb**, Waters found the stock size suited him without any changes needed, but suggested sailors over 6 ft think twice — see [crew size and cabin fit — reference sailor heights and weights](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#reference-sailor-heights-and-weights-and-a-two-up-alternative-the-bolger-micro). His overall verdict: a great boat, customizable "to your heart's content," with fitted cushions for the cabin sole still to make and no shortage of projects in sight.[12]

## Other contributions

- **Ballast.** Voted for 300 lb ballast in an August 2006 group poll, one of only two responses;[13] later settled on steel weight-lifting weights rather than lead — bulkier for the same weight, but chosen to keep toxic lead away from his young son, and available in a range of sizes for easy rearranging and re-securing to trim the boat. See [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast) and [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast material choice](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives).[14]
- **Epoxy.** Built the whole of "Raffles" with System Three epoxy, recommending it to other builders for its price, easy mixing, and customer service, and pointed to Aircraft Spruce in Corona as a Southern California supplier stocking it. See [epoxy brands, chemistry, and cure behavior — MAS epoxy validated for general use, and a California supplier](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#mas-epoxy-validated-for-general-use-and-a-california-supplier).[15]
- **Scarfing and plywood joinery.** Advised backing a plain butt joint with a butt block rather than relying on tape and epoxy alone, agreed the plywood veneers themselves are a good visual guide to a scarf's flatness and straightness, recommended the Gougeon Brothers' *Gougeon Brothers on Boat Construction* as the most comprehensive epoxy-plywood boatbuilding book available, and encouraged new builders to dive in without fear of early mistakes, practicing first on cheap construction-grade fir before committing to costlier finished plywood. See [construction/scarf-joints.md — choosing between a scarf, a butt block, and a taped butt joint](/construction/scarf-joints.md#choosing-between-a-scarf-a-butt-block-and-a-taped-butt-joint).[16][17]
- **Interior joinery.** Finished "Raffles"' interior in mahogany; a newer California builder, admiring the work in photographs, e-mailed Waters directly to ask what wood he had used and adopted the same choice for his own interior. See [boats/wizard-spawn.md — mahogany for the interior, inspired by another builder's finish](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#mahogany-for-the-interior-inspired-by-another-builders-finish).[18]
- **Ground tackle.** Weighed a sealed chain pipe of his own for a planned trip needing a fair amount of anchoring chain, prompting the archive's rule of thumb on chain quantity: 4 metres of chain backed by a 30 metre warp typically covers most conditions, with a minimum of two anchors carried. See [ground tackle and anchoring — a 2009 chain-quantity rule of thumb](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-2009-chain-quantity-rule-of-thumb-and-a-considered-chain-pipe).[10]
