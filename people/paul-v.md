# Paul V.

**Paul V.** is an American builder from the **Great Lakes region** who built a Paradox to the plans through a winter in a wood-heated workshop,[1] then, having proved he could build someone else's design faithfully, designed and built a boat of his own, **"[Whaleback](/similar-boat-designs/whaleback.md),"** a dory-hulled chine-runner sharpie under construction by 2006.[2] He also followed Matt Layden's 2003 Everglades Challenge win checkpoint by checkpoint on the public WaterTribe results site; the full record is at [boats/original-paradox.md](/boats/original-paradox.md#the-finish-checkpoint-4-and-the-win).[3] Full design and build account of Whaleback: [similar-boat-designs/whaleback.md](/similar-boat-designs/whaleback.md).

## Building to the plans before changing them

He built through a winter cold enough that his wood stove strained to heat his workshop, laminating deck beams from six layers of thin plywood and epoxy and keeping a "Build Book," a habit of documenting only techniques he knew to work rather than a definitive method, a discipline carried over from writing build documentation years earlier at a production boatbuilder; see [deck and cabin beams](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md) for the fuller lamination record.[4] His guiding principle was that "a boat is far more than the sum of its parts": changing one part of a refined design changes the whole system, so a stern extension or a rig change would need a full, independent redesign rather than a simple modification, and he set aside an early idea of designing a boat from scratch, reckoning it would take at least three attempts to work the bugs out of a new design. His own planned departures from the stock Paradox were smaller: a hinged, watertight hatch in place of the sliding hatch, lead ballast in place of the built-in water tank, plus extra insulation and bug protection, aimed at a planned cold-climate cruise; see [motorsailer Paradox](/design/ideas/motorsailer-idea.md#pauls-own-planned-modifications) for the fuller record.[5] The same caution carried into open water: he called himself "a real chicken" about big water and meant to prove any boat of his own first on Lake Michigan's sheltered water.[6] He also worked out a low-tech way to ballast the chine runners, packing lead birdshot into thickened epoxy by hand rather than casting or buying pre-shaped weights; see [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#lower-tech-casting-lead-birdshot-in-epoxy).[7]

## Designing and building Whaleback

Having spent a winter proving he could build someone else's design faithfully, he turned to a design of his own. He first proposed **"Whaleback"** in February 2002 as a dory-hulled alternative to Paradox's own chine-keel hull, choosing self-righting as his first priority ahead of full flotation, on [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md)'s advice on hull form, and raised [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md)'s "Bris" hull shape for comparison at the same stage.[8] By 2006 the design had picked up chine runners inspired directly by Matt Layden's own boats and become a stitch-and-glue sharpie, built after trying out ideas of his own rather than following anyone else's plans, the same design-first-then-build-it-yourself discipline that had shaped his own Paradox.[9] He named her "Whaleback" that August, working through many of the same building problems Paradox's own builders faced.[2] Full design and construction record: [Whaleback](/similar-boat-designs/whaleback.md).
