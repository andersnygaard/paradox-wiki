# William Johnston

**William Johnston** posted to the mailing list for the first time in July 2015 with his newly rigged sail already up and reefing trouble to show for it — by his own account, a novice who had never sailed before.[1] No location or boat name is given on record.

## Learning to rig and reef the sail

His sail's yard dropped at the rear when hoisted, pulling the tack-strop spacer forward off vertical and leaving the boom barely clearing the cabin top; lowering showed the same fault in reverse, the yard's peak dropping while the luff stayed taut, so the sail would not roll onto the boom to reef.[1] Still unclear after a first reply on how a doubled pulley is actually rigged on the yard, since his own halyard ties off directly to it, he asked for the arrangement spelled out in more detail — and once it was, reported it finally made sense, thanked the builder who had walked him through it, and said he would report back on his own progress.[2][3] The underlying cause and the fix — insufficient rig tension, and a compound halyard purchase — are covered in full at [furling system — a newcomer's rigging troubles, and a further compound halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-newcomers-rigging-troubles-and-a-further-compound-halyard-purchase-july-2015). Following that advice, moving the tack-strop extender closer to the mast markedly improved matters, though a further asymmetry showed up in use — the sail rolled onto the boom most evenly with the boom swung to starboard, and became difficult to roll on, with the luff rolling tighter than the leech, the further the boom swung aft.[4] An established owner suggested the cause was more likely binding somewhere in the roller mechanism than the boom's own position, recommended fitting a topping lift, and noted that furling is an acquired technique needing halyard and downhaul tension worked together at the same time.[5] A second established owner, drawing on his own boat's furling history, agreed binding must be ruled out first and offered his own fix once it is — a **half-topping-lift** tying the topping-lift halyard directly to the roller's protruding stainless bar, relieving the boom-yard-sail's weight without a full topping lift's tackle.[6] Working back through the mechanism confirmed the boom really was binding at the retaining screw, with its end hole not quite centered causing a slight wobble, though otherwise its dimensions were nearly perfect; a demonstration video clarified that both furling lines are meant to be spooled together rather than in the two separate motions tried at first, with a topping lift of his own planned next.[7] Full detail on the roll asymmetry, the diagnosis, and both fixes lives at [furling system — a newcomer's rigging troubles, and a further compound halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-newcomers-rigging-troubles-and-a-further-compound-halyard-purchase-july-2015).

## A warped boom, and a further rigging tip

A further general tip arrived in reply on the same thread — tying one continuous line as both halyard and reefing line, so one end feeds out as the other is pulled in, keeps loose rope from piling up in the cabin.[8] A separate problem then surfaced: suspending the boom by its ends and reefing it showed it was not lying smooth as expected, but warped. Unsure whether to try straightening it — weighting the centre, after it had sat resting on two saw horses for a long time, was one idea — or replace it, the boom being spruce and fir seeming the likelier wood, the question was left open on record.[9] André-François Bourbeau's own advice, drawing on his experience building canoe paddles, was to start over with very dry wood rather than try to straighten it — a warped piece "remembers" its natural shape and rarely stays corrected for long, which is exactly why dry wood, whose natural shape is already settled, matters so much.[10] He took the advice: within hours he confirmed he would remake the boom from scratch, and was already shopping for a piece of good, straight, dry Douglas fir.[11]

## A crowded masthead (2015)

Having bought a double pulley for the yard and a further pulley for the mast, ran into the same problem again a few days later at the masthead itself, where the halyard and topping-lift hardware compete for space; full detail on the layout advice this drew, and on the general rigging-friction lessons it prompted from other owners, is at [components/rig/running-rigging.md](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#a-crowded-masthead-and-rigging-friction-lessons-2015).[12]

## Rope material for the rigging (2015)

Unable to find the dacron rope specified throughout the plans locally, asked whether nylon or polypropylene would serve instead, so long as the working load was acceptable; answered that nylon stretches considerably more than dacron.[13] Still unable to find dacron locally, noted that plenty of polyester rope is available, but sellers rarely label it "dacron" in their specifications — closing his own thread on the point.[14] See [components/rig/running-rigging.md — rope materials: what is actually discussed](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rope-materials-what-is-actually-discussed).
