# Glen C. Maxwell

**Glen C. Maxwell** is a professional boatbuilder from **Live Oak, Florida** — trained building large wood-and-epoxy charter catamarans at Gold Coast Yachts — who founded the Paradox builders' mailing list in July 2001 and kept it running for the next eighteen years, while building and sailing his own Paradox, **"[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)."**[1][2][4] In March 2003 he sailed her alongside designer **Matt Layden's** own original Paradox — the first time on record two Paradoxes had sailed in company — the start of a firsthand acquaintance that makes his account one of the richer firsthand Matt Layden records on file; see the [Matt Layden eyewitness record](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md). He crossed the Atlantic the following year aboard a larger Paradox-family sharpie, and in 2016, sailing under junk rig, became the first American to take part in England's singlehanded Jester Azores Challenge.

## Founding and keeping the list

Maxwell's founding message, in July 2001, introduced both the mailing list and "Zoë" together.[4] He remained its steward for the next eighteen years, through a 2019 shutdown scare on the platform then hosting it: he identified himself as head moderator and offered to archive what could be saved, was confirmed by David C. Smith as the group's sole owner and the only one able to grant the access a migration needed, and — a few weeks later, on returning from a trip to the Azores — granted that access himself.[5][6][7] He kept the fleet's earliest running census, repeatedly confirming four Paradoxes under sail between December 2001 and June 2002 — Matt Layden's own, two more builders', and "Zoë" — with a rough geographic spread and none for sale, the reference point newcomers relied on before the fleet grew.[8][9][10] He sold spare bronze and pipe stock to fellow builders at cost, cutting and posting pieces to at least two of them directly,[11][12][13] and walked a newcomer through creating a photo album and uploading pictures to refit and rephotograph "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)," pronouncing the transformed result "can't believe it's the same boat I saw."[14][15] The wide construction, painting, rig, and seamanship knowledge he gave the list across those years is folded throughout the relevant topic and boat pages of this wiki, cited to him by name wherever it survives.

## Building "Zoë"

Maxwell built "Zoë" to a lower "ext A/B" plywood grade rather than Marine grade — an acceptable workboat finish for less money, at the cost of more finishing labor to bring it up nicely, which in his settled view "makes marine ply a steal" by comparison.[16] Seven years after her launch, asked for an owner's assessment of the design, he restated his verdict in two exchanges: "I launched 'Zoe' in 2001, it was built to plans and after all these years I still love it. I know of nothing in it's class that can compare." Pressed on what "its class" meant, he narrowed it rather than argue the point: "I don't want to get into a hair splitting contest so let's just say cruisers less the 16 feet."[17][18][19] The full build, sailing, and departure record — hours, cost, launch, sea trials, rig, and systems — lives on [Zoë](/boats/zoe.md).

## A patient tinkerer: the self-steering gear

Maxwell designed a horizontal-axis wind-vane self-steering gear on paper in 2005 and began sea-trialing it on "Zoë" from 2007 onward. Testing continued intermittently over several years, with a prolonged pause around 2007-2008, but the project was delayed rather than abandoned.[23][24][25] By July 2007 a full sea trial held a steady heading within a couple of degrees on a proper course-holding run, once early teething problems — an oar tiller jumping its crank, the vane blade blowing out of alignment in gusts, vibration-loosened screws — were sorted out underway.[26] The gear was still confirmed working as late as May 2016, days before he left for that year's Jester Azores Challenge under an entirely different rig.[27] Full design and trial record at [Zoë](/boats/zoe.md).

## A working sailor's judgment

Maxwell's stance on heavy weather is plain: under a sea anchor, pull the rudder blade fully up rather than lash it to one side, which risks broaching; and, having ridden out four miserable experiences lying a-hull on other boats, carry a sea anchor rather than heave to.[28] The practice was tested on a delivery off St. Croix, where a 30 ft sea anchor collapsed and came aboard again in just 30 minutes.[29] Reading Gerry Spiess's *Alone Against the Atlantic* prompted a further precaution he floated but never reported building — seat belts at shoulder, thigh, and shin level, fastened to eye bolts in the cabin sole and stowed in a locker when not in use.[30] Not every idea of his own held up without a laugh at his own expense: sculling hard to clear a fishing pier once cost him a whole rode-and-anchor bag over the side, to the jeers of the pier fishermen watching, a lapse an afterdeck bungee has since prevented.[31] He backs the Build Manual's full tool list as a starting point rather than a requirement — he "cannot imagine working without" his own table saw, but agrees Paradox could be built with a hatchet and a hand saw, and holds that a tool earns its place only once a real need for it appears.[32][33] The instinct to overbuild goes back further than Paradox: crewing a Gold Coast Yachts 34 ft trimaran, "Hatter," in the Around St. Croix Race, a 20-kt gust once drove her to roughly 23 kt indicated and nearly overpowered the spinnaker, and to windward she later went fully airborne off a wave before landing hard and shaking it off — her builder's own verdict, "I built her too heavy," is the case Maxwell has made ever since for building strong.[34] He feels safer in "Zoë" than in a bigger boat "because I'm sitting in her and not on her" — an endorsement, offered from 36 years' sailing and over 30,000 blue-water miles, that has directly convinced at least one newcomer to buy plans.[3]

## "Zoë," "Hedwig," and choosing the right boat for two

Nine boats, by his own last count, have passed through Maxwell's hands.[20] For cruising with his wife **Nancy**, he chose not to make "Zoë" work two-up but bought a second, smaller boat — a **Peep Hen** named "**Hedwig**," designed by Rubin Train — reasoning that a road trip to fetch her plus $3,000 bought roughly what "Zoë" had taken in materials, for far less building time.[21] A weekend aboard "Zoë," day-sailing and camping ashore, set against four days and three nights actually cruising Cayo Costa aboard "Hedwig," settled the question of two-up cruising decisively in the smaller boat's favor. The Peep Hen became something of a family boat — by 2008 his extended family owned four of them, including one built by his brother Greg, the first Peep Hen Maxwell himself ever sailed and an experience that left him surprised how well so broad-beamed and short a hull could sail; measured against "Zoë"'s own cabin, the extra room felt like "a palace."[22]

## An Atlantic crossing aboard "Hogfish Maximus"

In May 2004 Maxwell joined **Chris Morejohn** aboard Morejohn's own liveaboard sharpie, **"Hogfish Maximus"** — a roughly 38 ft Paradox-family hull, sized up from the same shoal-draft, flat-bottomed lineage — for an Atlantic crossing to **Portugal**, by way of **Bermuda and the Azores**, with three months allowed for the passage, including an extended stay visiting all the islands of the Azores.[35][36] See [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) for the boat's designer and builder's own boats and background, and ["Hogfish Maximus"](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md) for the hull as a design in its own right.

### A report from Horta, and chine runners proven on an ocean crossing

A report from **Horta, in the Azores**, in mid-June 2004 covers the crossing in full. It went well — never rolled or sunk — though slow: the leg to **Bermuda** took nearly 14 days for **1,070 miles** in light winds; after a 10-day stay there, the leg on to **Flores** in the Azores took almost 18 days for **1,890 miles**, riding the tail of a front for 20-plus-knot following westerlies and a best day's run of **138 miles** under about a third of the jib and a poled-out staysail, altering south of the planned 39N track to dodge forming lows and gale-force winds. After barely a day at Flores — its harbor, exposed to the northeast, had built to 5 ft swells as another low approached — a last 24-hour hop brought them into Horta.[37]

Maxwell's own verdict, bearing directly on [Paradox's blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md): the **chine runners work well even at this larger size**, the daggerboard needed only when hard on the wind and used for under 48 hours of the whole passage, the boat otherwise going to weather fine without it. She pounded no more than some racing boats he had sailed elsewhere, moved comfortably, and was quiet enough below that the crew had to stay deliberately alert to deck conditions so as not to miss deteriorating weather. Set against an earlier Tortola-to-Jacksonville delivery aboard a Hans Christian 38, "Hogfish Maximus" was the more comfortable boat of the two, with noticeably better roll damping. The 38-footer itself was too big for his own taste, but the crossing left him with a settled view that a **21 to 25 ft version of Paradox** would suit ocean voyaging well.[37]

### The full return report: cockpit size, pounding, and "Modified Sharpie" versus sharpie

A fuller verdict followed in January 2005. Loaded to the waterline with the boat's own tools, spares, and close to a year's food for a family of four — not sailed light — "Hogfish Maximus" behaved beautifully across the whole crossing; the one real shortcoming was too small a cockpit. Pounding was no issue at all, markedly better in that respect than fin-keeled boats he had sailed elsewhere, and broad-reaching in 12-15 kt against the same Hans Christian 38 comparison was far more comfortable aboard "Hogfish Maximus" — the Hans Christian rolled badly enough on the same point of sail to make sleeping difficult, where the bigger sharpie's motion stayed comfortable with almost no rolling, matching what he had already felt firsthand aboard "Zoë."[38]

One design point he passed on directly from the boat's own designer: though "Hogfish Maximus" resembles an enlarged Paradox, she is not a scaled-up Paradox but a separate design, reached through a separate process. A firm line was drawn between her and traditional sharpies — which sit low in sheer and center of gravity and sail differently — with the term **"Modified Sharpie"** coined to mark the distinction.[38] Off the wind, she wandered under hand steering and cost real effort chasing the course back after an over-correction — a trait he judged possibly his own, since Morejohn never complained of it — and a hoped-for objective comparison never quite happened: a 300-mile non-stop test course planned for "Zoë" in the Gulf of Mexico, staying within 50 miles of port throughout, was meant to gather three or four days of her own at-sea data against "Hogfish Maximus" on this score, once a suitable weather window arose.[39]

## The 2016 Jester Azores Challenge

By 2016 Maxwell had moved to sailing junk-rigged boats more generally. That May he was in England, entered in the **Jester Azores Challenge** — a small-boat, singlehanded ocean race in the Jester Challenge tradition — as the first American to take part, aboard a junk-rigged boat he called **"Tao Zou."** Shortly before leaving, his own self-steering gear for "Zoë" was confirmed still working well; partway across, bad weather cost him the two uppermost panels of his junk sail and put his self-steering out of action.[27] Dave Bolduc, who had not spoken to him in years, was amazed at the news and recalled having already seen video online of him sailing junk-rigged boats.[40] Days from the finish, with under 30 nautical miles to go, his wife **Nancy** was already waiting on the island for him.[41] He made landfall at Praia da Vitória after a hard beat through what fellow builders following along judged a very tough season for weather — by one account from the marina, the first junk-rigged boat in that year's field, with restaurant patrons by the water spotting his small mast and rushing out into the street to meet him.[42] Once repairs were made, he meant to sail on toward Florida with Nancy.[43]
