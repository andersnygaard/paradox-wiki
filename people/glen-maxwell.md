# Glen C. Maxwell

**Glen C. Maxwell** is a professional boatbuilder from **Live Oak, Florida**,[1] trained building large wood and epoxy charter catamarans at Gold Coast Yachts.[2] He founded the Paradox builders' mailing list in July 2001, introducing it together with his own Paradox, **"[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)"**,[3] and kept it running for the next eighteen years. In March 2003 he sailed her alongside designer Matt Layden's own original Paradox at Ft. Desoto, days before that year's Everglades Challenge, the start of a firsthand acquaintance recorded in full at the [Matt Layden eyewitness record](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md). He crewed the following year's Atlantic crossing of a larger Paradox-family sharpie, and in 2016, sailing under junk rig, became the first American to take part in England's singlehanded Jester Azores Challenge. Full build account: [boats/zoe.md](/boats/zoe.md).

## Founding and keeping the list

A 2019 scare on the hosting platform threatened the list's continuity but cleared without lasting disruption; the full account of that episode is at [references/mailing-list.md](/references/mailing-list.md#the-2019-yahoo-groups-shutdown-scare).[4] By June 2002, four Paradoxes were known under sail: Matt Layden's own, two more builders' and "Zoë," with a rough geographic spread and none for sale.[5][6][7]

## Building "Zoë"

Maxwell built "Zoë" to a lower workboat grade of plywood rather than Marine grade, judging in his settled view that the extra finishing labor a fine finish demanded makes marine-grade ply a bargain by comparison.[8] Seven years after her launch, he judged he knew of nothing in her class to compare with her, an assessment he narrowed to cruisers under sixteen feet.[9][10] The fuller build, sailing and systems record, hours, cost, launch, sea trials and rig, is at [Zoë](/boats/zoe.md).

## A patient tinkerer: the self-steering gear

Maxwell designed a horizontal-axis wind-vane self-steering gear on paper in 2005 and began sea-trialing it on "Zoë" from 2007 onward, working through early teething problems, an oar tiller jumping its crank, the vane blade misaligning in gusts, vibration-loosened screws, to a full course-holding trial that July that held a steady heading within a couple of degrees.[11][12] Testing continued intermittently over several years and the gear was still working in May 2016, days before he left for that year's Jester Azores Challenge under an entirely different rig.[13] Full design and trial record at [Zoë](/boats/zoe.md) and [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md).

## A working sailor's judgment

Maxwell's approach to heavy weather is plain: under a sea anchor, pull the rudder blade fully up rather than lash it to one side, which risks broaching, and, having ridden out four uncomfortable spells lying a-hull on other boats, carry a sea anchor rather than heave to.[14] The practice was tested on a delivery off St. Croix, where a 30 ft sea anchor collapsed and came aboard again within thirty minutes.[15] He backs the Build Manual's full tool list as a starting point rather than a requirement, judging that Paradox could be built with only a hatchet and a hand saw and that a tool earns its place only once a real need for it appears.[16][17] The instinct to overbuild goes back further than Paradox: crewing the Gold Coast Yachts trimaran "Hatter" in the Around St. Croix Race, a gust once drove her to over 20 knots and nearly overpowered the spinnaker, and to windward she later went fully airborne off a wave before landing hard and shaking it off; her builder's own verdict that he had built her too heavy is the case Maxwell has made ever since for building strong.[18] He feels safer in "Zoë" than in a bigger boat "because I'm sitting in her and not on her," an endorsement he offers from thirty-six years of sailing and over 30,000 blue-water miles.[19] Not every idea of his held up without a laugh at his own expense: sculling hard to clear a fishing pier once cost him a whole rode and anchor bag over the side, a lapse an afterdeck bungee has prevented since.[20]

## "Zoë," "Hedwig," and choosing the right boat for two

Nine boats, by his own count, have passed through Maxwell's hands.[21] Rather than adapt "Zoë" for two-up cruising, he bought a second, smaller boat, a Peep Hen named "Hedwig," designed by Rubin Train, reasoning that a road trip to fetch her plus $3,000 bought roughly what "Zoë" had taken in materials, for far less building time.[22] A weekend aboard "Zoë," day-sailing and camping ashore, set against four days and three nights actually cruising Cayo Costa aboard "Hedwig," settled the question of two-up cruising decisively in the smaller boat's favor. The Peep Hen became something of a family boat, with four owned across his extended family by 2008, including one built by his brother Greg, the first Peep Hen he himself ever sailed and an experience that left him surprised how well so broad-beamed and short a hull could sail.[23] Full comparison at [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md).

## An Atlantic crossing aboard "Hogfish Maximus"

In May 2004 Maxwell joined Chris Morejohn aboard Morejohn's own liveaboard sharpie, "Hogfish Maximus," a roughly 38 ft Paradox-family hull sized up from the same shoal-draft, flat-bottomed lineage, for an Atlantic crossing to Portugal, by way of Bermuda and the Azores, with three months allowed for the passage.[24] See [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md) for the boat's designer and builder, and ["Hogfish Maximus"](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md) for the hull as a design in its own right.

### A report from Horta, and chine runners proven on an ocean crossing

By mid-June 2004 the crossing had reached Horta in the Azores, going well throughout: she never rolled or sank, though was slow in light winds on both legs.[25] Maxwell's own verdict, bearing directly on [Paradox's blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#glen-maxwells-case-for-blue-water-capability-from-personal-experience): the chine runners worked well even at this larger size, the daggerboard needed only when hard on the wind and used for under 48 hours of the whole passage, the boat otherwise going to weather fine without it. She pounded no more than some racing boats he had sailed elsewhere and was quiet enough below that the crew had to stay deliberately alert to deck conditions. Set against an earlier Tortola to Jacksonville delivery aboard a Hans Christian 38, "Hogfish Maximus" was the more comfortable boat of the two, with noticeably better roll damping.[25] The full passage log, day by day, is at ["Hogfish Maximus"](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#chine-runners-at-ocean-scale--the-2004-atlantic-crossing) and [Paradox's blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#glen-maxwells-case-for-blue-water-capability-from-personal-experience).

### The full return report: cockpit size, pounding, and "Modified Sharpie" versus sharpie

A fuller verdict followed in January 2005. Loaded to the waterline with the boat's own tools, spares and close to a year's food for a family of four, not sailed light, "Hogfish Maximus" behaved beautifully across the whole crossing; the one real shortcoming was too small a cockpit. One design point he passed on directly from the boat's own designer: though "Hogfish Maximus" resembles an enlarged Paradox, she is not a scaled-up Paradox but a separate design, reached through a separate process, with the term "Modified Sharpie" coined to mark the distinction from a traditional sharpie's lower sheer and center of gravity.[26] Off the wind she wandered under hand steering, a trait he judged possibly his own since Morejohn never complained of it; a hoped-for objective comparison, a 300-mile non-stop test course planned for "Zoë" in the Gulf of Mexico, never quite happened.[27]

## The 2016 Jester Azores Challenge

By 2016 Maxwell had moved to sailing junk-rigged boats more generally. That May he was in England, entered in the Jester Azores Challenge, a small-boat singlehanded ocean race in the Jester Challenge tradition, as the first American to take part, aboard a junk-rigged boat he called "Tao Zou." Shortly before leaving, his self-steering gear for "Zoë" was still working well; partway across, bad weather cost him the two uppermost panels of his junk sail and put his self-steering out of action.[13] Word of the crossing reached old friends: Dave Bolduc, who had lost touch with him, had already seen him sailing junk-rigged boats online and followed his progress to the finish.[28] Days from the finish, with under 30 nautical miles to go,[29] he made landfall at Praia da Vitória after a hard beat through what fellow builders following along judged a very tough season for weather; diners at a restaurant by the marina, spotting his small mast coming in, rushed into the street to meet him, the first junk-rigged boat home that year.[30]
