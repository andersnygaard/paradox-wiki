# Alan (NZ)

**Alan** is a **New Zealand** correspondent, signing consistently from **43.5°S, 172.5°E** on New Zealand's South Island, who gave sustained, technically detailed engineering answers to Paradox builders on masts, batteries, self-steering and hatch mechanisms across seven years, between November 2003 and September 2010.[1][2] Among the builders he helped most was [Alberto Callejon](/people/alberto-callejon.md) of Patagonia, whose own aluminum-mast question drew one of his more detailed technical answers. He took up other builders' problems as engineering puzzles worth solving in their own right.

## Masts and rigging

Mast engineering was Alan's steadiest subject, taken up independently three times over three years. One of the earliest blue-water-suitability opinions on record for a Patagonian build, reasoned from a comparable southern latitude rather than firsthand knowledge of local conditions or of what else sailed there, went to Alberto Callejon: Paradox, in this view, was stronger and safer for its size than most other boats. See [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-high-latitude-query-from-argentine-patagonia).[4] Alberto's own aluminum-mast question needed the tube's wall thickness first; once that was given, the question drew a specific fix rather than a flat yes or no: cut the same length of tube lengthwise, force half of it inside the lower end as a sleeve and taper the sleeve's top at roughly a 6:1 slope to avoid a sudden change in strength. The full technique is at [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#aluminum-as-a-mast-material-engineering-fit-not-just-substitution).[5] A further question from Alberto, on hinging the mast to clear low bridges on the Chubut river, drew a pointer to a separate junk-rig group's own files for a hinged-tabernacle diagram, and to Matt Layden's own tabernacle on Little Cruiser as a further reference point, a secondhand mention of Layden's boat rather than firsthand knowledge of it; a same-day follow-up added a copy of the diagram to the Paradox group's own files. See [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#a-2009-question-hinging-the-mast-to-clear-low-river-bridges).[6][7]

Mast material came up unprompted once more, on a different builder's own aluminum-mast question: wood over aluminum was argued on engineering grounds, since wood floats, and a square cross-section with radiussed edges is actually the strongest shape for a given weight and holds paint better than a round tube.[8] The same exchange explained why the plans' boom carries a taper at all: it keeps the sail's shape correct as it furls and takes up the boom's own droop once fully rolled, with a suggestion to simulate the taper on an already-bought untapered tube using glued-on wood or foam strips rather than rebuilding it from scratch.[2]

## Battery and electrical systems

Electrical systems were Alan's second recurring subject, taken up independently several times rather than once. Sealed-battery orientation and leak risk came up first as a practical concern,[9] followed later by sealed lead-acid and gel-cell batteries weighed against an ordinary motorcycle battery's discharge cycling for suitability aboard,[10] and, separately, the hazard of chlorine gas forming where a battery meets salt water through electrolysis, with an argument for a genuinely waterproof, jettisonable battery housing rather than trusting a sealed case alone.[11] The arithmetic of using batteries themselves as movable ballast worked out to 16 to 18 Ah cells at roughly 14 lb each, ten cells reaching about 140 lb.[12] See [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md) and [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for how these questions settled.

His most distinctive contribution here was not an opinion but a measurement: a year-long field test of a cheap solar garden light run as an anchor light, still lit at 3 a.m. in midsummer and down to about two hours after dark by autumn, a firsthand, dated data point rather than a guess. See [operations/running-lights.md](/operations/running-lights.md).[13]

## Self-steering

Alan's approach to self-steering was theoretical and thorough. The same two standard references were recommended twice, three years apart: John S. Letcher's *Self-Steering for Sailing Craft* and Bill Belcher's *Yacht Wind-Vane Steering*,[3][14] alongside a sheet-to-tiller, weighted-rudder-head self-steering idea worked through in full mechanical detail in the earlier of the two messages.[3] See [operations/self-steering.md](/operations/self-steering.md) for how the design's own self-steering options are documented.

## Hatch and cabin mechanisms

The same unhurried, problem-solving approach carried into cabin-opening mechanisms. A builder's worry about being trapped inside during a knockdown drew a bolt-on hard-top alternative to a fabric-sided roll-top hatch,[15] and, close behind it, a folding-and-sliding pop-top hatch, a "griffiths hatch," worked out in full mechanical detail: blocks sliding in coach-roof tracks, locking down when closed and freeing to slide or lift when open.[16] See [components/hatch.md](/components/hatch.md) for how the design's hatch options are documented. A separate caution addressed the transom ventilation port: an inward-opening cover could be punched in by a wave in bad conditions, met with either an inner-face handle doubling as a lanyard attachment or two back-to-back ports so one is always closable from the working side.[17]

## Disambiguation

Alan is not [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md), a separate correspondent from Yeovil, England, who has his own page; both men answer to short names beginning with "Al" in the archive, and their similar-sounding signatures have caused confusion before. They are two distinct men in two different countries, established from Alan's own repeated New Zealand coordinates and Alastair's own repeated English signature; the two also answer questions in some of the same exchanges, including on Alberto Callejon's own tiller and ballast questions. Alan's own account carries one nickname change without a change of person: an early profile name gives way to a later one partway through the record, with no break in the first-name signature "Alan" across the switch. The account-level detail is in the citations.
