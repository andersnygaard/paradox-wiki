# Alan (New Zealand)

**Alan**, of New Zealand's South Island, worked through engineering problems in mast reinforcement, battery ballast, self-steering and cabin hatches. Reasoning from a comparable southern latitude, he judged Paradox stronger and safer for its size than most other boats.[1] Full account: [design/blue-water-suitability.md](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-high-latitude-query-from-argentine-patagonia).

## Mast and rig

He proposed a fix for a builder's aluminum mast, strengthening the lower end with a sleeve cut from the same tube and forced inside it, its top tapered at roughly a 6:1 slope to avoid a sudden change in strength.[2] On a later aluminum-spar question he favoured wood instead, since it floats and a square cross-section with radiussed edges is the strongest shape for a given weight and holds paint better than a round tube.[3] He also explained why the plans' boom carries a taper at all, to keep the sail's shape correct as it furls and take up the boom's own droop once rolled, and suggested simulating the taper on an untapered tube with glued-on wood or foam strips.[4] For a mast that needed to hinge to clear low river bridges, he pointed to a diagram held by a separate junk-rig group and to Matt Layden's own tabernacle on Little Cruiser as a further reference point.[5] Full account: [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#aluminum-as-a-mast-material-engineering-fit-not-just-substitution), [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#a-non-paradox-aluminum-boom-and-the-case-for-keeping-the-booms-own-taper) and [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#a-2009-question-hinging-the-mast-to-clear-low-river-bridges).

## Batteries and lighting

He cautioned that a sealed battery can be mounted in any orientation but can still leak if cracked or badly overcharged, so a long-term installation needs real care regardless of type,[6] warned that an ordinary motorcycle battery is a poor substitute for a true sealed lead-acid or gel-cell type, since it fails within a handful of full-discharge cycles where a proper type is rated for several hundred,[7] and raised the hazard of chlorine gas forming where salt water reaches a battery through electrolysis, favouring a housing built genuinely waterproof and ideally jettisonable in a swamping emergency over trusting a sealed case alone.[8] He also worked out the arithmetic of using smaller-format batteries as movable ballast: 16 to 18 Ah cells at roughly 14 lb each, ten of them reaching about 140 lb.[9] Full account: [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) and [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#a-2009-revisit-an-in-service-battery-and-sizing-figures).

His most distinctive contribution was a measurement rather than an opinion: a year-long field test of a cheap solar garden light run as an anchor light, still lit at 3 a.m. in midsummer and down to about two hours after dark by autumn.[10] Full account: [operations/running-lights.md](/operations/running-lights.md#cold-cathode-fluorescent-ccfl-as-an-led-alternative).

## Self-steering

He worked through a sheet-to-tiller, weighted-rudder-head self-steering idea in mechanical detail, leading the mainsheet to an aft extension on the rudderhead adjustable from inside via a traveller: "I'm keen to try this method sometime, all I need is a boat!!"[11] He recommended the same two references three years apart, John S. Letcher's *Self-Steering for Sailing Craft* and Bill Belcher's *Yacht Wind-Vane Steering*.[12] Full account: [operations/self-steering.md](/operations/self-steering.md#a-pendulum-weight-self-steering-idea-and-its-limits) and [operations/self-steering.md](/operations/self-steering.md#a-recommended-self-steering-reference-catalogued-and-freely-available).

## Cabin hatches

For a builder worried about being trapped inside during a knockdown, he set out a bolt-on hard-top alternative to the fabric-sided roll-top hatch, then a hooped fabric cover built like a dome tent once the hard-top proved impractical.[13] He later worked out a sliding-and-folding hatch adapted from a published "Griffiths hatch," with blocks sliding in coach-roof tracks that lock the hatch down when closed and free it to slide or lift when open.[14] On the transom ventilation port, he cautioned that an inward-opening cover could be punched in by a wave in bad conditions and proposed either a handle doubling as a lanyard attachment or two back-to-back ports so one is always closable from the working side.[15] Full account: [components/hatch.md](/components/hatch.md#fabric-roll-top-versus-the-stock-hard-hatch-security-headroom-and-knockdown-risk), [components/hatch.md](/components/hatch.md#a-folding-hatch-and-a-combined-sliding-and-folding-design) and [construction/transom-deck-plate.md](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#a-2006-revisiting-an-inward-opening-cover-a-handle-and-lanyard-or-twin-back-to-back-ports).
