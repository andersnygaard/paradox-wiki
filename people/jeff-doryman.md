# Jeff ("Jeff UK")

**Jeff**, known through the record as **"Jeff UK,"** is a builder from the **Isle of Wight**, England, active from 2001 to 2009. By message count he was one of the ten most frequent correspondents on record, ninth of 659 senders across the whole run, his contributions concentrated overwhelmingly on construction and design.[1] He had owned as many as five boats at one point, since reduced to two, and ordered materials for his own Paradox derivative within weeks of first appearing.[1] He held to his own reading of a design's "spirit" over its letter: adapting rather than following it, never using a jig.[2] Years later he built a lighter, shallower-draft Paradox and Enigma hybrid, **"[Noli](/boats/noli.md),"** the ambition behind it traceable to a from-scratch 16 ft microcruiser he had drawn much earlier, **"[Paramour](/similar-boat-designs/paramour-design.md)."** He signed off with a line of his own: *"May the strong wind behind you never be your own."*[1] His surname is not recorded.

## Building "Noli"

Hull parts for an earlier, related project were already cut by 2005. Jeff disclosed the design under the working name "Paranigma," a Paradox/Enigma portmanteau, and renamed her **"Noli"** in January 2007, after a childhood pet name of his late mother, Nora.[3] Built to the same length as stock Paradox but roughly 500 lb lighter and drawing only 7 in, she was restated by her builder as, in effect, "a Paradox-sized Enigma."[4] Progress ran through 2008; the last dated update, that April, still had her unlaunched, painting under way, with a late-June or early-July target.[5] He detailed her fastening choices, economizing with bronze only below the waterline and brass above, and cut her deck beams from a single profiled sheet of plywood rather than laminating them, a technique that sidesteps the usual worry over a laminated beam's cross grain entirely.[6][7] A fuller account of her beamless, laminated cabin top, given over a year after his first report of it, adds the lofting, the roofline lowered by eye for headroom and the single hatch beam cut to its finished curve rather than built up.[8] For power he built his own version of a Japanese sculling oar, preferring it to the stock yuloh for the sake of his wrists.[9] He had owned an earlier boat, a Hunter 19 named "Willing Griffin."[10] The full technical record is at [boats/noli.md](/boats/noli.md).

## "Paramour," a design of his own

Before he had settled on any of this, his first plan had been simpler and heavier: a direct Paradox derivative in 3/8 in ply, glass and Kevlar over a two-layer bottom.[11] It was overtaken within weeks by a bigger idea. In February 2002 he described **"Paramour,"** a from-scratch 16 ft stitch-and-glue microcruiser sharing Paradox's rig and rudder but with a flatter hull run, motivated by the UK having no equivalent to the American rule that lets an unpowered craft under 14 ft go untitled.[12][13] The design drew the record's first buyer interest, but Jeff held off finishing the drawings, flattered but wanting to check them for errors, and busier at the time with paid design work of his own.[14] No plan set, price or sale followed. Eighteen months later he confirmed the drawings were finished after all, though by then described far more modestly, simply as an 8 in stretch of stock Paradox for extra living space rather than a new hull.[15] He also worked out, separately, a full set of scaled-up figures for stock Paradox itself, corrected the same day to a 1.15x factor once he had rechecked his arithmetic.[16][17] The habit of sketching more than he built recurs elsewhere: a complete motor-only "Power Paradox" conversion for someone else's proposal,[18] a distinct 17 ft Paradox/Little-Cruiser-style hybrid disclosed alongside Noli's own construction but never built,[19][20] and a rig for a shrunk-down 7 ft 6 in Paradox/Enigma that got no further than a promise to "get on with the drawings right away."[21] None of these became a second hull. Noli, built years afterward under a different name, is the one idea from this run that did.

## Building to the design's spirit, not its letter

Asked how to make chine runners without pre-shaping blocks, Jeff described cutting a single timber to the drawing's curve and kerfing it to bend, filling the kerfs with epoxy afterward: "in glue and dust we put our trust." He advised against facing the runners in metal without real metalworking skill, since their shape governs how well the boat points.[22] He also proposed, and nobody is recorded as having tried, a straight reach between Paradox and Little Cruiser with her centerboard raised, to settle for good whether the runners work as a fence against leeway or merely help by being there.[23] Twice, a year apart, he reached for the same precedent to reassure a builder over thin plywood: Shane Acton's "SHRIMPY," a small sloop that circumnavigated on an unglassed 5/16 in skin.[24][25] He recommended a proper 3D modelling program over the more architectural Google Sketchup for drawing a boat's individual parts and assembling them into a whole model.[26] Sourcing, for him, ran through what could be found rather than what was listed: "I want my cake and eat it Derek. I want marine ply and the toys, that I know I wont use. Thank heaven for eBay."[31]

That same instinct shaped his case against changing Paradox itself. He argued against fitting bilge keels: they add drag and need extra internal structure, draw more water and show more leeway when heeled and suit a flat-bottomed hull poorly regardless besides, pointing to a 16 ft rowing skiff of his own design that a later owner cut open for an outboard it was never meant to take as the model of a boat compromised past its purpose.[27] Against building Paradox in steel, he judged the design's whole point is that it can be built by anyone with modest woodworking skill, and that steel buys fabrication problems, shaping the chine runners, fairing a round chine edge, fitting a wooden deck to a steel hull, for no real gain when the plywood original has already sailed thousands of miles.[28] He judged the fullest technical rebuttal of a steel Paradox on record, from a professional boatbuilder, as hitting "the nail right on the head," and used the moment to lay out his own concrete build plan: 3/8 in sides, a two-layer bottom, solid chine runners screwed in place and egg-box frames sharing the water tank's longerons, delayed three years but about to begin, the epoxy kept at a constant low temperature throughout the wait.[29] He raised the same objection against a full-skeg, barn-door rudder redesign put to him once: it shifts the center of lateral resistance and the sail's center of effort aft, changing the sail's lead for no clear gain. "The thing about PARADOX is her simplicity."[30] He called Matt Layden, whose own plywood-specification reasoning he welcomed into one of these same arguments, "a far sighted tinker."[32]

## A wind-vane maker who thought Paradox needed none

Jeff's working life ran through drawing offices: twelve years producing drawings for sheet-metal workers and welders at a large engineering firm, then contract drawing-office work for boat and hovercraft builders.[33] By 2004 he directed a UK manufacturer of servo-pendulum wind-vane self-steering gear, described as the lightest of its kind.[34] Despite an obvious commercial stake in selling more of it, he judged fitting a wind vane to Paradox "a waste of time": the boat sails at too shallow an angle of heel for the gear to earn its keep, and Matt Layden's own Everglades Challenge win, sailed largely hands-off, was proof enough that Paradox already looks after itself without one.[34] He judged the whole question "purely academic" for what he called a coastal cruiser that only thinks it's an offshore one, explicitly excepting Matt, Dave and Mindy, in whose hands she already is one.[35] Set against a 14 ft West Wight Potter, he judged Paradox likely the better sea boat, pointing to a published account of the Potter's own ocean passage.[36] His own preference for a modified sculling oar over the stock yuloh traced to an old injury: weak wrists from clenching copper nails on his first job, planking a 46 ft motor-sailer.[9]

## One builder under two names

Jeff wrote first in 2001 under one e-mail account, then fell silent for about thirteen months before returning in 2003 under a second. Across both he kept the same profile handle throughout, and signed variously "Jeff," "Jeff UK," "Jay," and "Jeffery Please," the last of these a running pun on his own first name rather than a real surname. His first appearance under the second account came soon after Matt Layden's 2003 Everglades Challenge win, welcoming him and asking for detailed photographs of the boat.[37] That September, a fellow correspondent asked in public whether anyone had heard from "Jeff Please," recalling the 16 ft stitch-and-glue design he had once been working on.[38] The same day, Jeff answered in his own words: "I'm still here. Lurking. I am still going to build a PARADOX but still have not started yet due to work commitments."[15] It is the clearest link in the record between his two accounts, one man answering, unprompted, to a name only the earlier one had used. That September he also met [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) in person, aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" at Cowes, remarking on how much room Matt Layden had designed into so small a boat, and confirmed he was still on the Isle of Wight three years later.[15][39] He defended a departing correspondent's choice to leave for a motor cruiser, on the grounds that most of the several hundred people on the record were never Paradox owners in the first place, and separately observed that secondhand microcruisers in the UK ran to roughly twice US prices.[40][41] He also gave the fullest defense on record of the plans' own toe-rail shape over a bolt-on metal substitute: cheap, low in windage and self-cleaning, against an aluminum extrusion that would sit at the wrong angle and open leak paths at every mounting screw.[42]
