# Jeff (UK, "doryman_uk")

**Jeff**, a UK-based amateur boatbuilder and designer posting to the `paradoxbuilders` list under the e-mail handle **doryman_uk** (also as "Jeff Please"), ordered materials to build his own [Paradox](/paradox.md) derivative and became one of the list's most active construction voices.[1] He had owned as many as five boats at one point (since reduced to two), worked professionally on design projects, and drew his own 16 ft microcruiser, "Paramour," as an enlarged from-scratch answer to Paradox.[1][2] He described his approach to any design as adapting its "spirit" to his own building style rather than following it rigidly, disliking jigs, and signed off on the list with the recurring motto *"May the strong wind behind you never be your own."*[1][3] His surname is not recorded.

## What he built and designed

Jeff ordered material for a Paradox derivative but, with only a lean-to behind his house to build in, planned to start the following spring.[1] His build plan called for **3/8 in marine plywood sides** and a **two-layer 3/8 in ply bottom** with a heavy glass-and-Kevlar layup — reasoning that few builders cruise as hard as Matt Layden or the Bolducs, that 3/8 in ply matches 1/2 in exterior ply for strength while lasting longer and bending fairer, and that on a wider (4 ft) stitch-and-glue bottom the [chine runner](/chine-runners/construction.md) would be fixed outside, flush, then glassed over.[4] See [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md) for the full thread.

His larger project was **"Paramour,"** a from-scratch 16 ft stitch-and-glue microcruiser sharing Paradox's rig and rudder but with a flatter hull run — 1,507 lb displacement and a .566 prismatic coefficient (via PLYBOATS) against Paradox's 1,410 lb and .580.[2] It grew from a speculative "design a new 16 ft hull" idea — floated because the UK has no US-style 14 ft trailering rule (in the US, an unpowered craft under 14 ft needs no hull registration numbers)[5] — into a named design drawn over several months and estimated ~0.5 kt faster than stock.[6] Its reveal drew the design's first recorded buyer interest, with two list members asking to buy a plan set; flattered, Jeff held off until he could check the drawings for errors, citing a crowded workload (drum fittings, a 53 ft motor-cruiser layout for a friend) and a struggle to get a scanner working to post a sketch, and no completed plan set, price, or sale is recorded.[7] See [Paramour](/similar-boat-designs/paramour-design.md) for the full specification and [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) for the enlargement debate.

## Construction contributions

- **Chine-runner construction, the kerf-and-bend method:** rather than pre-manufacturing runners from small blocks, cut a single timber to the drawing's shape and saw kerfs (4–6 in apart, ~12 mm of wood left, cut deeper toward the sharper sweep aft) through the back and bottom edges so it bends to the curve, then fill the kerfs with epoxy — "in glue and dust we put our trust." See [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md).[8]
- **Against metal-edging the runners:** the runners' shape governs the boat's **windward ability**, so refacing them in metal risks the hydrodynamics unless the builder has real metalworking skill.[8]
- **How chine runners work:** they act as a **fence** stopping water from breaking off the hull sides and running underneath — the main cause of leeway on a flat-bottomed hull — rather than resisting leeway like a centerboard, and so should work at any angle of heel. A head-to-head reach between [Paradox](/paradox.md) and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) (centerboard raised) was proposed to settle the point; no such test is recorded. See [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).[9]
- **Thin-ply precedent:** **Shane Acton's "SHRIMPY,"** an 18 ft 6 in Caprice-class sloop built near Cowes that circumnavigated on a 5/16 in plywood skin with no glass, was cited repeatedly — both to justify light scantlings on his own build and to reassure a builder whose secondhand Paradox turned out to have ~6 mm sides rather than the specified 12 mm that 6 mm plus glass was fine on a 13 ft 10 in boat. See [Help! — undersize side panels](/construction/undersize-side-panel-ply.md).[4][10][11]
- **Materials sourcing:** a Kevlar bottom never meant paying retail — a Paradox needs only ~5 sq m, best scrounged as offcuts from a race-boat yard. See [epoxy and fiberglass materials sourcing](/references/materials-sourcing-links.md).[12] A UK epoxy/copper antifouling coating, **"Copperbot,"** claimed to last ~10 years, is also on record from him.[6]
- **Ply thickness and fastener choice for topside panels.** Exterior-grade ply's extra thickness (1/2 in per the plans) is needed because it's weaker than marine ply, which can go down to 3/8 in instead; brass screws, backed by the epoxy bond and a glass-cloth covering, need no second thought as a fastener. See [fastening and gluing — ply thickness and fastener choice](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#ply-thickness-and-fastener-choice-for-topside-panels).[13]
- **NOLI's laminated parts, and an economical fastening split.** Only three of NOLI's parts were laminated from plywood — the coachroof's forward section, the windscreen's front, and the hatch top — fastened throughout with brass screws and glue; fitting bronze only below the waterline and brass above is one way to economize while still using bronze where it matters most. See [boats/noli.md](/boats/noli.md#laminated-parts-and-a-belowabove-waterline-fastening-idea).[14]
- **Deck beams cut from a solid profile, not laminated.** Rather than laminate NOLI's deck beams from thin strips over a jig, as in the stock design, Jeff cut the beam profile directly from a single piece of 3/8 in marine plywood, glued and screwed alongside a matching-length piece of 3/4 in deal (spruce) — the plywood alone strong enough to take his full weight, the deal there only to stop it flexing and to give the deck a bearing surface. See [deck and cabin beam lamination — a profile-cut alternative](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#a-profile-cut-alternative-no-lamination-at-all).[15]
- **A 3D-modeling recommendation.** Recommended TrueSpace, a genuine 3D modeling program, over Google SketchUp — which he judged better suited to architectural work — for drawing a boat's individual parts and assembling them into a complete model, noting the free 7.5 version (up from the roughly £240 he had once paid for version 6.6) came with a manual, video tutorials, and an active user forum. See [people/stephen-yahn.md — asking about Google Sketchup](/people/stephen-yahn.md) for the fuller exchange.[16]
- **Cabin top construction, the full build sequence.** A fuller, step-by-step account of NOLI's beamless cabin top than his original 2007 report — lofting the side panels, laminating the windscreen and roof over formers, adjusting the roofline for headroom by eye, and building the single forward-hatch beam to the finished curve rather than laminating it — given in reply to a fellow builder's own forward-beam question. See [boats/noli.md — a beamless, laminated cabin top](/boats/noli.md#a-beamless-laminated-cabin-top).[17]

## Design arguments

Jeff made the case **against** modifying Paradox toward bilge keels: they would add drag, need extra internal structure, increase leeway and draft when heeled, suit the flat hull poorly, and make trailer-launching much harder.[6] The underlying principle — illustrated by a 16 ft rowing skiff of his own design that a later owner cut open for an outboard it was never meant to take — is that Paradox is unique and already proven at sea, and anyone needing more capability should choose a different design rather than compromise this one.[6] See [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).

## Disambiguation: "Jeff UK" (`roverticket` / "Jay")

A separate correspondent posted as **"Jeff UK"** (Yahoo profile `roverticket`; one e-mail header gives the real first name as **"Jay"**),[18] appearing from March 2003. Whether this "Jeff UK" is the same person as this page's Jeff (`doryman_uk`) is an **open question**, tracked separately in the project backlog: a shared scaling-answer role and reuse of the same Shane Acton "SHRIMPY" precedent are circumstantially consistent with `doryman_uk`, but **across all of "Jeff UK"'s appearances (March 2003 – February 2004) no shared boat name, location, or self-identification links the two**, and the "Jay"/"Jeff" header mismatch cuts neither way. Relatedly, a friend of "Jeff UK" — not "Jeff UK" himself — was met ashore in Portland Harbour.[19] Everything below concerns this second "Jeff UK."

This second correspondent went on to build his own lighter Paradox/Enigma hybrid, disclosed in 2006 under the working name "Paranigma" and renamed **"Noli"** in 2007 — see [Noli](/boats/noli.md) for the full build record. Only his person-level background, design commentary, and community contributions unrelated to that build are covered below.

### Background and his own build plans

"Jeff UK" had a professional design background — 12 years in a large engineering company's drawing office, then contract work for boat and hovercraft firms, with a professional-welder brother — and by early 2004 was a **director of VectaVane Ltd.**, a UK manufacturer of what he described as the lightest servo-pendulum wind-vane self-steering gear available.[20] He placed himself on the Isle of Wight and had met a fellow builder in person at Cowes, aboard "[little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," during that builder's Poole-to-Cowes crossing, remarking on how much interior room Matt Layden had designed into so small a boat.[21][22]

Ten months after an earlier query on the same subject, "Jeff UK" confirmed he was still lurking on the list and still meant to build a Paradox, though work commitments had kept him from starting.[23][21] His drawings for a 16 ft Paradox-type design were by then finished with enough detail to build from, though he described the design more modestly than the earlier "Paramour" specification — as simply **stretching Paradox by 8 in** to gain extra living space, rather than a from-scratch redesign — and his building area remained committed to two other boats.[21] In the "Steel?" thread that December he laid out a further concrete build plan — **3/8 in sides, a two-layer 1/2 in + 3/8 in bottom, solid-shaped screwed-in-place chine runners, and egg-box main frames sharing the water tank's longerons** — three years delayed by family circumstances but about to begin, using epoxy kept at a constant low temperature for those three years.[24] This stated plan differs in several particulars from the lighter, laminated-runner construction he actually used later on "Noli" (see [Noli](/boats/noli.md)).

His first appearances came soon after Matt Layden's 2003 Everglades Challenge win, including a request for detailed Paradox photos.[18] He defended a departing member who had sold his Paradox for a motor cruiser, arguing the group's several-hundred-strong membership was never mostly Paradox owners or builders in the first place;[25] he separately noted that UK secondhand microcruisers typically cost roughly twice as much as comparable boats in the US.[26] He also mentioned owning an earlier boat of his own, a Hunter 19 named "Willing Griffin" — see [Noli](/boats/noli.md#nolis-vee-shaped-washboard-and-a-hunter-19-recalled) for the context in which it came up.

This view agreed with a designer-fidelity case made elsewhere on the list while still defending a builder's right to tinker with a design (an early-automobile red-flag-law analogy), called Matt Layden "a far sighted tinker," and, once Matt Layden himself weighed in with his own plywood-specification reasoning, welcomed it, noting marine plywood was already on hand regardless.[27][28][29] See [hull planking — is a second layer of cloth worth it?](/construction/hull-planking.md#the-case-for-a-second-layer-of-cloth) and [hull planking — Matt Layden's own account of the scantling choice](/construction/hull-planking.md#matt-laydens-own-account-of-the-scantling-choice).

- **Beam-crop shortcut:** the curve dimensions given for bulkhead #2's master beam crop on plan sheet 7 are common to all four bulkheads, so a builder can mark out bulkhead #2 once and use the cut piece itself as the crop for the others — lining up centerlines and deck marks and scribing directly onto the next sheet of ply.[31] See [panel layout and nesting — the missing transom-radius dimension, resolved](/construction/panel-layout.md#the-missing-transom-radius-dimension-resolved).
- **Lead ballast:** contributed to a lead-ballast casting question.[32] See [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#casting-lead-ballast-into-the-runners).
- **The plans-request FAQ:** answered twice in one month — pointing a newcomer to microcruising.com and its sub-$50 price, then suggesting a post to the **`microcruising` Yahoo Group** directly, reasoning Dave and Mindy would be more likely to see a message there, while noting the same difficulty getting their e-mail to work.[33][34] See [building plans — two more 2004 recurrences, and how to reach the Bolducs when e-mail fails](/references/building-plans.md#why-paradox-resists-easy-enlargement).
- **Terminology notes:** the bilge pump used aboard "Zoë" (a Whale product) is sold in the UK as a "Gusher" rather than the US "Guzzler"[35] — see [bailing and bilge pumps — pump installation](/operations/bailing-and-bilge-pumps.md#pump-installation-whale-guzzler-known-as-gusher-in-the-uk); a chine-runner/spray-rail terminology confusion was also corrected in a thread about the Gibbs Aquada amphibious prototype, with the observation that its roughly £150,000 price tag could instead build a 40-45 ft steel Paradox with a helipad.[36] See [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — a later tangent: the Gibbs Aquada, and "spray rails" vs. chine runners](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#a-later-tangent-the-gibbs-aquada-and-spray-rails-vs-chine-runners).
- **Toe rail design rationale:** gave the fullest defense on record of the plans' own toe-rail shape over a bolt-on metal substitute — cheap, low windage, and self-cleaning, against a right-angled aluminum extrusion that would sit at the wrong angle, stand too high, and open leak paths at its mounting screws — settling a UK builder's design question. See [boats/enuf.md](/boats/enuf.md#toe-rail-is-it-needed-and-where-to-source-one-in-the-uk) and [blue-water suitability — a catalogue of conventional offshore features](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-catalogue-of-conventional-offshore-features-weighed-and-mostly-declined).
- **UK plywood sourcing, and a cabin-headroom/ballast exchange.** Recommended 3/8 in "Far Eastern Marine Ply," bought via UK eBay listings, for the sides and 3/4 in for the bottom on a UK build, noting the stock plans' 1/2 in sides assume exterior-grade rather than true marine ply;[37][38] in a separate exchange, argued against screwing sheet steel to the outside of the hull bottom to compensate for a lowered, more headroom-friendly cabin sole — too much hassle, and Paradox already has ample ballast room without it — while giving detailed dimensions (25 mm sole drop, 100 mm frame-3 clearance, 85 mm locally at the lower bins) for gaining headroom within the stock design; framed UK "dinghy" cruising, always near civilisation, as needing far less stores capacity than the month-away-from-support cruising Paradox was drawn for.[39][40] See [Little Jim — twenty years on: trip reports, why they've stopped, and plywood costs for a UK build](/boats/little-jim.md#twenty-years-on-trip-reports-why-theyve-stopped-and-plywood-costs-for-a-uk-build) and [Little Jim — reworking the cabin for headroom](/boats/little-jim.md#reworking-the-cabin-for-headroom-a-lowered-sole-and-an-outside-ballast-idea-discussed-and-dropped).
- **Relaying an existing scale-up.** Rather than re-deriving new figures, pointed a fellow builder enlarging Paradox to an already-worked 20% scale-up of the design's principal dimensions, sail area, and displacement already posted in the group's Files section.[41] See [enlarging Paradox for a tall/heavy sailor — a worked 20% scale-up](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement).
- **Propulsion choice for Noli.** Built his own modified Japanese "VE-SCULL"-style sculling oar, preferring its lighter demand on the wrists — the legacy of an old boatyard job clenching copper nails — to a ball-and-socket yuloh, then settled on the yuloh over the two Hobie Mirage Drives he owned: sculling standing up gives a clearer view than pedaling seated, and the drive's trunk would rise above the waterline and intrude through the cabin floor.[42][43] See [Noli — propulsion philosophy](/boats/noli.md#propulsion-philosophy-a-narrow-transom-and-a-home-built-yuloh) and [pedal power, and why a Mirage Drive does not suit Paradox](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#pedal-power-and-why-a-mirage-drive-does-not-suit-paradox).

### The "Steel?" design-philosophy objection

The strongest design-philosophy case on record against building a Paradox in steel argued: her whole point is that she can be built by anyone with low woodworking skills, and steel adds fabrication problems (faithfully shaping the chine runners, fairing a round-tubed steel chine edge, fitting a wooden deck to a steel hull) for no real gain, when Matt Layden has already sailed the plywood original thousands of miles.[44] The case was answered directly — see [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — a builder's case against steel, on design-philosophy grounds](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#a-builders-case-against-steel-on-design-philosophy-grounds).

The technical questions were then answered in more detail — distinguishing sailing in fog from navigating near it, disputing that a Bruce Roberts steel design validates a steel Paradox, and arguing that if steel were really better, Matt Layden would have built his own Paradox in it — the design-philosophy objection was restated point by point once more, and a list of steel-specific build questions was posed.[45] The thread's fullest technical rebuttal of a steel Paradox was taken as hitting "the nail right on the head," and the moment was used to lay out a concrete build plan (above); the steel question was not raised again.[24] See [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — answering the objection: fog, precedents, and fabrication specifics](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#answering-the-objection-fog-precedents-and-fabrication-specifics) and [rebuttal: why a steel Paradox would be "a horrible mistake"](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#smiths-rebuttal-why-a-steel-paradox-would-be-a-horrible-mistake).

### Seamanship and gear opinions

- **Anchoring:** Matt Layden's own view on anchoring was sought out "after all he has sailed a few miles now in his boat"; a bow-ring/entry-pipe/bridle scheme was sketched for retrieving the anchor rope from the afterdeck without going forward, with anchoring from the stern judged simpler still; a detachable anchor stowed under the floor was floated so a single rope could serve both a working anchor and a sea anchor; and the question of whether the Paradox afterdeck could simply be flat, as a work surface and short-term anchor stowage spot, was raised directly — no answer is recorded.[46][47] See [ground tackle and anchoring — a bridle scheme, and a flat-afterdeck counterproposal](/operations/ground-tackle.md#jeff-uks-bridle-scheme-and-a-flat-afterdeck-counterproposal).
- **Wind vanes:** despite a commercial stake in selling more VectaVane units, fitting any wind vane to Paradox was judged "a waste of time" — the boat sails at too shallow an angle of heel (10-20 degrees) for such gear to earn its keep, stowing spars inside the hull would foul a centerline-mounted rudder linkage, and Matt Layden's own Everglades Challenge win, sailed largely hands-off, is proof enough that Paradox already self-steers adequately without it.[20] An untried alternative seen on the James Wharram catamaran-building website was separately floated — a paddle-less wind vane, steered purely by wind pressure and mounted forward of the mast with lines led aft to the tiller — while the whole question was judged "purely academic" for a boat described as a coastal cruiser that only thinks it's an offshore one, explicitly excepting Matt, Dave, and Mindy, in whose hands she already is one.[48] See [self-steering — a VectaVane director's skeptical outside view](/operations/self-steering.md#a-vectavane-directors-skeptical-outside-view).
- **Planing under power:** the definitive answer on record on whether a Paradox can plane under power is given in full at [auxiliary outboard power — weighing a 2 hp four-stroke against a bigger motor and whether Paradox can plane](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#weighing-a-2-hp-four-stroke-against-a-bigger-motor-and-whether-paradox-can-plane).[49]
- **A full motor-only "Power Paradox" conversion scheme:** answering a query about a sail-free, motor-only Paradox, laid out a complete conversion — reduced draught and rocker, a small 2-2.5 hp four-stroke with the throttle led into the cabin, trim adjusted by tilting the engine rather than shifting ballast (the hull will not plane regardless of engine size), no chine runners needed, a forward deck hatch and solar vent in place of the vent trunk, a fold-down single-burner galley in the same area, wash boards as on Little Cruiser, and 2 in of extra transom rake with a small outboard well and skeg. See [Power Paradox — a full conversion scheme](/design/ideas/power-paradox.md#a-full-conversion-scheme).[50]
- **Boom taper and a plastic-pipe boom.** On the sail-furling drum question, agreed the boom's taper — not any drum fence — controls where the sail sits when reefed, tunable with a plane/spokeshave or built up with wrapped draught excluder to clear persistent creases, and floated a boom laminated from three or four lengths of 1 in plastic water pipe on plywood spacers (hidden by the sail's sleeve). See [rig and sail — the reefing triangle, and why reshaping the boom taper is the wrong fix](/components/rig/index.md#the-reefing-triangle-and-why-reshaping-the-boom-taper-is-the-wrong-fix).[51]
- **Enigma-style vee for an enlarged hull.** Suggested, for an 18-19 ft enlarged Paradox under construction, that veeing the bottom panel forward of midships (as on Enigma) would save a few hundred pounds of displacement. See [stretched Paradox — an 18-19 ft "maxi paradox," under way](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#a-builders-18-19-ft-maxi-paradox-under-way).[52]
- **A 7'6" Paradox/Enigma rig, mentioned then dropped.** On seeing a carbon-tape boom-core idea, declared it the answer for the rig of a "7' 6" version of PARADOX / ENIGMA" discussed off-list, and said the drawings would follow "right away" — but no drawings or further mention of this scaled-down rig followed on record, beyond a closing exchange of jokes (a Venetian-blind sail, and whether it would be "controlled by electric motors"), closed out with a one-line quip blaming a keyboard glitch for a run of missing R's. See [a scaled-down "mini" Paradox (speculative)](/design/ideas/scaled-down-idea.md#reception-a-comparison-to-the-bolger-brick-and-a-cheaper-and-quicker-alternative).[53][54][55]
- **Scaling a mast and sail together for an enlarged hull.** For the 18-19 ft "maxi paradox," gave a worked method for sizing a new rig to an enlarged hull: design the mast to fit inside the boat first, then scale the Mk2 sail up to match (expecting to reduce it slightly once drawn), using the stock Paradox sail plan as a guide for the sail's lead — recalling a past calculation that put the lead at just under **10%**. See [stretched Paradox — an 18-19 ft "maxi paradox," under way](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#a-builders-18-19-ft-maxi-paradox-under-way).[56]
- **Objecting to a full-skeg, barn-door-rudder redesign.** Answering a query about a drawn Paradox interpretation, explained why moving the rudder onto a long skeg is not a simple hardware swap: it shifts the center of lateral resistance aft, forcing the sail's center of effort and the mast aft with it, and alters the sail's lead — concluding the change is not worth making without fully understanding the consequences, since it only adds draught and risks a worse-sailing boat. Full text at [design/rudder.md — a proposed full skeg and barn-door rudder, and why it shifts the whole design](/components/rudder.md#a-proposed-full-skeg-and-barn-door-rudder-and-why-it-shifts-the-whole-design).[57]
- **Blue-water comparison.** Judged Paradox likely a better sea boat than a 14 ft West Wight Potter when answering a newcomer's stated plan to sail his own finished Paradox to Hawaii, pointing to a published account of that design's own ocean passage. See [design/blue-water-suitability.md](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2008-marina-del-rey-to-honolulu-plan-and-the-answer-it-drew).[58]

"Jeff UK" dismissed Bolger's 39 ft Fiji Yawl as a scaling shortcut on both size and looks grounds, and disclosed a separate, from-scratch 17 ft Paradox/Little-Cruiser-style design — distinct from his own Paradox-derived build under way at the time (see [Noli](/boats/noli.md)) — with early stats and, once clarified against a smaller, unrelated "Jeffs New Boat" concept, a fuller specification: a double-chined-fore-and-aft hull retaining Matt Layden's shorter, wider chine runners, 1,832 lb displacement, 0.548 prismatic coefficient, about 150 sq ft of sail. See [stretched Paradox — further scaling cautions](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#further-scaling-cautions-stability-an-existing-39-ft-bolger-hull-and-the-fourth-power-rule-restated) and [stretched Paradox — a 17 ft Paradox/Little-Cruiser-style hull, developed independently of "PARANIGMA"](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#a-17-ft-paradoxlittle-cruiser-style-hull-developed-independently-of-paranigma).[59][60][61][62]
