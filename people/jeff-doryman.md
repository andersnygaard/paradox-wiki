# Jeff

**Jeff** is a builder from the **Isle of Wight**, England, active in the design's community from 2001. Before he built anything he drew: a direct Paradox derivative first, then a from-scratch 16 ft microcruiser of his own, **"[Paramour](/similar-boat-designs/paramour-design.md),"** and several further designs that stayed on paper. Years afterward he built the boat that drew on all of it, **"[Noli](/boats/noli.md),"** a lighter, shallower-draft Paradox and Enigma hybrid he restated as, in effect, "a Paradox-sized Enigma."[1] He offered one line of his own early on: *"May the strong wind behind you never be your own."*[2]

## Building Noli

He had already begun cutting hull parts for a related project by 2005. He disclosed the boat the following year under the working name "Paranigma," a Paradox and Enigma portmanteau, then renamed her Noli in January 2007.[3] Built to the same length as stock Paradox but roughly 500 lb lighter and drawing only 7 in, she dispensed with the plans' roof beams for an all-plywood, beamless cabin top of his own devising; her deck beams were cut from a single profiled sheet of plywood rather than laminated, a detail that sidesteps the usual worry over a laminated beam's cross grain.[4][5][6] He had owned an earlier boat, a Hunter 19 named "Willing Griffin."[7] For power he built his own version of a Japanese sculling oar rather than the stock yuloh, preferring it for the sake of his wrists, worn from clenching copper nails on his first job planking a 46 ft motor-sailer decades before.[8] The full technical record, including her construction schedule, dimensions and build timeline, is at [boats/noli.md](/boats/noli.md).

## Designs on paper before Noli

His first plan, sketched within weeks of joining, was a straightforward derivative of stock Paradox in heavier ply and glass; he set it aside almost immediately for something larger.[9] In February 2002 he described **"[Paramour](/similar-boat-designs/paramour-design.md),"** a from-scratch 16 ft stitch-and-glue microcruiser that kept Paradox's rig and rudder but gave the hull a flatter run, motivated by the United Kingdom having no equivalent to the American rule that lets an unpowered craft under 14 ft go untitled.[10][11] The design drew early buyer interest, but he held off finishing the drawings, flattered but wanting to check them for errors and busy at the time with paid design work of his own.[12] Eighteen months later the drawings were finished after all, by then described more modestly as an 8 in stretch of stock Paradox for extra living space. No plan set or sale followed.[13] Around the same time he worked out a further set of scaled-up figures for stock Paradox itself, catching and correcting his own arithmetic the same day.[14] The habit of drawing more than he built recurred afterward. He sketched a motor-only "Power Paradox" conversion for someone else's proposal, a distinct 17 ft Paradox and Little Cruiser hybrid disclosed alongside Noli's own construction but never built and a rig for a shrunken 7 ft 6 in Paradox and Enigma that got no further than the promise to draw it.[15][16][17] Noli, built years later under a different name, is the one design from this run that became a boat.

## Building to the design's own logic

His own chine runners were cut from a single kerfed and bent timber rather than built up from pre-shaped blocks, the kerfs filled with epoxy once bent to the drawing's curve.[18] The same regard for the design's own logic ran through his view of proposed departures from it: building Paradox in steel bought fabrication problems the plywood original had already solved across thousands of sailed miles; a full-skeg, barn-door rudder redesign shifted the sail's center of effort for no real gain.[19][20] "The thing about PARADOX is her simplicity."[20]

## A wind-vane maker's own case against one

His working life ran through drawing offices: twelve years producing drawings for sheet-metal workers and welders at an engineering firm, then contract drawing-office work for boat and hovercraft builders.[21] By 2004 he directed a UK manufacturer of servo-pendulum wind-vane self-steering gear, described as the lightest of its kind.[22] Despite an obvious commercial stake in selling more of it, he judged fitting one to Paradox a waste of time. The boat sails at too shallow an angle of heel for the gear to earn its keep and Matt Layden's own Everglades Challenge win, sailed largely hands-off, was proof enough that the boat already looks after itself. The exception he made was for sailors taking her properly offshore, as Layden and the Bolducs did.[22][23]
