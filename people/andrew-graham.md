# Andrew Graham

**Andrew Graham** is a Paradox builder in **Brisbane, Queensland**, Australia. He had owned and cruised a 17 ft trailer sailer for about a decade, with no previous boatbuilding experience, when he took up Paradox plans, holding them nearly a year before conditions let him start: a covered patio had to be built first, then a proper shed, before any hull work began.[1] His Paradox, **[Aussie Paradox](/boats/aussie-paradox.md)**, launched as "**Zero**" on 29 November 2003, was sailed and slept aboard within days.[2] Full build account: [boats/aussie-paradox.md](/boats/aussie-paradox.md).

## Building to Australian plywood

Andrew built from standard **2400 x 1200mm** Australian plywood sheets rather than the larger imported stock the plans' own cutting layout assumes, cutting all of Zero's frames and her transom from a single sheet. Each departure this forced was carefully flagged as unapproved and undiscussed with the designer: the transom moved to frame 4's position for an extra 25mm of bunk length, the mid bins omitted for more footroom and no cleats used to attach the frames.[1] Unwilling to bend a single 18mm sheet for the bottom and finding the sides still flexed once the chine logs and sheer clamp were in, he doubled up thinner sheets instead, laminating a 12mm base panel with a further 4mm outer layer on both surfaces for extra stiffness.[3]

He checked rather than trusted: frame sides were cross-checked against the projected hull panels to within 1mm and the frame bevels checked by protractor against the lines drawing (following *The Gougeon Brothers on Boat Construction*) and matched with a batten test, the two agreeing.[1] The hull, deck framing, rudder and interior all went together to the same standard; the fuller chapter-by-chapter account is on [Aussie Paradox](/boats/aussie-paradox.md#a-fuller-progress-report-sides-bottom-deck-framing-and-interior-detail).[3]

## A robust build, and no regrets

Robust construction was a matter of principle to him: building your own boat means accepting the consequences of your own scantling and material choices, with no bias against foam sandwich, aluminium or any other material, since good boats are made from many materials. Zero's **16mm ply sides and bottom** reflect that reasoning, chosen without regret, with Shane Acton's circumnavigation in 9mm-ply "Shrimpy" standing as evidence that even a much lighter hull can serve, tempered by how lucky Acton was not to lose her on either of her two shipwrecks. Full discussion: [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md#the-case-for-a-second-layer-of-cloth).[4]

## Sailing Zero in Moreton Bay

Seeing "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"'s launch photos turned pure envy into the commitment to start building.[5] Zero sails out of **Brisbane**, in **Moreton Bay**, a stretch of sheltered water between the Gold Coast and Caloundra behind four large sand islands, used at first for a shakedown routine until Andrew trusted the rig.[5]

His approach to trim was as careful and measured as his building. An early solo sail found the boat tender under sail without ever burying her rail; in place of the plans' yuloh he sculled her with a straight 3.1 m sweep and nylon rowlock, awkward at first until he found the right angle to hold the blade, after which he was struck by how effectively it moved the boat.[2] Across successive trials he increased her ballast and stores from 165 kg at launch to 190 kg for an 18-nautical-mile passage across Moreton Bay to Dunwich and back, finding her stability and pointing satisfactory only once he reached that figure and recommending 200 kg or more as the mark for a builder wanting her full performance.[2][6] A three-day singlehanded cruise to Moreton Island the following month, in 15-knot winds and 1-metre waves, again used Zero's shallow draught to shortcut across sandbanks rather than beat around channel markers, the same tactic that had paid off on the shorter passage.[7]

He judged himself likely to become a better sailor for having no motor aboard to fall back on, and was glad to be spared the anxiety of an outboard that might not start.[6] Three years after launch, a wooden pole appeared on Zero's deck to mount an umbrella, doubling as a parasol against the Queensland sun.[8] Looking back on the build, he would rebuild the cabin front as a flat plywood panel fitted with commercial opening ports rather than rely on the stock vent trunk, trading an occasional splash and some forward visibility for better ventilation at anchor and would shrink the side windows to keep the cabin cooler; he also holds that two-up cruising succeeds or fails more on the compatibility of the crew than on the choice of boat. Full discussion: [hatch and roof options](/components/hatch.md#a-removable-roof-and-hatch-proposed-but-not-built).[9]
