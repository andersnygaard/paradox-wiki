# Bill Serjeant

**Bill Serjeant**, who also wrote under his full name **William Serjeant**,[1] is a small-boat sailor from **Essex, England**, on the **River Crouch**, who had already been sailing for years, including a boat he called "Bumper," before he took up Paradox.[2][43] After receiving the plans in October 2003 he built the community's principal UK web presence and its closest thing to a build registry, a role he held for roughly five and a half years, welcoming every UK and overseas builder who reported in, until the site closed at the end of March 2009.[3][4] He built his own Paradox, **"[Faith](/boats/faith.md)**," to the stock plans over about two years, launched her at Burnham-on-Crouch in June 2007, cruised her to the Isles of Scilly and sold her in 2009.[5] He later took on a second Paradox, kept the name **"[Minnow](/boats/enuf.md)"** she already carried and worked on her before selling her on in turn.[6] Looking back on Faith years afterward, while weighing whether to part with Minnow too, he wrote plainly: "The only boat I've regretted selling was, 'Faith'..."[7]

## The UK web presence and registry

After receiving the plans, Bill added a Paradox section to his existing site, **`www.smallsailboats.co.uk`**, then split it out into a dedicated **`www.paradox-uk.co.uk`**, giving the design's UK builder community a home-country counterpart to microcruising.com.[3] He also set up a companion online discussion group and pointed builders to a third-party small-sailboats users' group. The site went down twice in its early years, in 2004 and again in June 2006; both times Bill sorted out the registration himself and had it back up within a day or two.[8][44][45][46] Full detail, including the site's sailmaker recommendations and its role relaying the designer's contact address, lives at [references/paradox-uk-website.md](/references/paradox-uk-website.md).

He compiled and maintained the community's closest thing to a build registry: a running list of completed and in-progress Paradoxes, always dependent on builders reporting their own boats in, and its original tools list, drawn from the tools used building an earlier Paradox; see [construction/build-effort.md](/construction/build-effort.md#tools).[9] Over the following years he renewed the registry's open invitation repeatedly and folded in each new builder as they reported: Mark Waters and a Los Angeles builder in 2006, "JoHanna" in May 2007, Jimmy Lu of Taiwan that July, and Ian Lang and his "[Irene May](/boats/irene-may.md)," each welcomed by name and each already told in full on their own boat's page.[10][47][48][49] He was still fielding registry check-ins as late as September 2008, more than two years into his own build.[11]

That same month he did the same work off his own patch entirely. When the American small-craft weblog [70.8%](/references/70point8-blog.md) opened a series on microcruising with a post about his site, his boat and [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)'s, Bill answered in the comments under his own name, correcting the record with the UK build order ("Al Law was the first in the UK to build a Paradox sailboat. Derek Clark built the next which is now in Norway and others are being built here") and adding his own verdict on Faith: "I'm really pleased with mine which is easy to sail, cheap to keep and maintain." It is the only instance on record of him speaking for the UK fleet on someone else's platform.[12]

The site closed for good at the end of March 2009, after three months' notice; the community fell back on the companion discussion group, by then under new stewardship, and [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md) kept a personal-use copy of the site's content. Full detail at [references/paradox-uk-website.md: closure, and where the content survives](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#closure-and-where-the-content-survives-2009).[4] Bill also arranged for a *Messing About In Boats* sea-trials article on "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" to be reproduced on his own site, with permission, as "Zoë's Sea Trials."[13]

## Building and launching Faith

He came to Paradox already an experienced small-boat sailor, ordered his timber from Robbins of Bristol in August 2005 and spent about two years building his own boat; needing neither a table saw nor a bandsaw, he relied on an ordinary rip saw, hand planing and an electric jigsaw, with the clamp the one truly indispensable tool.[14] In March 2006, after weighing about eighty candidate names, a shortlist running from Braveheart and Crusader to Mudlark and Xanadu, he settled on "Faith": "I require faith more than anything to make my dream of ownership a reality."[15] She was launched at Burnham-on-Crouch in mid-June 2007.[5] Answering the congratulations that followed, Bill thanked Alastair Law by name: without the loan of his own "Little Jim" for a sail, he wrote, the build would never have started; he thanked Matt Layden too, as "the genius behind the drawings and research," and expected Faith to be exactly the right retirement boat.[16] Full build record at [Faith](/boats/faith.md).

## Cruising Faith, and a gale that tested her design

A member of the **Dinghy Cruising Association**, Bill cruised Faith along England's south coast toward the Isles of Scilly in 2008 and wrote the voyage up for the DCA's own bulletin; the full account is at [merits/faith-scilly-cruise.md](/merits/faith-scilly-cruise.md).[17][50] Falling overboard from Faith's foredeck during a 2007 Dinghy Cruising Association meeting at Cobnor, he reboarded via the rudder's built-in step: "with an enormous effort, I managed to hoist myself aboard by using the boarding step built into the rudder stock."[18] He wore a buoyancy aid rather than a lifejacket ever after, judging it easier to swim in even though a lifejacket would right an unconscious wearer that a buoyancy aid will not.[19]

Crewing a 40 ft yacht through a genuine Force 8 gale roughly 130 miles offshore in the Bay of Biscay and the English Channel, Bill judged Faith would have given a rougher but no worse ride, and a considerably drier and warmer one; the same voyage's sprung leak, stopped only by tacking plywood with mastic over the worst of it, and a tiller fitting repaired underway, made the case for always putting to sea with a proper tool box and bosun's chest. Reasoning from the Fastnet Race disaster that a sound boat given sea room usually outlasts her crew, he judged nothing about Faith needed changing for genuine offshore use, and credited Matt Layden with proving the boat could cope.[20][51] Refuelling underway without a harness was, he held, a deliberate choice of free movement over restriction, kept only to conditions mild enough to be refuelling at all;[21] and a Blackwater estuary outing against wind and tide, timed to reach a drying home mooring by high water, was one case where, in his own judgment, only the outboard would do.[22]

By December 2008 he had never heard of a Paradox owner dissatisfied with the design, boats sold over the years but never from dissatisfaction,[23] and after two full seasons reported nothing he would change: sailing from inside, fully protected from the weather, remained "tops" for him.[24][52] He sold Faith in 2009 to the first person who offered to take her, with "a pang of guilt" afterward.[25] Her later ownership is recorded at [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md#sold-in-2009-the-forepeak-reworked-a-higher-peaked-sail-and-the-yuloh-reshaped).

## On modifying Paradox, and what suits a sailor's build

Bill held firm, evidenced views on what the stock design is for. He argued that Paradox suits a sailor built like Matt Layden particularly well, giving his own reference figures, 5'8" and 149 lb, as "absolutely right" for the boat,[53] and that a considerably taller, heavier or less agile sailor is better served choosing a different design outright rather than modifying this one.[26] Replying at length to a fellow builder's defence of modification, he worked through the argument point by point: changing a boat's rig is a real change to the original design even when it produces a better arrangement for a particular owner; his own view that Paradox does not suit a large sailor is a personal judgment, not a claim that larger sailors owe the design any deference; and, agreeing that boats, like cars and houses, are commonly personalized by their owners, held that doing so does not itself show any lack of appreciation for the original design.[27] He put the line squarely: a sufficiently altered boat is no longer a Paradox but a Paradox derivative.[28] His own Wharram Hina catamaran was once knocked down by a sudden, freak gust on an otherwise calm afternoon, an experience he cited as the reason not to depart significantly from Matt Layden's stock design even for waters judged sheltered.[29][54]

Answering a fellow UK builder's question of whether Enigma is an equal boat to Paradox as a coastal cruiser, Bill judged Paradox the more able, more comfortable coastal cruiser and Enigma the faster, quicker-built lightweight day-sailer: an early, considered comparison of the two designs, since joined by fuller firsthand accounts from other owners; see [matt-layden/enigma.md](/matt-layden/enigma.md#paradox-and-enigma-compared-overbuilt-strength-versus-light-displacement-simplicity).[30] Building his own yuloh, he raised the plans' ambiguous curvature figure and pivot-socket dimensions in a thread that drew the fullest technical treatment of yuloh camber and gearing on record; see [components/yuloh.md](/components/yuloh.md#blade-camber-the-ford-nomenclature-and-pivot-socket-dimensions).[31][55] On a splitting boom-tang bolt hole, he held that every moving part in a boat is a built-in weakness, and that a sailing boat cannot avoid articulation altogether: Paradox's own lifting rudder, tack arrangement, roller-reefing system and boom tang fitting all simply have to be strongly made and checked regularly in use.[32] And when a fellow builder worked out a 20% scale-up, he judged the resulting boat could suit a good many people, offering "ParadoxPlus" as a name for it.[33]

## A lifetime of small boats

Self-steering interested Bill across several boats and many years, not only Faith. In 1974 he ran a Hasler SP90 windvane that worked well downwind with only slight meandering,[34] and later fitted a QME vane, cheap and popular though reliable only on an exceptionally well-balanced boat, to his own Hunter 19.[35] A 2007 report of a fellow builder's new vane gear tried at the Cedar Key gathering[36] drew his admiration: he judged the solution ingenious, having by then given up trying to fit one to Faith himself,[56] and a question he asked about a proposed twin-rudder layout prompted what became the fullest comparison on record between an autopilot and a wind-vane self-steering setup.[37] He settled, in the end, on a simple electronic autopilot for Faith, judging himself unlikely ever to attempt long ocean passages.[38]

He did not stop sailing small boats after Faith. Within three years he had taken on a 'C' Type West Wight Potter, and found her a real contrast: sailing Faith seldom got a sailor wet, since the hatch could be pulled over even in poor conditions, where the Potter demanded someone holding the tiller whenever she needed attention at the helm, and was nowhere near as easy to reef.[39][57] He logged a 66-day cruise along England's South Coast in 2013 aboard her, the only figure on record for its length,[40] and an outside weblog covering his travels that year, [intheboatshed.net](/references/intheboatshed.md), caught something no citation of his own quite does: "for many years he has taken a particular pleasure in changing boats regularly, often building the smaller ones himself."[41]

That habit extended to a second Paradox. In August 2013 Bill took delivery of [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s old hull "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)," by then renamed **"Minnow"** by an intervening Norwegian owner; he kept the name through his own ownership rather than reverting it, setting aside the sailors' superstition against renaming a boat and worked through a leak at the mast step, a rough cabin top and a polystyrene buoyancy fill he tested and found dangerously flammable before removing it entirely.[6] He put "Minnow" up for sale in 2014 and sold her on the following year; full record of the acquisition, the leak and the refit at [boats/enuf.md: second owner in Norway, and delivery to Bill Serjeant](/boats/enuf.md#second-owner-in-norway-and-delivery-to-bill-serjeant-2013). Days after taking her on, still active on the mailing list though no longer building himself, he was still passing on his own chine-log technique to a newcomer starting a build in Sweden, recalling his own difficulty bending and clamping Faith's chine logs years before.[42]

## Disambiguation

Several other men named Bill appear elsewhere in this archive, most often **Bill Longyard**; none is this Bill Serjeant. His own surname is also widely misspelled **"Sergeant"** by third parties across the archive, a misspelling that survives here only inside direct quotation.
