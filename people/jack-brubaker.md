# Jack Brubaker

**Jack Brubaker** is a Paradox correspondent from **Bloomington, Indiana**, active on the mailing list from December 2014. For most of that time he was a recurring technique voice rather than a builder of his own boat — missing a secondhand Paradox by a matter of days in December 2014, and mentioning in passing the following May that he was building "a compromise" of his own with some lighter scantlings. By November 2016 that project was confirmed as an **18 ft enlarged Paradox**, built with room forward for a composting head and general storage, and fitted with a combined foredeck hatch and anchor locker of his own design.

## Building an 18 ft enlarged Paradox

Building his own boat to 18 ft rather than the stock 14 ft gave him room forward of the mast for a composting head and general storage. Into that same forward space he built a **foredeck hatch combined with an in-deck anchor locker**, meant to let anchoring and bow-line handling be done standing in safety rather than crouched on deck; splash rails around the hatch and rubber seals against which it closes keep most water out, and he expects the hatch to help ventilation in dry weather.[11] Full debate this answered, including a contrasting built solution from another builder: [components/hatch.md — a foredeck hatch, revisited (2016)](/components/hatch.md#a-foredeck-hatch-revisited-2016-access-and-ventilation-against-dryness-and-a-solar-panel). The same combination had been proposed, unbuilt, over a decade earlier as a way to keep ground tackle out of the accommodations without losing visibility forward; see [ground tackle and anchoring — a flush anchor well](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-flush-anchor-well-weight-distribution-and-drainage).

Asked whether the enlargement had been scaled up uniformly or lengthened more than widened,[12] and, on a guess of almost 30 percent overall — the largest reported anywhere in the archive to date — for length, beam, displacement, draft, and sail-area figures,[13] he described a reshaping of his own devising rather than a proportional blow-up: only a bit wider, fuller at the transom, and deeper from deck to bottom, to fit ballast tanks into the sides with foot room left between them for a more comfortable sitting position. He drew up the plan himself, lofted it to get his bulkheads, and built the hull on a strongback; about a year and a half in by November 2016, he judged it realistic to get her wet the following spring, and was working on a blog to document the build.[14] The side-tank, raised-seat layout drew a direct comparison from a fellow correspondent troubled by bad knees, who found it much easier to get up when the seat isn't at the same level as the heels;[15] agreeing, he described himself as still very physical but troubled by unfolding after being down for a while.[16] Building the hull in sections on a strongback separately drew interest from that same correspondent six weeks later, this time for a bad back — seeing potential in the method for breaking the largest panels, the sides and especially the bottom, into smaller, more manageable pieces, and even for rigging the strongback so the whole hull could be rotated to keep the working surface horizontal and at a convenient height throughout.[17] Answering directly, he described his own panel-fitting method rather than working from a pre-drawn cutting layout: measurements taken straight off the frame to rough-cut an eight-foot rectangular panel of the width needed for the next section, clamped in place to mark the panel for trimming and to show bulkhead and stringer locations, left bare wherever a joint would later be glued, and epoxied everywhere else while still on the bench — scarfing the joints as he went.[18]

## A near-miss secondhand purchase, and an early mention of his own build

Replied within hours to a Paradox listed for sale in December 2014, giving a contact address, only to find it already sold; took the near-miss in stride.[1][2] Answering a deck-carlin question the following May, judged that Matt Layden's later, more lightly built designs carry additional structure to support their correspondingly lighter decks — and mentioned, in the same message, that he was himself building "a compromise" with some lighter scantlings, deck carlins among the additions he was making.[5]

## Technique contributions

Cautioned, citing Matt Layden's own comment, that the large lateral-resistance rudder on Layden's boats takes more load from a beam sea than a rudder built only for steering would — not a criticism of the design, but a caution toward understanding the forces involved.[3] See [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#citations).

Answering a fellow owner's November 2019 question on protecting the rudder's leading edge, offered an old piece of half-round bronze, salvaged from a former Thistle dinghy's bow fitting, screwed to the leading edge and bottom with the voids filled in thickened epoxy — adding candidly that he had separately had trouble both from bottom contact and from forgetting to raise the rudder before pulling the boat up the ramp. See [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative).[25]

Argued for a Birdwatcher-like raised-deck cabin from his own experience designing several unbuilt hulls along those lines: the farther a glazed opening sits from the sailor's eye, the less it actually shows, with heel and wave motion narrowing the view further, whereas near ports let a small head movement change the view considerably.[6] See [components/pilothouse.md](/components/pilothouse.md#citations).

On sail choice, reasoned that how much sail a Paradox needs depends mainly on where she sails: building in the American Midwest, where August winds run light, chose to maximize sail area with the Mk2 rather than the smaller sail a stronger-wind sailor might prefer, expecting to reef early much of the time but wanting all available power when the wind and water go flat — and enjoying the challenge of nursing headway out of light air.[4] See [components/rig/mk2-sail.md](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#citations).

Raised, in 2016, how a junk-rig sheeting arrangement might square with Paradox's own far-aft sail balance; the point was garbled in transmission and went unresolved, though he confirmed a tweaked sail shape or an aft-leaning sheeting spar could compensate.[7][8] See [design/ideas/junk-rig.md](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#citations).

Asked what pump other builders were using to move ballast water in and out of their tanks, and whether the filler lines in one such system ran down through the pumps themselves — the exchange that drew the fullest working ballast-pump account on record. See [people/rob-christchurch.md — a working ballast pump system, June 2016](/people/rob-christchurch.md#a-working-ballast-pump-system-june-2016).[9][10]

Prompted by a fellow owner's account of a freak wave rolling his boat 90 degrees, raised a hypothesis on the chine runners' own behavior in a beam-sea knockdown specifically: a vertical foil like a keel loses its lateral effect as a hull heels away from a wave, whereas a chine runner does the opposite, biting in and becoming more effective as the hull heels down — raising the possibility that the combination could keep a hull rolling on past 90 degrees rather than resist it. Full discussion: [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a further hypothesis](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-further-hypothesis-could-the-runners-amplify-a-beam-wave-roll-past-90-degrees-2017).[19]

Relayed a chine-depth rule of thumb from Matt Layden, agreed with Jim Michalak in conversation at Sail Oklahoma: chine runners need a hull deep enough at the chine, relative to the bottom's width, to develop real lateral resistance — Matt's own preferred ratio runs 1-to-4, chine depth to bottom width, with 1-to-5 as the outer limit — explaining why Michalak's own, shallower-chined designs don't support them. See [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — the chine-depth ratio a runner needs](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-a-critique-of-his-view-of-chine-runners).[20]

Asked Roselt Croeser, on the same "Furling Friction" thread, whether his junk-rig conversion had kept the original sail's shape or been reworked toward a standard junk-rig plan. See [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2017-recurrence-and-three-further-independent-fixes) and [people/roselt-croeser.md](/people/roselt-croeser.md#a-2017-mailing-list-exchange-furling-friction-a-later-photo-answer-and-a-bermuda-rig-critique).[21] The next day, on the same thread, asked to see a photo of the rig, or a diagram of it on the hull — answered two days later, after the thread had already turned to a Bermuda-rig question, with photos pointed to in the "Planktos" album and more promised on the next sail out.[22][23]

Answering a straight-stitch-versus-zigzag sailmaking question the same week, pointed out that a home machine's real limit is bulk rather than stitch type: it can stall outright where several layers stack up at the corners and along the luff, the area of a sail under the greatest load, holding up the peak and the yard — anything a machine cannot manage there can still be hand-stitched, as sails were for centuries before machines existed. See [construction/sailmaking.md — straight stitch versus zigzag](/construction/sailmaking.md#straight-stitch-versus-zigzag-needle-size-stitch-length-and-a-cheap-machine-that-worked-2017).[24]
