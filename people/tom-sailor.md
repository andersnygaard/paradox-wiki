# Tom Sailor

**Tom Sailor** — the name he posts under in the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) — is an **Australian** builder who **restarted a long-shelved [Paradox](/paradox.md) build in February 2020** and carried it from stored, already epoxy-coated bulkheads to a **flipped hull by the end of May**.[1][2] He had built before: a small **Wharram outrigger canoe** in stitch-and-glue, which he found harder work than the Paradox.[2]

## Coming back to the design

He began the Paradox years earlier, drawn by its "submarine" feel, shoal draft, interior space "as well as its sheer quirkiness," and less taken with its lines and "that crazy furling system." He drifted off to a **"Harmonica" shanty boat** — engineless, shorter than the Paradox, similarly enclosed and single-handed — and then to a **Wharram Pahi 26** as a retirement dreamship, until "its sheer scale the cost and complexity just bamboozled me. Her frames litter my bedroom." The recurring "warm fuzzy feeling about the Paradox" won: "it's just so practical."[1] By mid-2019 he already had the **frames and transom built** and had got hold of **CD-grade 19 mm plywood for a strong bottom**, with extra ballast in mind.[3]

## Assembling the hull

His account of the dry fit is the archive's most vivid on how a Paradox hull behaves before it is glued. Lining up the pieces "is like eating a bucket of mashed potatoes. It just feels so plastic, there's nothing to grasp hold of and know that it's right because when you align one bit another goes out." What surprised him most was needing to apply **opposing forces to the outside of the hull** to bring it true — "if someone had told me I had to apply opposing forces to the exterior of the hull, I would not have believed it" — which he attributes to the two sides having **different degrees of stiffness**. He also warns that a measurement will sometimes tell you something your eye disputes, and settled for a **centreline, square bulkheads and diagonals within a centimetre**: "One thing I know, Matt Layden is a wizard."[4] The dry fit did its job — it found a **bevel cut wrong** and a **scarf broken by over-cranking** the assembly, both dealt with before glue.[5]

He works in the tradition Don Elliott describes in the introduction to his manual, of 1970s Californian surfers who built to looser tolerances and let epoxy, roving and mat carry the strength — "That's me, not fine [joinery]."[2] A small habit worth copying: after taping scarf joins he keeps **a spare epoxy job ready for the leftover resin**, which on one occasion put a tape along the leading edge of the rudder.[6] The hull was **flipped on 30 May 2020** — "A lot less difficult than I *imagined* it could be. Such a neat little boat."[2]

## Departures, planned and made

Tom Sailor's build is not to plan, and he says so up front. He **reshaped the bow**: "I've never been in love with the standard bow profile. My intention was always to square it up like traditional old timber boats, or modern catamarans" — roughed out in April 2020 and judged "a goer."[7] He intends a **[junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md)** and "a prettier wheelhouse," the junk chosen for ease and speed of reefing and because the stock furling gear "weirds me out"; he had earlier floated a bowsprit and gaff main to make more of the short mast, and expected that to need standing rigging.[1][8]

Before restarting he canvassed building a **Paradox in Divinycell foam core** after reading Sven Yrvind, noting Yrvind's own **Exlex** was under construction in 30 mm Divinycell and epoxy — while acknowledging that the lighter, longer, narrower **Enigma** is closer to Yrvind's ideal than the Paradox is.[9] He also raised the **rudder-post-to-deck** question from a coastal sailor's angle: whether extending the post to deck level is compulsory or merely preferable, "the prospect of having a wave-gulping slot across the stern gives me the heebie geebies."[10]
