# John Starkie

**John Starkie**, of **East Wretham, Norfolk, England**,[49] posted to the mailing list from September 2013, signing "John" or "John Starkie" and referencing CambridgeSchoolofNavigation.com. He cruises the Thames Estuary, the Scottish Isles, Ireland, and the South Coast, avoiding the Solent where he can since the steaming cone is locally understood there to mean "examiner on board" — and states plainly, "Not yet in a Paradox!" A rowing eight once collided, he recalls, with either Matt Layden's Enigma or a fellow builder's Paradox while at anchor, leaving him with unanswered questions about whether a shape or light was shown, whether the rowing crew kept a lookout or observed a local speed limit, and how close the anchorage lay to a channel.[1] He bought his own set of plans in 2014,[13] but no construction start or boat name is confirmed on record; a November 2017 aside — apologizing for a wrong guess drawn from memory of his own modification to the design rather than the original plans — is the first sign he has made some change of his own, its nature otherwise unstated.[42]

## Building versus sailing

Introducing himself while making dumb-sheaves and blocks for a boat he may never build, he argued there is no rule that a builder must sail the boat they build, or that a sailor must have built the boat they sail — some build, some sail, some do a bit of both.[2] Found nothing to argue with in a fellow correspondent's philosophy of simply having fun and learning a lot along the way.[3]

## A navigation-rules exchange: lights, day shapes, and tacking angle

A single wide-ranging reply opened three threads at once. On yuloh technique: asked whether a "real" yuloh or a plain sculling oar was actually in use, pointing to Slieve McGalliard's own yuloh page for further reading. On navigation rules: IRPCS Rule 23(d) and Rule 25 let a vessel under 7 metres and 7 knots show a single all-round white light, but the same rules also call for a black ball at anchor (Rule 30(a)) and a black cone motor-sailing (Rule 25(e)), regardless of any exemption on the light itself, linking a UK MCA lighting-and-shapes document. On tacking angle: GPS course-over-ground gives a misleading figure, since a boat tacks through the water rather than over the ground — true tacking angle is defined by the apparent wind and a compass, not a GPS.[4]

Continuing the same day, added on the rules question that a posted summary list is someone's own brief and therefore a misleading paraphrase, not the regulations themselves — the IRPCS is international and includes the US, whose own MCA- and USCG-issued documents match it; the black ball is not the steaming cone and serves a different purpose, and Dutch and Portuguese waters are reported to impound a boat found without both. Restated the tacking-angle point in reply to a follow-up question: "tacking angle" is defined by the apparent wind and a compass, and while GPS course-over-ground is useful for sailing the boat, it is not the same thing.[5]

On the yuloh question, judged that Matt Layden's careful, innovative design thinking stops the moment a sculling oar is simply bent around the curve of the gunwale instead of built to the yuloh's own design — the bend is all-important, has to be in the right place for the boat and the oarsman, and has to be sharp — crediting Bob Groves and Slieve McGalliard with having put real research and thought into the question. Having sculled a conventional oar on his own dinghy for decades, "hard, concentrated work," judged a proper yuloh "a revelation" by comparison, after being let try one aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" — though it "wasn't intuitive" at first.[6]

Closing the rules thread, confirmed again that the ball is not a steaming cone; the IRPCS exists to prevent collisions, so not flying the ball fails to do all one can to prevent one even where technically exempt on the light; he and his son have observed that hoisting a cone while motor-sailing, or a ball while anchoring, reliably prompts several nearby boats to do likewise — a sailor cannot pick and choose which rules to obey.[7]

## The mast-offset thread, and converting the ballast tanks

Weighing in on why the mast sits offset to port, suspected the geometry of the mast position, the boom, and the way the boom support swings to port is carefully calculated, so that moving anything would mean redesigning the boom support and the control-line routing through the drain into the cabin.[8]

On the recurring question of converting the built-in ballast tanks to storage, said he has always been in two minds about water ballast — the plumbing is complicated, and drinking water carried in it is lost as ballast as it is consumed, taking about 11 times the stowage volume of lead, though it does keep the trailer weight down — and prefers several small drinking-water containers (2-pint and 4-pint bottles) over one large one, so any contamination stays confined to a container or two that can simply be discarded.[9]

## A 2015 carbon-monoxide and fire-risk caution

Restated the danger of an odorless, invisible killer more sharply: charcoal, wood, kerosene, gas, and even a candle under a flowerpot all release carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, gases that give no warning by smell, taste, or sight, unlike a charcoal heater's own visible, smelly "evil smoke" — genuinely less dangerous for announcing itself. Separately warned that a heater's own heat can ignite plywood and, worse, epoxy resin, which burns more fiercely than plywood, hot enough to melt the glass in the mat, releasing thick, toxic black smoke. Full exchange: [operations/cabin-heating.md — a 2015 recurrence](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-2015-recurrence-convection-physics-a-resin-fire-risk-detail-and-a-kerosene-lantern-in-occasional-use).[26]

## Bulkhead curvature and deck-beam crown, explained geometrically

Answering a fellow builder's question on why bulkhead sides carry a slight convex curve, gave the fullest geometric account on record: plywood as thick as a bulkhead's side member can only be bent into a conical or cylindrical section, and the curve and twist of the hull sides along the boat forces a conic section onto it — leaving a bulkhead's sides straight can leave the side panel not touching it everywhere, though careful nailing and thickened epoxy in the gaps, or simple shimming, can fix it. Full exchange: [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight).[14] Answering a further newcomer's own CAD-drawing questions the next day, corrected a common but mistaken shortcut: sharing one curve radius across every deck beam, rather than each bulkhead's own fair line, leaves the deck unfair as a beam's span narrows from one bulkhead to the next, needing beams shimmed and shaved afterward to compensate; also gave two sheet-specific figures, that bulkhead 3 takes two drilled holes for the tiller lines and the transom's centerline height on drawing 8 is 459 mm plus a 25 mm deck beam. Full exchange: [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#deck-beam-dimensions-and-the-deck-curves-true-shape).[15] Gave a further, complementary account in 2017 for a different newcomer, this time of the bulkhead-side bevel and its lofting method. Full exchange: [panel layout — bulkhead side curves](/construction/panel-layout.md#bulkhead-side-curves).[38]

## Chine runners as a delta wing, not a foil

Weighing in on a runner-shape thread, argued a chine runner together with the hull bottom behaves like a **delta wing** — Concorde, or a crab-claw sail — rather than a conventional foil, since its lift comes from generating vortices rather than from laminar flow, and it has no true leading edge for an aspect-ratio figure to attach to. The practical consequence: the whole runner must stay submerged, with the boat heeled enough to bring its upper, planar face toward vertical — the opposite of a centreboard or daggerboard, which needs the boat kept horizontal to stay at its own most efficient. Full exchange: [chine runners — a further 2014 recurrence](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-further-2014-recurrence-a-scale-model-width-trial-and-a-delta-wing-framing-for-aspect-ratio).[11]

## A modified Paradox with no chine runners, and a keel theory of tacking

Replying to a fellow correspondent's registry question about a photographed boat — said to be "Tom's Paradox," carrying a triangular sail and, by the asker's own reading, apparently missing its chine runners[18] — corrected the sail type from "Marconi" to **Bermudan**, the term Marconi properly denoting how a mast is wire-stayed rather than a sail's shape, and wondered how the centre of effort, which looked to have moved a long way forward, affected the chine runners' own function, and whether the boat suffered lee helm.[19] The correspondent confirmed there seemed to be no chine runners at all.[20] Wondered in turn how leeway is controlled on a hull built that way — a leeboard, perhaps, or a centreboard — and, having recently met **Richard Green**, [Minnow](/boats/enuf.md)'s new owner (see [boats/enuf.md — Richard Green's ownership](/boats/enuf.md#richard-greens-ownership-and-a-first-tacking-report-august-2015)), wondered whether the chine runners themselves function rather like a long keel, since Richard reported her reluctant to tack. Drew a further comparison to catamarans, generally reluctant to tack themselves, one of which had been made to tack by fitting a high-aspect-ratio dagger board — reasoning a dagger board would never suit a Paradox, but a high-aspect-ratio leeboard might be worth trying on "a tiny barge yacht" of this kind.[21]

The keel theory grew further once the registry thread's tacking question spun off into its own "Paradox tacking difficulties" thread. Replying to a fellow correspondent's submerged-forefoot/vortex-inhibition theory (see [chine runners — a third strand](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy) and [sharpie lineage — whether sharpie theory explains her handling](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-sharpie-theory-explains-her-handling) for the fuller exchange), worked out why the chine runner behaves like a keel at all: Paradox is flat-bottomed only at rest — under sail she is a deep-V of roughly 40° dihedral, making the immersed chine runner a long, asymmetric keel. Long-keeled boats are renowned for tracking well and for their reluctance to change tack; fin-keeled boats are known for the opposite — "we can't have it both ways." Sailed upright, agreed the runners would inhibit vortex formation around the chine much as argued; but since Paradox's own "long keel" is canted to leeward rather than vertical, reasoned its helical vortex is smaller than a true long keel's, and on that reasoning better at resisting leeway.[22]

The speculative leeboard-or-centreboard question raised above about the same photographed, chine-runner-less boat was answered, eight months later, by the correspondent who had first flagged her as missing them: research into her history found the chine runners had been removed first, and a leeboard fitted afterward — a genuine conversion rather than a hypothetical one, and, once made, irreversible.[30] The same boat resurfaced fourteen months on again, this time in her own right rather than as an illustration of the keel theory, on a thread weighing a Bermuda-rig conversion for a different Paradox entirely: her builder was named for the first time — **Tom Steinmetz** — with a video of her under sail linked and a further, unlocated video of her leeboard recalled from memory.[39]

Revisiting the same photograph the following day, alongside a companion observation that its rig showed no shrouds at all and would therefore struggle to hold luff tension in the foresail,[31] added two more technical points of his own: converting to a Bermudan rig needs shrouds and stays fitted, short of something like an aerorig; and, against the "no way back" framing above, fitting leeboards does **not** actually require removing the chine runners at all — simply adding battens gives the leeboard clearance to bear against, clear of the runners.[32]

Restated the keel theory eighteen months later in blunter terms: ballast is irrelevant to the choice between chine runners and leeboards, and Paradox sailors generally report the design tacks slowly and poorly — consistent with the chine runners acting as long keels — while leeboards are, in his view, actually better than a deep fin for tacking.[35] Pressed for evidence, deflected the challenge — "don't listen to me; I just use them" — pointing to unspecified Dutch leeboard data.[36] Returning once more, set out the fuller case: fin keels must be symmetrical and lose apparent area as a boat heels, whereas leeboards can be built asymmetric for high lift to windward and gain apparent area as the boat heels rather than losing it, aspect ratio held equal being more a matter of aerofoil (or hydrofoil) shape than of the mechanism itself; corrected "Marconi" to "Bermudan" once again — Marconi properly describes how a mast is stayed, not a sail's shape; and pointed to Slieve McGalliard's own published results as evidence against the idea that a junk rig cannot point.[37]

## Why the sail sits to port: the tack/furler arm's own geometry

Weighing in on why the sail sits to port rather than starboard, tied the choice to the tack/furler arm fitting itself: its geometry is built specifically around stowing the boom and yard along the port side deck, so moving the fitting to the boat's starboard side would change that geometry substantially rather than being a simple mirror-image swap. ## Ballast placement: dynamic feel versus static righting moment

Corrected a claim that placing ballast further outboard would help a Paradox's righting moment: it may improve roll characteristics and dynamic balance, but the righting moment itself is unaffected, since each outboard weight is balanced by an equal heeling moment on the other side. Full exchange: [design/weight-and-ballast.md — ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) and [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016).[28]

## A 2017 defense of both rigs, and encouragement for an untested design

Weighing in on both sides of a renewed junk-rig-versus-chine-runner debate in July 2017, cited Slieve McGalliard's own published results as evidence that a fully battened lug sail can perform remarkably well to windward, and gave the fullest windward verdict on record for the chine runners themselves: not as good as a deep keel, but not in doubt either — Paradox points quite well despite its unconventional sail, though with a lot of leeway since the runners simply lack the area, and tacks slowly through a wide turning circle; well suited to coastal cruising in shallow water, though poor by the standard of a modern racing dinghy. Traced a Chinese lug's per-batten sheeting lines ("sheetlets") to the historical "vang," a control that fell out of general use on small cruising yachts once paid hands became too expensive to keep it rigged, still used by the more competent Thames-barge skippers to markedly improve a spritsail's own windward performance. Closed by urging patience for Eerik Mee's own newly launched, still-untested boat: theory is a good start, but nothing trumps experiment. Full exchange: [design/ideas/junk-rig.md — a 2017 objection: is the underlying windward question even settled?](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-2017-objection-is-the-underlying-windward-question-even-settled) and [chine-runners/windward-performance.md](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#a-2017-balanced-verdict-adequate-for-coastal-cruising-no-match-for-a-modern-dinghy).[40]

## A mast-support answer, corrected against his own modification (November 2017)

Answering a newcomer's question about how the mast is supported, described the mast step as sitting an inch or two above the waterline, with the partners at deck level about three feet above it, no stays or shrouds anywhere on the rig, and the surrounding vent trunk — built into bulkhead 2 — carrying rainwater from the deck down to a drain below the step, keeping the mast itself out of sight in the cabin.[41] Corrected the figure the next day after another builder gave the stock distance as 500 mm (20 in) rather than three feet: the wrong guess had come from memory of his own modification rather than a fresh look at the original plans, giving "a very wrong impression" of the mast's bury depth and the cabin's height, and he added that the vent trunk carries several further functions besides the ones already given. Full exchange: [components/rig/mast.md — mast step height above the waterline, and the stock partners dimension](/components/rig/mast.md#mast-step-height-above-the-waterline-and-the-stock-partners-dimension).[42]

## A filler-mechanics theory: fairing versus structural fillers (February 2018)

Weighing in on what an epoxy filler actually does, reasoned the economics and mechanics through from first principles: creating space in the resin with microballoons is what makes a cured mix cheap and easy to sand, since it puts less resin into the joint, while a porous filler such as wood dust or baking flour instead soaks up more resin, making the mix thicker, less fluid, and better suited to filling a joint that needs strength rather than a smooth finish — his own long-standing technique being wood dust straight from an electric sander, with a further builder's use of baking flour noted from an outside link. Full exchange, and the firmer correction it drew (microballoons for fairing only; fibrous fillers for structural joints): [construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md — the same thread resolved](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#the-same-thread-resolved-fairing-fillers-versus-structural-fillers).[43][44][45]

## A worked critique of a trailing man-overboard rescue line (August 2018)

Liked a fellow owner's idea of a floating rescue line trailed astern for a man overboard to grab, but worked through the numbers skeptically: allowing for cold-water-shock recovery time and orientation, the boat would already be some 40 m ahead before the swimmer started swimming, meaning the line would need to run at least 100 m; grabbing a rope moving at 4 knots with wet hands, then hauling hand over hand against that flow for the same distance, was judged likely to leave the hands raw before ever reaching the boat. Proposed the grab-and-haul portion as testable on dry land — a car towing a rope at 4 knots — and did not intend to try the water version himself, but asked that anyone who does report back. Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#a-skeptical-physics-critique-of-the-trailing-rescue-line-and-a-counter-anecdote).[46]
