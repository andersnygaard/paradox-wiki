# John Starkie

**John Starkie** posted to the mailing list from September 2013, signing "John" or "John Starkie" and referencing CambridgeSchoolofNavigation.com. He cruises the Thames Estuary, the Scottish Isles, Ireland, and the South Coast, avoiding the Solent where he can since the steaming cone is locally understood there to mean "examiner on board" — and states plainly, "Not yet in a Paradox!" A rowing eight once collided, he recalls, with either Matt Layden's Enigma or a fellow builder's Paradox while at anchor, leaving him with unanswered questions about whether a shape or light was shown, whether the rowing crew kept a lookout or observed a local speed limit, and how close the anchorage lay to a channel.[1] No plans, construction start, or boat name is ever confirmed on record.

## Building versus sailing

Introducing himself while making dumb-sheaves and blocks for a boat he may never build, he argued there is no rule that a builder must sail the boat they build, or that a sailor must have built the boat they sail — some build, some sail, some do a bit of both.[2] Found nothing to argue with in a fellow correspondent's philosophy of simply having fun and learning a lot along the way.[3]

## A navigation-rules exchange: lights, day shapes, and tacking angle

A single wide-ranging reply opened three threads at once. On yuloh technique: asked whether a "real" yuloh or a plain sculling oar was actually in use, pointing to Slieve McGalliard's own yuloh page for further reading. On navigation rules: IRPCS Rule 23(d) and Rule 25 let a vessel under 7 metres and 7 knots show a single all-round white light, but the same rules also call for a black ball at anchor (Rule 30(a)) and a black cone motor-sailing (Rule 25(e)), regardless of any exemption on the light itself, linking a UK MCA lighting-and-shapes document. On tacking angle: GPS course-over-ground gives a misleading figure, since a boat tacks through the water rather than over the ground — true tacking angle is defined by the apparent wind and a compass, not a GPS.[4]

Continuing the same day, added on the rules question that a posted summary list is someone's own brief and therefore a misleading paraphrase, not the regulations themselves — the IRPCS is international and includes the US, whose own MCA- and USCG-issued documents match it; the black ball is not the steaming cone and serves a different purpose, and Dutch and Portuguese waters are reported to impound a boat found without both. Restated the tacking-angle point in reply to a follow-up question: "tacking angle" is defined by the apparent wind and a compass, and while GPS course-over-ground is useful for sailing the boat, it is not the same thing.[5]

On the yuloh question, judged that Matt Layden's careful, innovative design thinking stops the moment a sculling oar is simply bent around the curve of the gunwale instead of built to the yuloh's own design — the bend is all-important, has to be in the right place for the boat and the oarsman, and has to be sharp — crediting Bob Groves and Slieve McGalliard with having put real research and thought into the question. Having sculled a conventional oar on his own dinghy for decades, "hard, concentrated work," judged a proper yuloh "a revelation" by comparison, after being let try one aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" — though it "wasn't intuitive" at first.[6]

Closing the rules thread, confirmed again that the ball is not a steaming cone; the IRPCS exists to prevent collisions, so not flying the ball fails to do all one can to prevent one even where technically exempt on the light; he and his son have observed that hoisting a cone while motor-sailing, or a ball while anchoring, reliably prompts several nearby boats to do likewise — a sailor cannot pick and choose which rules to obey.[7]

## The mast-offset thread, and converting the ballast tanks

Weighing in on why the mast sits offset to port, suspected the geometry of the mast position, the boom, and the way the boom support swings to port is carefully calculated, so that moving anything would mean redesigning the boom support and the control-line routing through the drain into the cabin.[8]

On the recurring question of converting the built-in ballast tanks to storage, said he has always been in two minds about water ballast — the plumbing is complicated, and drinking water carried in it is lost as ballast as it is consumed, taking about 11 times the stowage volume of lead, though it does keep the trailer weight down — and prefers several small drinking-water containers (2-pint and 4-pint bottles) over one large one, so any contamination stays confined to a container or two that can simply be discarded.[9]

## Bulkhead curvature and deck-beam crown, explained geometrically

Answering a fellow builder's question on why bulkhead sides carry a slight convex curve, gave the fullest geometric account on record: plywood as thick as a bulkhead's side member can only be bent into a conical or cylindrical section, and the curve and twist of the hull sides along the boat forces a conic section onto it — leaving a bulkhead's sides straight can leave the side panel not touching it everywhere, though careful nailing and thickened epoxy in the gaps, or simple shimming, can fix it. Full exchange: [hull assembly — why the bulkhead-side curve exists](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#why-the-bulkhead-side-curve-exists-and-fixing-one-already-cut-straight).[14] Answering a further newcomer's own CAD-drawing questions the next day, corrected a common but mistaken shortcut: sharing one curve radius across every deck beam, rather than each bulkhead's own fair line, leaves the deck unfair as a beam's span narrows from one bulkhead to the next, needing beams shimmed and shaved afterward to compensate; also gave two sheet-specific figures, that bulkhead 3 takes two drilled holes for the tiller lines and the transom's centerline height on drawing 8 is 459 mm plus a 25 mm deck beam. Full exchange: [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#deck-beam-dimensions-and-the-deck-curves-true-shape).[15]

## Chine runners as a delta wing, not a foil

Weighing in on a runner-shape thread, argued a chine runner together with the hull bottom behaves like a **delta wing** — Concorde, or a crab-claw sail — rather than a conventional foil, since its lift comes from generating vortices rather than from laminar flow, and it has no true leading edge for an aspect-ratio figure to attach to. The practical consequence: the whole runner must stay submerged, with the boat heeled enough to bring its upper, planar face toward vertical — the opposite of a centreboard or daggerboard, which needs the boat kept horizontal to stay at its own most efficient. Full exchange: [chine runners — a further 2014 recurrence](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-further-2014-recurrence-a-scale-model-width-trial-and-a-delta-wing-framing-for-aspect-ratio).[11]

## A modified Paradox with no chine runners, and a keel theory of tacking

Replying to a fellow correspondent's registry question about a photographed boat — said to be "Tom's Paradox," carrying a triangular sail and, by the asker's own reading, apparently missing its chine runners[18] — corrected the sail type from "Marconi" to **Bermudan**, the term Marconi properly denoting how a mast is wire-stayed rather than a sail's shape, and wondered how the centre of effort, which looked to have moved a long way forward, affected the chine runners' own function, and whether the boat suffered lee helm.[19] The correspondent confirmed there seemed to be no chine runners at all.[20] Wondered in turn how leeway is controlled on a hull built that way — a leeboard, perhaps, or a centreboard — and, having recently met **Richard Green**, [Minnow](/boats/enuf.md)'s new owner (see [boats/enuf.md — Richard Green's ownership](/boats/enuf.md#richard-greens-ownership-and-a-first-tacking-report-august-2015)), wondered whether the chine runners themselves function rather like a long keel, since Richard reported her reluctant to tack. Drew a further comparison to catamarans, generally reluctant to tack themselves, one of which had been made to tack by fitting a high-aspect-ratio dagger board — reasoning a dagger board would never suit a Paradox, but a high-aspect-ratio leeboard might be worth trying on "a tiny barge yacht" of this kind.[21]

The keel theory grew further once the registry thread's tacking question spun off into its own "Paradox tacking difficulties" thread. Replying to a fellow correspondent's submerged-forefoot/vortex-inhibition theory (see [chine runners — a third strand](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy) and [sharpie lineage — whether sharpie theory explains her handling](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-sharpie-theory-explains-her-handling) for the fuller exchange), worked out why the chine runner behaves like a keel at all: Paradox is flat-bottomed only at rest — under sail she is a deep-V of roughly 40° dihedral, making the immersed chine runner a long, asymmetric keel. Long-keeled boats are renowned for tracking well and for their reluctance to change tack; fin-keeled boats are known for the opposite — "we can't have it both ways." Sailed upright, agreed the runners would inhibit vortex formation around the chine much as argued; but since Paradox's own "long keel" is canted to leeward rather than vertical, reasoned its helical vortex is smaller than a true long keel's, and on that reasoning better at resisting leeway.[22]

## A rope-material correction: polyester is not dacron

Corrected a fellow correspondent's assumption that ordinary polyester rope could substitute for the plans' specified dacron, and recommended asking instead for Dyneema, Spectra, or a modern non-stretch synthetic by name. Full exchange: [components/rig/running-rigging.md](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rope-materials-what-is-actually-discussed).[23]

## Budget realism: cheap electronics done right

Answering a prospective builder's $12,000 "state of the art" guess with a dose of realism, priced his own working electronics well under a tenth of that: a GPS under £100, a handheld VHF under £80, a compass that was simply a Christmas present, and an IRPCS-compliant nav light improvised from a converted garden solar lamp doubling as an anchor light. Full exchange: [construction/build-effort.md — cost](/construction/build-effort.md#cost).[17]
