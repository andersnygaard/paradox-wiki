# John Starkie

**John Starkie** is a sailor from **Norfolk, England**.[1] He has sailed for decades in boats of his own around the Thames Estuary, the Scottish Isles, Ireland and the South Coast.[2] Having sculled a conventional oar for decades, hard, concentrated work, he found a proper yuloh a revelation by comparison, though trying one aboard [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) showed the technique took some learning.[3] He bought a set of Paradox plans in 2014 but has not built one.[4] He is known for two things: navigation rules explained by the rule number rather than by a rough summary and the physics and geometry of a Paradox's hull and rig, worked out from first principles for whoever asks.

## Navigation rules

He reads the navigation rules by the rule number rather than by a rough summary.[5] IRPCS Rule 23(d) and Rule 25 let a vessel under 7 metres and 7 knots show a single all-round white light, but Rule 30(a) still calls for a black ball at anchor and Rule 25(e) a black cone motor-sailing, regardless of that exemption. The international IRPCS binds UK, US, Dutch and Portuguese waters alike. A summary passed hand to hand is someone's own brief rather than the regulations themselves. Full account: [Running lights](/operations/running-lights.md).

## Chine runners and hull geometry

He treats a chine runner, together with the hull bottom, as a delta wing generating lift from vortices rather than laminar flow: the whole runner has to stay submerged, with the boat heeled enough to bring its upper, planar face toward vertical, the opposite of a centreboard or daggerboard, which wants the hull kept horizontal to stay at its own most efficient, vertical orientation.[6] Heeled, since Paradox's flat bottom becomes a deep V of roughly 40 degrees dihedral under sail, the immersed runner instead behaves as a long, asymmetric keel, which on that reasoning explains why the design tacks slowly and tracks well rather than the reverse.[7] The same habit of working from the drawings answers questions of build geometry too. A bulkhead's sides carry a slight convex curve because plywood that thick can only be bent into a conical or cylindrical section; the hull's own curve and twist forces a conic section onto it regardless.[8] Full account: [How chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) and [Mast](/components/rig/mast.md).

## Materials, safety and practical questions

An epoxy filler's job is decided by what the joint needs: microballoons create space in the resin for a mix that is cheap and easy to sand, while a porous filler such as wood dust soaks up more resin for a joint that needs strength rather than a smooth finish.[9] Carbon monoxide from any solid or liquid fuel, including a candle under a flowerpot, kills without warning, unlike a charcoal heater's own visibly smelly exhaust. A heater's own heat can also ignite plywood and, worse, epoxy resin, hot enough to melt the glass in the mat and release toxic smoke.[10] Ballast placed further outboard can improve roll and dynamic balance without changing the righting moment itself, since each outboard weight is balanced by an equal heeling moment on the other side.[11] A guess of $12,000 for "state of the art" electronics was well above what such gear actually costs: a GPS, a handheld VHF, a compass and a navigation light together cost a small fraction of that.[12] Full account: [Epoxy brands and chemistry](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md), [Cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md), [Weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) and [Running rigging](/components/rig/running-rigging.md).
