# John Starkie

**John Starkie**, of **Norfolk, England**,[22][3] is a sailor active since **August 2013** who signs his messages "John" or "John Starkie" and, in some, adds a reference to **CambridgeSchoolofNavigation.com**,[11] a connection never explained beyond the name itself. He has sailed for decades in boats of his own around the Thames Estuary, the Scottish Isles, Ireland and the South Coast, sculling a conventional oar the whole time until he was let try a proper yuloh aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" and called it "a revelation."[1] He bought his own set of Paradox plans in 2014, and the record runs twelve years without a Paradox of his own, though not without boats: "Now I breathe clean air and build small boats," he wrote in 2020, after a wood-dust allergy cost him nothing worse than a powered respirator.[2] What he became instead is a steady authority in the record on two things, the navigation rules argued from the rule number rather than a rough summary of them, and the physics and geometry of a Paradox's own hull and rig worked out cold from first principles for whoever happened to ask. He is as ready to correct himself, in public, as he is to correct anyone else.

## The rules of the road, cited by rule number

His earliest substantial message on record, from October 2013, recalls a rowing eight once colliding, he thought, with either Matt Layden's Enigma or a fellow builder's Paradox while at anchor, a secondhand account he never claims to have witnessed himself; he was left with unanswered questions about whether a shape or light was shown, whether the rowing crew kept a lookout, and how close the anchorage lay to a channel.[4]

The same month, a single wide-ranging reply laid out the navigation-rules reasoning that became his sustained authority in the record. IRPCS Rule 23(d) and Rule 25 let a vessel under 7 metres and 7 knots show a single all-round white light, he wrote, but the same rules also call for a black ball at anchor (Rule 30(a)) and a black cone motor-sailing (Rule 25(e)), regardless of any exemption on the light itself, linking a UK MCA lighting-and-shapes document.[5] Continuing the same day, he added that a summary passed hand to hand is someone's own brief and therefore a misleading paraphrase, not the regulations themselves; the IRPCS is international and includes the US, whose own MCA- and USCG-issued documents match it, and Dutch and Portuguese waters are reported to impound a boat found without both cone and ball.[6] Closing the point, he confirmed again that the ball is not a steaming cone, and that the IRPCS exists to prevent collisions, so not flying the ball fails to do all one can to prevent one even where technically exempt on the light; he and his son had observed that hoisting a cone while motor-sailing, or a ball while anchoring, reliably prompts several nearby boats to do likewise, a sailor cannot pick and choose which rules to obey.[7]

Five years on, in October 2018, the same rigor recurred: answering a fellow owner's complaint that commercial vessels would not give way in a narrow, industrial river stretch, he quoted the governing rules directly, IRPCS Rule 9 (narrow channels) and Rule 18 (responsibilities between vessels), confirming a vessel confined to a narrow channel is in fact owed room under the collision regulations. Full account: [boats/thor.md, another day of sailing](/boats/thor.md#another-day-of-sailing-alterations-proven-and-ballast-physics-restated-october-2018).[8]

## Building, sailing and a yuloh worth the name

His first appearance on record, responding to "SaSa" photographs in early August 2013, already carries the two throughlines his record would follow for the next twelve years: he was undecided whether he would build a Paradox at all, and he raised the same water-ballast skepticism he would restate in detail four months later, the contamination risk of a single large container against the safety of several small ones.[9]

Introducing himself formally the following month, while making dumb-sheaves and blocks for a boat he may never build, he argued there is no rule that a builder must sail the boat they build, or that a sailor must have built the boat they sail; some build, some sail, some do a bit of both.[10] He found nothing to argue with in a fellow correspondent's philosophy of simply having fun and learning a lot along the way.[11]

He confirmed a year later that the plans were still on sale, having bought his own set about a month earlier by taking a chance and sending sterling cash by post; the plans arrived about a week later.[12]

## Chine runners as a delta wing, and the keel theory that followed

Weighing in on a runner-shape question in June 2014, he argued a chine runner together with the hull bottom behaves like a delta wing, generating lift from vortices rather than laminar flow, unlike a conventional foil with a true leading edge; the practical consequence is that the whole runner has to stay submerged, with the boat heeled enough to bring its upper, planar face toward vertical, the opposite of a centreboard or daggerboard, which wants the boat kept horizontal to work at its own most efficient.[13]

The theory grew further a year later, once a registry question spun off into a wider debate over Paradox's reluctance to tack. Replying to a fellow correspondent's submerged-forefoot theory, he worked out why a chine runner behaves like a keel at all: Paradox is flat-bottomed only at rest, but under sail she is a deep-V of roughly 40 degrees dihedral, making the immersed chine runner a long, asymmetric keel. Long-keeled boats track well and resist changing tack; fin-keeled boats do the opposite, "we can't have it both ways." Sailed upright, he agreed the runners would inhibit vortex formation around the chine as others had argued, but since Paradox's own "long keel" is canted to leeward rather than vertical, he reasoned its helical vortex is smaller than a true long keel's, and on that reasoning better at resisting leeway.[14]

That framework met its clearest real-world test at second hand. In August 2015 a fellow correspondent shared a photograph of a Paradox, later identified as Tom Steinmetz's, that appeared to have lost her chine runners entirely; Starkie helped work out what the boat actually was and wondered how her leeway was controlled without them, and eight months later learned she had in fact been deliberately converted, her chine runners removed and a leeboard fitted in their place.[15][16][17][18] Her builder was named two years after that first photograph; her fuller story, including the conversion itself, is at [Tom Steinmetz's Paradox](/boats/tom-steinmetzs-paradox.md).[19]

Restated eighteen months later in blunter terms: ballast is irrelevant to the choice between chine runners and leeboards, and Paradox sailors generally report the design tacks slowly and poorly, consistent with the chine runners acting as long keels, while leeboards are, in his view, actually better than a deep fin for tacking.[20] Pressed for evidence, he deflected the challenge, "don't listen to me; I just use them," pointing to unspecified Dutch leeboard data.[21] Returning once more, he set out the fuller case: fin keels must be symmetrical and lose apparent area as a boat heels, whereas leeboards can be built asymmetric for high lift to windward and gain apparent area as the boat heels rather than losing it, aspect ratio held equal being more a matter of aerofoil shape than of the mechanism itself; he pointed again to Slieve McGalliard's own published results as evidence against the idea that a junk rig cannot point.[22]

Weighing in on both sides of a renewed junk-rig-versus-chine-runner debate in July 2017, he cited Slieve McGalliard's own results as evidence that a fully battened lug sail can perform remarkably well to windward, and gave his own balanced verdict on the chine runners' windward performance: not as good as a deep keel, but not in doubt either, Paradox points quite well despite its unconventional sail, though with a lot of leeway since the runners simply lack the area, and tacks slowly through a wide turning circle, well suited to coastal cruising in shallow water though poor by the standard of a modern racing dinghy. He traced a Chinese lug's per-batten sheeting lines, "sheetlets," to the historical "vang," a control that fell out of general use on small cruising yachts once paid hands became too expensive to keep it rigged, and closed by urging patience for Eerik Mee's own newly launched, still-untested boat: theory is a good start, but nothing trumps experiment.[23]

## Hull and rig geometry, worked out from the drawings

Weighing in on why the mast sits offset to port, in December 2013, he suspected the geometry of the mast position, the boom and the way the boom support swings to port is carefully calculated, so that moving anything would mean redesigning the boom support and the control-line routing through the drain into the cabin.[24] The following November, he cautioned against raising the mast step to gain rig height, since the mast already has insufficient bury as it is.[25]

Answering a fellow builder's question in November 2014 on why bulkhead sides carry a slight convex curve, he gave a detailed geometric account: plywood as thick as a bulkhead's side member can only be bent into a conical or cylindrical section, and the curve and twist of the hull sides along the boat forces a conic section onto it; leaving a bulkhead's sides straight can leave the side panel not touching it everywhere, though careful nailing and thickened epoxy in the gaps, or simple shimming, can fix it.[26] Answering a further newcomer's own CAD-drawing questions the next day, he corrected a common but mistaken shortcut: sharing one curve radius across every deck beam, rather than each bulkhead's own fair line, leaves the deck unfair as a beam's span narrows from one bulkhead to the next, needing beams shimmed and shaved afterward to compensate; he also gave two sheet-specific figures, that bulkhead 3 takes two drilled holes for the tiller lines and the transom's centerline height on drawing 8 is 459 mm plus a 25 mm deck beam.[27] He gave a further, complementary account in 2017 for a different newcomer, this time of the bulkhead-side bevel and its lofting method.[28]

Cautioned, in May 2015, when connecting an autopilot to a rudder-mounted trim tab was floated as a self-steering option, that trim tabs and pendulum-vane gear alike seriously complicate lifting the rudder in shallow water.[29] Weighing in the following January on why the sail sits to port rather than starboard, he tied the choice to the tack/furler arm fitting itself: its geometry is built specifically around stowing the boom and yard along the port side deck, so moving the fitting to the boat's starboard side would change that geometry substantially rather than being a simple mirror-image swap.[30]

Answering a newcomer's question in November 2017 about how the mast is supported, he described the mast step as sitting an inch or two above the waterline, with the partners at deck level about three feet above it, no stays or shrouds anywhere on the rig, and the surrounding vent trunk, built into bulkhead 2, carrying rainwater from the deck down to a drain below the step.[31] He corrected the figure the next day after another builder gave the stock distance as 500 mm (20 in) rather than three feet, in the clearest example on record of his own visible self-correction: the wrong guess, he explained, had come from memory of a modification of his own rather than a fresh look at the original plans, giving "a very wrong impression" of the mast's bury depth and the cabin's height. It is the first sign on record that he made some change to the design of his own; its nature is not otherwise recorded.[32]

## Two safety claims, tested and corrected

Guessed in January 2014 that a recollection of a Paradox builder or sailor once heating his boat adequately all winter on a single candle referred instead to boat blogger Dylan Winter's separate, unrelated idea of concentrating a candle's heat under an inverted flowerpot, a proposal he recalled being discussed at length on outside boating forums, by his own account without any experiment or measurement ever settling it; whichever it was, he found the idea of a woodstove aboard so small a boat "quite frightening." Full exchange: [operations/cabin-heating.md](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-2014-recurrence-an-ammo-can-stove-idea-and-a-flue-placement-technique).[33]

Restated the danger of carbon monoxide more sharply in October 2015: charcoal, wood, kerosene, gas and even a candle under a flowerpot all release the gas, which gives no warning by smell, taste or sight, unlike a charcoal heater's own visible, smelly "evil smoke," genuinely less dangerous for announcing itself; separately warned that a heater's own heat can ignite plywood and, worse, epoxy resin, which burns more fiercely, hot enough to melt the glass in the mat and release thick, toxic black smoke. Full exchange: [operations/cabin-heating.md, a 2015 recurrence](/operations/cabin-heating.md#a-2015-recurrence-convection-physics-a-resin-fire-risk-detail-and-a-kerosene-lantern-in-occasional-use).[34]

One of the best stories on the page followed in August 2018: he liked a fellow owner's idea of a floating rescue line trailed astern for a man overboard to grab, but worked through the numbers skeptically. Allowing for cold-water-shock recovery time and orientation, the boat would already be some 40 m ahead before the swimmer started swimming, meaning the line would need to run at least 100 m; grabbing a rope moving at 4 knots with wet hands, then hauling hand over hand against that flow for the same distance, was judged likely to leave the hands raw before ever reaching the boat. He proposed the grab-and-haul portion as testable on dry land, a car towing a rope at 4 knots, and did not intend to try the water version himself, but asked that anyone who does report back. Full account: [operations/personal-safety-gear.md](/operations/personal-safety-gear.md#a-skeptical-physics-critique-of-the-trailing-rescue-line-and-a-counter-anecdote).[35]

## A rope-material correction, retracted the next day

Corrected a fellow correspondent's assumption in August 2015 that ordinary polyester rope could substitute for the plans' specified dacron, recommending Dyneema, Spectra, or a modern non-stretch synthetic by name instead.[36] He retracted the correction himself the next day, "Oops, my mistake. Dacron is a trade name for polyester: sorry about that," then found plenty of dacron rope for sale after all on both US and UK eBay.[37][38] Full exchange: [components/rig/running-rigging.md](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rope-materials-what-is-actually-discussed).

## Ballast: what moving it can and cannot fix

On the recurring question of converting the built-in ballast tanks to storage, he said he has always been in two minds about water ballast, since the plumbing is complicated and drinking water carried in it is lost as ballast as it is consumed, taking about 11 times the stowage volume of lead, though it does keep the trailer weight down; he prefers several small drinking-water containers over one large one, so any contamination stays confined to a container or two that can simply be discarded.[39]

Corrected a claim in February 2016 that placing ballast further outboard would help a Paradox's righting moment: it may improve roll characteristics and dynamic balance, he wrote, but the righting moment itself is unaffected, since each outboard weight is balanced by an equal heeling moment on the other side. Full exchange: [design/weight-and-ballast.md, ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) and [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016).[40]

Suggested two months later treating ballast placement as adjustable rather than fixed: move it, along with the motor, stores and crew, until the boat trims properly, sails well and tacks successfully; he recalled Alastair Law saying that while changing tack he moves his own "moveable ballast," himself, forward in the boat, and wondered whether re-trimming the internal ballast the same way would help. Full exchange: [design/weight-and-ballast.md, ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered).[41]

## Budget electronics, and what an epoxy filler actually does

Answering a prospective builder's $12,000 "state of the art" guess in July 2015 with a dose of realism, he priced his own working electronics well under a tenth of that: a GPS under £100, a handheld VHF under £80, a compass that was simply a Christmas present and an IRPCS-compliant nav light improvised from a converted garden solar lamp doubling as an anchor light. Full exchange: [construction/build-effort.md, cost](/construction/build-effort.md#cost).[42]

Weighing in on what an epoxy filler actually does, in February 2018, he reasoned the economics and mechanics through from first principles: creating space in the resin with microballoons is what makes a cured mix cheap and easy to sand, since it puts less resin into the joint, while a porous filler such as wood dust or baking flour instead soaks up more resin, making the mix thicker and better suited to filling a joint that needs strength rather than a smooth finish, his own long-standing technique being wood dust straight from an electric sander.[43][44] A further builder's use of baking flour was noted from an outside link. Full exchange: [construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md, the same thread resolved](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#the-same-thread-resolved-fairing-fillers-versus-structural-fillers).[45]

## Room for a newcomer

In December 2017, when a francophone builder's welcome to the community was questioned, he wrote plainly, "I can't [speak French]. My loss," and tried an online translator rather than see "the fellow drummed out."[46]

As late as July 2025, he was still helping a different newcomer with the same problem he had solved for himself in 2014: repeated e-mails to the plans distributor had gone unanswered, but sending the money got the plans sent regardless, "I asked repeatedly, and had no reply. i sent the money and he sent the plans."
