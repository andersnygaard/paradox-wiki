# Alastair Law

**Alastair Law** — "Al," of **Yeovil, England**, though Scottish by nationality[1] — is a professional CAD engineer who spent two years building the Paradox **"[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"** almost entirely single-handed, in a garage only about two feet longer than the finished hull, and launched her at Poole Harbour at Easter 2003: the first Paradox built in the United Kingdom (see [Little Jim — attested as the first Paradox built in the UK](/boats/little-jim.md#attested-as-the-first-paradox-built-in-the-uk-september-2008)). His only previous boats were an 8 ft pram dinghy, built some 25 years before, and a school canoe.[2] He has sailed Little Jim every season since, an unbroken record now into its third decade, and has written about her more exactly than most owners write about their own boats — under his own byline, at points, for the Dinghy Cruising Association *Bulletin* and for *Messing About in Boats*. He signs every message "Al, Yeovil, England," and closes many "Hoping for calm nights" — because, by his own account, he can't get to sleep if the water is all lumpy.[3] What makes the record worth reading is less its size than its temperament: an engineer's habit of testing where "sensible" actually ends, trusting the boat to bring him back when he crosses into what he calls "stupid" — and a dry, understated way of telling you about it afterward.

## Building Little Jim

The build itself was a two-year exercise in working around a genuinely tight space: the garage was only about two feet longer than the finished hull, with two feet of clearance on either side, and turning the hull over needed a second pair of hands and a set of improvised roller skates.[2] Before cutting any wood, he assembled the whole boat first as a 3D model in the CAD software he used professionally at work — time well spent, since it settled a number of construction questions before a single panel was cut; the fuller technical record of the build itself, including its costs, dimensions, and fastening details, lives on [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md#the-build).[4]

He advocates laminating a Paradox's chine logs rather than bending them from a single piece — and, on his own boat, did the opposite. A dry test-fit left the bent, unlaminated timber under enough stored energy that removing it again for gluing seemed too risky, so it went in as found, epoxy forced in around it.[5] The same honest answer came back unchanged four years later: he would laminate them, if building again.[5b]

Years after the boat was finished, a stranger on a beach struck up a conversation and took Little Jim for a boat built exactly to plan. It prompted him to sit down afterward and write out, in full, every way she actually differs from the stock design — changes made during construction, changes made from later experience, and ideas he would still like to try — a self-written accounting now folded into [Little Jim's own account of her departures from the plans](/boats/little-jim.md#a-full-accounting-of-departures-from-the-stock-plans).[6]

His own summary of the design reframes what a Paradox is made of as a "why" question rather than a "what" one: she solves a specific set of problems — comfort and safety in shallow water and poor weather, without demanding anything energetic of her crew — and a part borrowed out of that context, dropped onto a different hull, would likely make "a pig's ear" of both boats.[7] He has been equally plain about his own mistakes: a paint failure on the interior traced entirely to his own error, an epoxy-compatible primer applied in the depths of one winter, whose tin only afterward turned out to carry a minimum-application-temperature warning he'd missed.[8]

## Twenty years under sail

The arc of the record is its own argument. An early tide race, sailed not long after launch, found him thoroughly seasick and — in his own later words — with no idea what he was doing.[9] Within months he was sailing Little Jim through a genuine offshore knockdown: caught in a squall crossing the Solent from Poole to Cowes, a rogue wave laid the boat down to an estimated 80-90 degrees. He braced a hand against the window frame as she went over, and within a few seconds — before he had fully registered what was happening — she was upright and sailing again, the seat cushion still under him.[9b] He called it simply "a learning experience" — a reason not to go out in weather like that again, not a mark against the boat.[9c] The same crossing, sailed in storm-force conditions he judged afterward he might not have risked with a second person aboard,[10] brought him into Cowes just ahead of two 40 ft yachts, one of which had turned back.[10b]

Seventeen years later, presenting the boat to the Amateur Yacht Research Society, he called her simply "an old man's boat": sailing so relaxed that the most strenuous act of a whole cruise is winding the trailer winch to haul out, and everyday passages need so little attention that he "get[s] through a lot of paperbacks" while she runs "like it's on rails." Her flat, epoxy-glassed bottom means, in his own words, "I don't bother to clear the stones off the beach before I land."[16] In the same talk he put a number, from memory, on a righting-moment test run years before: a spring balance had pegged out at "well over 25 pounds," implying a moment "more than required to undo the wheel nuts on my car" — and he recalled watching a big man deliberately try to capsize the boat by hand and fail: "very, very stable."[17] He had already made the same point once, more plainly, with a photograph of himself trying and failing to tip the boat over by hand — offered, along with Matt Layden's own two-up sailing and Hugo Vihlen's 105-day *Father's Day* crossing, as evidence that a boat this size can do more than people expect.[15] An early knockdown on one of the first outings, sail fouled against the topping lift with the hatch not quite shut, left him with more confidence in the boat afterward than it shook.[13] Riding through breaking waves bow-on decades later, keeping the bow square to seas "considerably higher than the sheer," brought no water over the deck at all — "I was very impressed."[14]

Cold has never much troubled him either: through snow, overnight frost, and a night that touched -2°C, he has never once felt the need for a heater aboard, relying on body heat and a bedtime coffee instead.[12][12b] In August 2020, seventeen years into the boat's life, the rudder blade sheared cleanly through its pivot bolt in disturbed water off Lepe Spit in the Solent — an eight-mile downwind run in a Force 5 against a foul tide, on the last day of a week's cruise. No other damage followed, and the passage continued without incident. "Well, oh dear, I thought, or words to that effect."[11] Reflecting once on what pushing past ordinary caution actually costs, he put it plainly: sensible limits need testing occasionally, or sailing risks turning bland — "fortunately every time I have gone beyond sensible and into stupid my Paradox has bailed me out."[19] In the same 2020 talk, he traced a stretch of pitching that had looked violent on film back to something simpler — the helm seat sits close to the boat's own centre of rotation, so what looks dramatic from outside is barely felt from the seat, an effect he had noticed years earlier on a two-up passage across the Helford to Falmouth, sailed together with "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" as far as the two boats' 140-mile eastward cruise from that same coast.[18]

## A philosophy of leaving well alone

He was tempted, more than once while building, to improve on the plans[20] — and is glad, in hindsight, that he mostly didn't: every change he seriously considered making, he later judged, would have made the boat worse.[20b] The one deliberate exception to that discipline was tactical rather than technical: he fitted no motor at all in Little Jim's first season, specifically to force himself to learn to sail and scull properly before falling back on an engine.[21] Auditing his own build years later, he named only two changes he judges genuinely successful — a window let into the hatch, and splitting the underfloor locker lids so they open with the seat down — and set every other early "improvement" he tried down to simply not yet understanding the stock system.[22] A masthead light built to his own careful design has told a similar story in practice: over five years the tricolour has been switched on once, the all-round light used only occasionally at anchor, and a plain hand-held torch inspires more confidence than either fitted light.[23]

The same fidelity runs through how he argues about the design in the abstract. Pressed on why a change to Paradox wouldn't be an improvement, his answer was short: "If it ain't broke, why fix it? It would be easy to change Paradox, a lot harder to improve it."[24] Against the Moitessier "you could survive by kicking a can down the road" case for an aluminium-hulled Paradox, he was drier still: "Well, try a small wooden barrel. These days a plastic drinks bottle would probably survive better... I am not convinced that makes either of them a good design for a sailing craft, however."[25]

He has never chased a racing handicap for the boat, and said why in full when asked: competitive sailing runs against most of his own reasons for going sailing at all, since Paradox is "a comfortable home" rather than a means of arriving anywhere sooner — and he quoted the Water Rat's whole "there is nothing... half so much worth doing as simply messing about in boats" passage from *The Wind in the Willows* by way of explanation.[26] Judging the boat's capability against a hypothetical round-Britain attempt, he offered what may be the clearest single line anyone has put on the design: Paradox is "not really the boat for those who enjoy sailing on the edge," since most of the time she sails herself and the skipper is along for the ride.[27] An engine, in his considered view, earns its place only against a fixed schedule — "if you have a timetable you really do need a motor" — and he has made longer, unhurried passages without one "without a second thought."[28] Starting a build having never seen a real Paradox in person takes real nerve — the same position, as it happened, that he himself had been in building the very first one in the country — and his own dry gloss on that was: "Or, perhaps, an ignorant one."[29] Asked why he doesn't push harder to windward, when the waves rather than any missing keel are what actually kill a small boat's speed, he offered the same dry economy: "I try to be a gentleman and therefore resist sailing to windward."[30]

Not every attempt to out-think the design has gone his way, and he says so plainly rather than quietly fixing the record: convinced he could simplify the forward reefing fitting, he built his own version, found on a test hoist that it didn't work, and rebuilt it back to the stock design — which has worked perfectly ever since.[31] Asked more than once whether he'd ever consider an ocean crossing, his answer settles into the same shape each time: the boat could very likely do it, the open water itself he finds simply "very tedious" compared to a coastline, and there is a plainer test of the whole question closer to home — "What is the point in owning a boat like that if you don't put it on the ferry?"[32][33]

## A trailer built to launch herself

No commercial trailer on the market met his own requirements, so — with the same engineer's habit of brainstorming a problem from scratch that produced the pre-build CAD model — he designed and built one himself: it allows a genuinely single-handed launch and recovery with little physical effort, at the cost of needing a proper slipway rather than any random beach, since the core design requirement was keeping the trailer's own wheels dry throughout.[34] Launching deliberately onto a slipway left dry by the tide is a favourite habit rather than a mistake — it usually means having the whole place to himself, with no rush at all: drop the boat off the trailer, park the car, board, make coffee, read a book, and simply wait for the water to come back.[35] Four transverse rollers keep the trailer's own wheels out of the water on a slipway or a dried-out beach alike, and after thirteen years the brakes still hadn't got wet.[37]

The whole design "pays for itself" in a very direct way: he still launches and recovers the boat single-handed in about fifteen minutes, meaning a short evening sail is worth going out for at all — so the boat gets sailed more often as a direct result.[36]

## Answering the fleet

Across two decades on the mailing list, he answered questions with the same patient, exact register whether the subject was a first-timer's whole checklist of feared build steps — beam jig, panel layout, stem shaping, scarfing, epoxy mixing — or a single stuck detail years into someone else's build.[44] He could be gentle with a stranger's mistake: told of a builder who had just discovered a 6-inch shortfall in his own bottom panels, his advice was not technique but sympathy — sleep on it, and it will look less bad tomorrow.[40] He could be blunt, too, when patience wasn't the answer: told an impatient newcomer plainly that a more relaxed boat than the one being asked about might not suit him, and pointed him elsewhere.[45] Encouraging a new builder against a compressed schedule, he was realistic rather than falsely reassuring: a genuinely near-term launch date effectively answers, on its own, whether there's time left over to build a practice boat first.[46]

When [Derek Clark](/boats/enuf.md), builder of "Enuf," announced he was leaving outright, Alastair's reply drew him back with one of his best-remembered lines: "No need to leave Derek, in fact you have a duty to stay. You contain 11% of the complete build information in the known world."[38] He offered his own copy of a hard-to-find building manual to a newcomer who couldn't otherwise reach it — on the single condition that the newcomer clear it with the original author's estate first.[39] Watching two correspondents talk past each other, he defused it with a single dry distinction: one had given an opinion "with supporting reasoning," the other had not — which left everyone still free to form their own view, without turning it into a fight.[41] Handing a sailmaker the actual spars to work from, rather than paper dimensions alone, got him what he called an excellent sail — and the general lesson he passed on afterward: "if your sailmaker doesn't know why you are waving a yard and boom about find another sailmaker."[42] Bending the furling rod, in his own account, takes exactly seven words: "A strong vice, a long lever and a big hammer."[43]

He has also functioned, informally, as something like the fleet's own institutional memory. Corresponding from recollection about the UK and international ownership record, he supplied the corrections that anchor several other boats' own pages: that "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" passed from her builder to a second owner; that "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" changed hands twice more under a different name; and rounder, more hedged recollections of several boats built further afield, not all of which held up against those owners' own firsthand accounts once they were found.[47]
