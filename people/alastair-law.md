# Alastair Law

**Alastair Law** ("Al"), of **Yeovil, England**, though Scottish by nationality,[1] is a CAD engineer who built the Paradox **"[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"** almost entirely single-handed over two years, and launched her at Poole Harbour at Easter 2003, the first Paradox built in the United Kingdom (see [Little Jim, attested as the first Paradox built in the UK](/boats/little-jim.md#attested-as-the-first-paradox-built-in-the-uk-september-2008)). He has sailed her every season since, a record now in its third decade, and has published and spoken about the design in public: an owner's appraisal for the Dinghy Cruising Association *Bulletin*, a build-and-cruise website kept since 2002 and a 2020 talk to the Amateur Yacht Research Society. Full build account: [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md).

## Building Little Jim

Before cutting any wood, he assembled the whole boat first as a 3D model in the CAD software he used professionally at work, time well spent since it settled a number of construction questions before a single panel was cut.[2] His only previous boats were a school canoe and an 8 ft pram dinghy built some 25 years earlier. The garage itself was only about two feet longer than the finished hull, with two feet of clearance on either side, so turning the hull over needed a second pair of hands and a set of improvised roller skates.[3] Full technical account, including costs, dimensions and fastening details: [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#the-build).

He fitted the chine logs unlaminated, from a single piece of timber: a dry test fit left the bent wood under enough stored energy that removing it again for gluing seemed too risky, so it went in as found, epoxy forced in around it.[4] The same question, put to him again years later, drew the same honest answer: he would laminate the chine logs, building again.[5] Full account of the choice and the alternative: [construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md).

Years after the boat was finished, a stranger on a beach mistook her for a boat built exactly to the stock plans. It prompted him to sit down and write out, in full, every way she actually differs: changes made during construction, changes made from later experience and ideas he would still like to try.[6] Full accounting: [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#a-full-accounting-of-departures-from-the-stock-plans).

## Public work

About three seasons into sailing her, an owner's appraisal of the design appeared under his own byline in the Dinghy Cruising Association *Bulletin*: fifteen advantages and eleven disadvantages, offered as a plain, impartial account for anyone considering building one.[7] His own website, "Building a Paradox," documents the construction and cruising of Little Jim from the first cut in 2001 onward, and drew at least one later UK builder to the design.[8] In May 2020, seventeen years after the launch, a talk to the Amateur Yacht Research Society summarised the design and his long experience of sailing it, describing her as an old man's boat: sailing so relaxed that the most strenuous act of a cruise is winding the trailer winch, and undemanding enough on passage that he can read steadily underway while she runs on her own.[9]

## Twenty years under sail

His approach as a sailor matches his approach as a builder: methodical and willing to test where "sensible" limits actually lie. An early knockdown crossing the Solent in her first year and a rudder pivot bolt that sheared clean through off Lepe Spit in a Force 5 in 2020 both ended without further damage or injury, each told in full on the boat's own page.[10][11] He has never needed a heater aboard, even overnight below freezing, relying on body heat and a bedtime coffee instead.[12] His own summary of the habit: "fortunately every time I have gone beyond sensible and into stupid my Paradox has bailed me out."[13] Full sailing record: [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#how-she-sails).

He has also been generous with his own build knowledge: one discouraged builder was talked out of abandoning construction altogether with the reminder that he held eleven percent of the complete build knowledge then in existence.[14]

## A philosophy of leaving well alone

He was tempted, more than once while building, to improve on the stock plans, and is glad in hindsight that he mostly didn't: every change he seriously considered making, he later judged, would have made the boat worse.[15] Auditing his own build years afterward, he named only two changes he judged genuinely successful, a window let into the hatch and split underfloor locker lids that open with the seat down, and set every other early change down to simply not yet understanding the stock system.[16] The one deliberate exception was tactical rather than technical: he fitted no motor at all in Little Jim's first season, specifically to force himself to learn to sail and scull properly before falling back on an engine.[17]

The same fidelity carries into his stated view of the design in the abstract: "If it ain't broke, why fix it? It would be easy to change Paradox, a lot harder to improve it."[18] Not every attempt to out-think the plans succeeded: convinced he could simplify the forward reefing fitting, he built his own version, found on a test hoist that it didn't work and rebuilt it back to the stock design, which has worked perfectly ever since.[19] He has never chased a racing handicap for the boat, since competitive sailing runs against most of his own reasons for going sailing at all,[20] and treats an engine as a tool for a fixed schedule rather than a matter of course, having made longer passages without one and thought nothing of it.[21]
