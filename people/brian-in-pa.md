# Brian ("Brian in PA")

**Brian**, posting from the `kbgwp@...` account and signing "Brian in PA," is a prospective Paradox builder whose own boating is confined to ponds, rivers, and Curwensville Lake, near Clearfield, Pennsylvania — about 300 miles from a fellow correspondent in Syracuse, New York — inland waters needing neither electric nor gas propulsion.[1][2] He joined the mailing list in August 2013 while researching Don Elliott's build manual and the Paradox plans.[3] By mid-2015 he still had not started construction, remaining undecided among Paradox and several of Matt Layden's smaller designs.[4]

## Two small boats before Paradox

Before considering Paradox, Brian built two small boats of his own: one to David Beede's "Summer Breeze" design, and a one-sheet-plywood boat of his own devising, documented in a WoodenBoat Forum thread[5] he later deleted after criticism that it encouraged newcomers toward imperfect methods and materials.[1] Neither boat's sailing rig was ever finished — an early test sail on a too-sheltered pond proved inconclusive, with no rudder yet fitted and a leeboard left clamped in place pending trial — though both boats saw some use on the water regardless, and fitting an outboard to Paradox was floated as a fallback should sailing itself not appeal.[6] "I guess I like starting things better than finishing them," by his own admission — "a lifelong student at the school of hard knocks."[5]

## A miniature charcoal heater

After seeing a fellow builder's homemade charcoal heater in the group's photos, Brian designed and test-built a miniature version of his own, working first in sheet metal before turning to the original's copper-pipe construction — brazed rather than soldered, and judged the better material for an eventual real build despite its higher cost, for holding up with less brazing needed than his own sheet-metal version.[7][8] A CAD-drawn "Micro Charcoal Heater," 2 in diameter by 5 in high, was cut from flat sheet metal and proved in a mockup made from scrap aluminum flashing and duct tape, fuelled with vaseline-soaked cotton balls and softwood scraps — enough to take the chill off a small area, though needing constant feeding and, lacking a proper flue, likely to draw better with a longer chimney.[9][10] He judged that just having a Paradox, built or bought, gives a member a lot to offer the group.[11]

## Ordering the plans and manual

Brian e-mailed Wanda Elliott for the build manual, hearing nothing back at first and assuming she was flooded with requests once the corrected order address had been posted to the list.[3][12][13] He relayed her standard order notice to the list in full — an 80-page, 16-chapter booklet at $18 US, payable to `rite2wanda@...`, mailed to 711 Wisconsin Ave, Tomah, Wisconsin — after which his own copy arrived by e-mailed PDF within a day of ordering.[14][15] He separately e-mailed Dave Bolduc for the plans and PayPal'd him $40; Dave's own reply described them as 12 pages of detailed drawings on 11 x 17 in paper, without step-by-step instructions, for which he recommends Don Elliott's manual.[16] In an informal build-method poll around the same time, Brian was the sole scratch-build vote, reasoning that building every piece from scratch would let the cost spread out over probably years rather than needing to buy all the materials at once.[17] When a mangled e-mail address left two of his own replies unreadable, he tried a third time, spelling both addresses out in full.[18] From the manual he reported the plans' plywood quantities (1 sheet 6 mm, 3 sheets 9 mm, 4 sheets 12 mm, 2 sheets 18 mm) and bronze-ring-nail sizes and weights, noting the further call for 5-7 gallons of epoxy as "another big cost to figure on."[19] His first mailed set of plans went missing in the post; Dave resent them along with another PDF copy, and the larger printed replacement, once it finally arrived, was judged worth the wait.[20]

## Weighing which design to build

Reasoning that a boat can be used without her sails but the sails cannot be used without the boat, Brian argued for a hull-first, mast-and-sails-last build order, drawing on his own two small boats' still-unfinished rigs as precedent.[4] By 2015 he leaned toward Enigma, Elusion, or Sand Flea over Paradox on weight and ease of build, since his own sailing is confined to lake and river rather than open water — though he ultimately picked Paradox as the only one of Matt Layden's designs with actual plans available, planning to combine ideas from several designs to suit his own needs and budget.[1] He also considered Jim Michalak's "birdwatcher"-style boats as a stopgap, drawn to Paradox in the first place partly for the ability to operate the boat from inside — a feature several Michalak designs share — and raised, then dropped without elaborating, a further Matt Layden design, "Dockbox."[21] Following a fellow builder's pointer to the "Robbsboat" blog, he bookmarked it, surprised he had never come across it despite as much searching as he had done looking at Matt Layden's boats.[22]

## Testing the interior size

To gauge whether Paradox's cabin would suit him, Brian proposed a sturdier alternative to a remembered cardboard mockup: a box the size of the main interior space, built of cardboard and duct tape or a wood frame under a tarp, sized to fit a pickup-truck bed for an overnight test — reasoning that a tape measure alone gives no real feel for the space, and that his own tiny-pup-tent backpacking experience sets a low bar the cabin should clear.[23] He later ran a rougher version of the same test for real, capping his own shortbed pickup truck and measuring the covered bed, which turned out close in length and height to Paradox's main section — not as good as seeing a real boat in person, he judged, but enough to want to build the boat even more than before.[24]

## Still deciding (2015)

By July 2015 Brian still had not started building or settled on a design, sailing Curwensville Lake and the ponds and rivers near Clearfield County — "Paradox is more boat than I really need (build stronger)... the only waves here are from the other boats."[2] Around the same time he bought plans for "Ocean Explorer," a different small pocket cruiser similar in concept to a Puddle Duck Racer with a cabin but lacking Paradox's sail-from-inside feature, and asked [André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md) for further details of his "Paraduck" crossover design, "[Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md)" — side height, cabin height and length, and the controls for sail and rudder.[25] Bourbeau's reply left him keen for more detail on the steering and sail-control layout, judging that a Gorfnik-style Puddle-Duck/Paradox hybrid might suit his own inland boating areas as well as or better than a full Paradox, since his own main draw to Paradox was sailing and camping inside rather than open-water capability.[27] A few days later he reported having started drawing up a full set of Gorfnik's plans from Bourbeau's own build posts, posting his sketches to the Gorfnik photo album, and asked further about the leeboard, rudder, sail, and interior-control details still to come, as well as whether the cabin could be extended further forward for more interior room — answered on the boat's own page.[28] He kept at the drawing over the following days, guessing his own cabin height at 18 in before Bourbeau's confirmed numbers came through, then working out that 18 in sides plus the confirmed 15 in cabin gave 33 in of headroom — enough, by his own yardstick check, so long as he did not add much of a cushion — with the leeboard-hole bracing still to add and a question of his own left open about matching Paradox's ventilation; full detail lives on [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#a-second-independently-drawn-plan-set).[29][30] Reviewing his own drawing again a few days later, he wondered whether he had drawn the cabin's front and rear support posts too large, at 1½ in square rather than ¾ in square — answered directly (cabin frame supports 1½ x ¾ in, backrest support ¾ x ¾ in; full detail on [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#a-second-independently-drawn-plan-set)).[31] Days later he uploaded his own finished CAD drawing for Gorfnik's sides and airboxes, made in QCAD, to the group's Files area.[32] Following up the same day, he worked out Gorfnik's total plywood needs — two sheets of ¼ in for the sides and airboxes, a further sheet of 3/8 in (possibly ¼ in) for the bottom, and another ¼ in sheet for the decking — and noted that, unlike the Australian PDRacer variants, Gorfnik remains PD Racer-class legal and could be registered on the class website once built in 3D, floating the idea of building the bare hull first as a simple PDRacer to sail and test before adding the cabin; still did not know when, or even whether, he would start his own.[33] Days later still, holding off on posting further updated plans until fully worked out, planned an eventual shareable PDF of the complete plans, and, with the cabin top, leeboards, and rudder assembly still to design, wondered how the rudder is controlled from inside, guessing ropes as on a Paradox.[34] The same day, redrawing Gorfnik's side view to add a window, he asked whether its top starting point — level with the bottom of the 1 in top support, as he had drawn it — was correct, and worked out for himself that the plan's 1 x 1-1/4 in pieces added along the cabin's back top account for the 1/4 in plywood thickness; drafting up plans from build posts alone, he reflected, is harder than expected, and guessed others would build the design too if free plans were available — unanswered on record.[35] A week later he posted his own still-unfinished PDF plans to the group's Files area outright, mostly copy-pasted group messages with his own drawings and calculations added, hoping to draw more interest in the design; the reception drew both praise and a request to fix a builder's name credited in the file, met with a corrected edition the next day — full exchange at [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#the-unfinished-plans-posted-publicly-and-a-name-correction).[36][37][38][39] Once Bourbeau posted his own leeboard and rudder construction detail, Brian worked up his own leeboard drawing from the posted dimensions and photographs, close to the original as far as he could judge — full detail, including the airfoil-section proportions he worked out, at [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#a-second-builders-leeboard-shape-guidance).[40] On **26 August 2015**, having spent weeks drafting the plans, he made the first cut on plywood for his own copy of Gorfnik — a second, independent build of the design — deliberately using cheap, non-marine plywood as an inexpensive way to prove the plans out and generate the photographs still needed to finish them, preferring a full-specification Paradox to a marine-ply Gorfnik if spending real money either way; full build detail at [Gorfnik's own page](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md#a-second-builder-starts-his-own-gorfnik).[41] That September, still not building, he raised a further practical question about the plans themselves: though they show the steering line's gripping knots, he found no explanation of which knot to tie, offering the "eternity knot" as his own best guess; no reply is recorded in the archive.[26]
