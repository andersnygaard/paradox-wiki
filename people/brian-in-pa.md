# Brian ("Brian in PA")

**Brian**, who signs his messages "Brian in PA," is an American correspondent from Clearfield County, Pennsylvania, near Curwensville Lake, who wrote to the Paradox community from 2013 to 2020.[1][2] He bought the Paradox plans and Don Elliott's build manual within his first month, then spent two years weighing Paradox against several of Matt Layden's smaller designs, drawn most to the shared feature of sailing the boat from inside its cabin.[3][4] In August 2015 he cut the first plywood for a [Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md), the "Paraduck" design that pairs a Puddle Duck Racer hull with a Paradox-style enclosed cabin, becoming its second builder after the design's own creator; the boat was addressed by its designer as "Kinfrog."[5][6] Brian also built a working cabin heater from copper pipe, drew a 1:10 scale model of the Paradox plans for other builders and corrected a long-standing confusion in the plans' own mast drawing, all while keeping his Paradox plans in reserve for a build he still called possible as late as 2019.[7][8][9][10]

## Two small boats, and a choice among several designs

Brian bought the Paradox plans and Don Elliott's build manual within a month of first writing to the Paradox community in August 2013.[3] He had already built two small boats of his own: one to David Beede's "Summer Breeze" design, and a one-sheet-plywood design he devised himself, documented on a WoodenBoat Forum thread he later deleted after criticism that it encouraged newcomers toward imperfect methods and materials.[1][11] "I guess I like starting things better than finishing them," he wrote, unprompted, "a lifelong student at the school of hard knocks."[12]

For two years he weighed Paradox against Enigma, Elusion and Sand Flea on grounds of weight and ease of build, and against a Jim Michalak "birdwatcher"-style boat as a possible stopgap, raising and then dropping a further Layden design, "Dockbox."[4] He bookmarked the "Robbsboat" blog, surprised he had never come across it before.[13] To judge whether Paradox's cabin would actually suit him, he proposed a sturdier alternative to a remembered cardboard mockup, a box sized to fit overnight in the bed of a pickup truck, then capped his own truck bed and measured it for real: close enough in length and height to Paradox's main section to want to build the boat even more than before.[14][15] In an informal build-method poll he was the sole scratch-build vote, preferring to spread the cost of materials out over probably years rather than buy them all at once.[16] His first mailed set of plans went missing in the post; the replacement, once it finally arrived, was judged worth the wait.[17]

Boating confined to ponds, rivers and Curwensville Lake near Clearfield shaped the choice as much as anything: "Paradox is more boat than I really need (build stronger)," he wrote in July 2015, still undecided, "the only waves here are from the other boats."[2]

## Building "Kinfrog"

In July 2015 Brian took an interest in [André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)'s Gorfnik, a "Paraduck" pairing a Puddle Duck Racer hull with a Paradox-style cabin, and bought plans for a different pocket cruiser, "Ocean Explorer," while asking Bourbeau for the Gorfnik's own dimensions and controls.[18] Working from Bourbeau's own build record alone, he drew up his own complete set of Gorfnik's plans and worked out a leeboard shape from Bourbeau's own photographs and dimensions.[19][20]

On 26 August 2015 he made the first cut of plywood for his own copy, deliberately using cheap non-marine ply to prove the plans out and generate the photographs still needed to finish drafting them, becoming the design's second builder after Bourbeau himself.[5] That November, the boat was addressed by its designer as "Kinfrog."[6] The dated record of her construction runs to 5 November 2015, when she did not yet feel as warm inside as hoped; working out the difference against a stock Paradox's own foam insulation, he judged that "Matt really fit a lot of details into a small boat."[21] Full build detail lives at [Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md).

## A cabin heater, twice built

After seeing a fellow builder's homemade charcoal heater in photographs, Brian drew a CAD version of his own in 2013 and test-built a sheet-metal prototype from scrap flashing and duct tape, fuelled with vaseline-soaked cotton and softwood scraps, and judged copper pipe the better material for an eventual real build despite its higher cost.[22][23][24][25]

He returned to the idea in October 2015, as his own Gorfnik copy neared completion, weighing tea-light candles, black iron pipe and stainless bowls as materials before settling on copper pipe, drawing on his own prior experience heating with wood, coal and an unvented kerosene heater ashore, and planning a double-wall flue to keep the cabin's wood from charring.[26][27][28] In early November he built and test-fired the copper-pipe heater itself, confirming it worked, hot enough that a fireproof base and a mounted fire extinguisher were flagged as still needed.[7] Settling the heater's base, he tried a cake pan before choosing a plywood one, and thought of the boat, not expected to be in the water that year, as an "amphibian camper," recalling an old sheet-metal Army tent heater that had once thrown plenty of warmth from nothing more than burning junk mail.[21]

## Staying in touch

The Paradox plans are already mostly to scale, bulkheads at 1:5 and the hull at 1:10. He passed the point on in 2016, alongside the design's existing CAD files and his own 1:10 scale hull-cutting pattern, drawn and shared, printable across six sheets and offered for a stitch-and-glue model too with the hedge, "I didn't try this but it should work."[8][29][30]

Two years later he took up the plans' recurring mast-drawing confusion, corrected its stray drafting-error arrows and posted an annotated copy to his own Flickr page since the usual upload channel would not take it, wanting to know how the mast goes together in case he ever does build; a second round of edited drawings followed within the week.[9][31]

He remained in touch for years afterward. In 2019, six years after buying the plans, he was still, in his own words, "interested in paradox and may someday use the plans I bought."[10] In 2020 he sketched out a lighting technique he had never tried: single LED bulbs salvaged from solar yard lights, set into a small drilled hole with a dab of clear epoxy or silicone rather than housed in a fixture, throwing a surprising amount of light for so little wiring.[32]
