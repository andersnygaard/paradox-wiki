# Chris Morejohn

**Chris Morejohn** is a boatbuilder and designer from the **Florida Keys**, part of the wider circle of small-boat builders and cruisers around [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) and the [Bolducs](/people/bolduc/dave.md) since around 1991. He built a family of large, flat-bottomed liveaboard sharpies with chine runners of his own design, a separate hull from Paradox rather than a scaled-up version of it, sailed one of them across the Atlantic in 2004 and later sketched two smaller Paradox-inspired cruiser concepts on his own design blog. He is also known as the helmsman in a well-known 2001 photograph of a heavily reefed Paradox holding 6 knots in 25 knots of wind on Exuma Sound, with Layden among the crew below.[1] Full account of his own boats: [similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md).

## Sharpies at liveaboard scale

Morejohn built "Hogfish Lips," a 32 ft sharpie, and the larger "Hogfish Maximus," a roughly 38 ft hull, "basically a 38 foot version of Paradox."[2] A third, 40 ft version, "Jubilee," followed in 2005.[3] All three carry Paradox-style chine runners as their means of lateral resistance, the feature that most visibly ties the family to Layden's own boat. He is a boat builder by trade who has built a wide variety of craft in both power and sail and is also credited with designing some commercially successful, mass-produced fishing boats.[4] None of his sharpies was ever sold as plans, each hull was built once and sold outright.[4] Shown Layden's own sketches for a 23 ft hull, he judged he could frame one up in about two weeks.[5] After a straightforward weighted rudder blade proved unsuited to "Hogfish," pulling the wrong way for a balanced helm and building dangerous momentum in chop, he worked out a pivoting, line-tensioned blade with a fellow sailor, a workable alternative to Paradox's own simpler, gravity-loaded rudder.[6] Other members of the family, along with a prospective "Loose Moose 3" and a smaller "Hogfish 18," are covered in full at [similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#the-hogfish-family](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#the-hogfish-family).

## An Atlantic crossing

In May 2004 "Hogfish Maximus" crossed the Atlantic from Florida to Portugal by way of Bermuda and the Azores, with [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md) aboard and three months allowed for the passage.[2] The crossing proved the chine-runner hull at ocean-crossing scale: she never rolled or sank, and her forward daggerboard was needed only when hard on the wind, for under 48 hours of the whole passage.[7] Loaded to her waterline with a family's tools, spares and nearly a year's food, she pounded no worse than some fin-keeled boats and rolled markedly less than a Hans Christian 38 on the same points of sail, though an autopilot wore out after three days and she wandered off the wind under hand steering for the rest of the crossing.[8] Full account: [similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#chine-runners-at-ocean-scale--the-2004-atlantic-crossing](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#chine-runners-at-ocean-scale--the-2004-atlantic-crossing) and [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md#an-atlantic-crossing-aboard-hogfish-maximus).

## A collision in the Gulf Stream

Earlier in his sailing life, Morejohn struck a submerged obstruction, an oil drum, a log or a shipping container, one night at sea and sank the boat he was sailing.[9] He drifted north in the Gulf Stream for 24 hours with sharks in the water before fishermen picked him up.[9][10] It became a standing lesson that the real danger of an open-water passage is often a collision with something unseen, not the weather.

## Two Paradox-inspired sketches

Morejohn met Layden and sailed his Paradox in the Exumas around 1991.[11] He returned to the islands in 1994, cruising aboard "Hogfish Lips" with his family, and was met there by the Bolducs, who were sailing to the Bahamas in tandem with Layden; he has stayed in regular contact with Maxwell since.[5][12] On his own [Hogfish Design blog](/references/hogfish-design-website.md) he later drew two small-boat concepts from that experience: a 2016 ["16′ minimal cruiser"](/similar-boat-designs/morejohn-16-minimal-cruiser.md), his idea of the smallest cruiser that works well at sea, with a ballasted daggerboard, an exterior cockpit, a lowerable mast and a mizzen for heaving to on a sea anchor, and a 2017 ["MULLUSK 16"](/similar-boat-designs/morejohn-mullusk-16.md), his own version of Paradox "a bit longer," keeping Layden's roller-furling lug rig with a ballasted keel and deck seating.[13][11]
