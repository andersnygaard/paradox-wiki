# William H. Longyard

**William H. Longyard**, of Winston-Salem, North Carolina, is the author of ***A Speck on the Sea: Voyages in Improbable Vessels*** (McGraw-Hill), a survey of more than seventy small-boat ocean voyages running from Roman times to the present.[1] He designed and built his own microcruiser, **["Lucky Town 7"](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md)**, over roughly ten years and sailed her from North Carolina; her full design and build account is at [Lucky Town 7](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md).[2] He also knew the Swedish micro-cruiser designer Sven Yrvind personally and had worked on the steel fuselages of homebuilt aircraft, a background he later brought to bear on questions of hull material for Paradox's builders.

## A Speck on the Sea

*A Speck on the Sea* runs to 375 pages with 155 photographs, gathering stories that range from Roman times to the present and pitched, in Longyard's own words, as being of likely interest to fans of both Matt Layden's Paradox and Sven Yrvind's Bris boats.[1] He thanked Dave and Mindy Bolduc for help along the way, including photographs they lent or donated. He also offered autographed copies directly by mail for $22.[1][3] An independent account on the Bolducs' own website dates the connection further back: Longyard had visited them in person some years before the book's 2003 publication to see their boat *Little Cruiser* and had already spoken of his plans to publish it about a year before that.[4]

A compliment on the book and the loan of its photographs drew from Longyard a promise to someday tell the story of tracking down Tom McNally in a rough part of Liverpool "during a dark and stormy night."[5] That promise became the book's City of Ragusa chapter, the product of a research trip to Liverpool that drew continuing interest from Croatia, New Zealand and a UK television company, its mysteries not fully solved as of the book's writing.[6] The book sold well enough that Longyard hoped for a sequel, wanting it to become, with a second volume, "THE bible of small craft voyaging."[6] He placed Paradox in a lineage of small "capsule" voyaging boats that, in his own telling, ran back through Gerry Spiess and Serge Testa to Bill Verity's *Nonoalca*.[6] He had, in fact, hoped during his last year working on the book to include a Paradox voyage of his own, an idea he held over for a possible next edition.[7]

One reader praised its account of an amphibious jeep's circumnavigation.[8] Kjell Karlsson called it useful reading for fellow microcruisers.[9] The book's chapter on Stephen Ladd carries a sketch by fellow builder Don Elliott, reproduced with his permission.[10]

## Sven Yrvind, in person

Longyard spoke with Yrvind directly about why the Bris hulls are built to flex rather than stiffen, a composite skin meant to bounce off a floating log or a shipping container rather than crack.[11] He spoke with him again some months later while an English translation of Yrvind's new book was being arranged. Yrvind had no time to translate it himself; a friend had once attempted an earlier chapter and found it took a huge amount of time.[12] Longyard is one of two builders known to have discussed Yrvind's design philosophy with him directly, the other being [Kjell Karlsson](/people/kjell-karlsson.md), who built his own Paradox in Yrvind's own composite method. The fuller technical record of that method is at [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md), [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md) and [Sven Yrvind's book](/references/yrvind-book.md).

## Lucky Town 7, a design of his own

The production "mini cruising" boats then on the market left Longyard dissatisfied. He set out to design and build his own, wanting to show what a genuinely capable 14 ft hull could do.[2] She shares Paradox's idea of a boat sailed and managed entirely from below, worked out differently: a lifting keel in place of chine runners and a polytarp junk sail in place of a lug. Plans were later made available through Duckworks for a nominal charge. Her full design, specifications and equipment are at [Lucky Town 7](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md).

## Steel and an aircraft builder's eye

Longyard's interest in aviation predates his time with Paradox: a 1997 letter to *Messing About in Boats*, prompted by a photograph of his own kayak ketch meeting the Dymaxion car in San Francisco, traces the lineage of the Starling Burgess and John Dunne flying-wing designs.[13] He had worked on homebuilt aircraft with chrome-moly 4130 steel fuselages, the alloy and welding practice that popularized steel over wood in aircraft construction between the World Wars. He brought that background to Paradox's builders in a detailed account of the gauge, framing and finish a steel hull would need.[14] The technical case is carried in full, de-attributed, at [Aluminum and steel Paradox](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md). His own view of the choice between hull materials was that it came down to a matter of taste, not of fact.[15]
