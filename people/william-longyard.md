# William H. Longyard

**William H. Longyard**, of Winston-Salem, North Carolina, designed and built his own small boat, **["Lucky Town 7,"](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md)** over roughly ten years, and is the author of ***A Speck on the Sea: Voyages in Improbable Vessels*** (McGraw-Hill), a survey of more than seventy small-boat ocean voyages running from Roman times to the present. Active from 2002 to 2004, he corresponded personally with Swedish micro-cruiser designer **Sven Yrvind** about Yrvind's building philosophy,[1][2] a firsthand link to Yrvind's work shared elsewhere by [Kjell Karlsson](/people/kjell-karlsson.md), who built his own Paradox in Yrvind's own composite method. He became a frequent technical correspondent on questions of hull materials, rigging and self-steering, and anchored the fullest debate on record over building a Paradox in steel, a subject his own hands-on work on homebuilt aircraft fuselages gave him unusual standing to judge.

## Sven Yrvind, in person

Longyard's Yrvind correspondence was not secondhand reading. He recalled speaking with Yrvind personally about why the Bris hulls are built to flex rather than stiffen, a composite skin meant to bounce off a floating log or a shipping container rather than crack, and later spoke to him again while trying to arrange an English translation of his new book. He is one of two people in this record who corresponded with Yrvind directly about his building philosophy: the other, [Kjell Karlsson](/people/kjell-karlsson.md), built his own Paradox in Yrvind's own composite method. The fuller technical record lives at [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md), [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md), [Sven Yrvind's voyages and boats](/people/yrvind/voyages-and-boats.md) and [Sven Yrvind's book](/references/yrvind-book.md): the Divinycell/flexible-resin sandwich, Yrvind's own integrated rudder-transom system and the long volunteer effort led by Karlsson to translate his Swedish book into English.

## "A Speck on the Sea"

Longyard's book, ***A Speck on the Sea: Voyages in Improbable Vessels*** (McGraw-Hill, 375 pages, 155 photographs), gathers more than seventy small-boat ocean-crossing stories running from Roman times to the present, written for readers of small craft rather than the yachting or naval-history audience most such books target, and pitched as being of likely interest to fans of both Matt Layden's Paradox and Sven Yrvind's Bris boats.[3] He credited Dave and Mindy Bolduc's help along the way, including photographs they lent or donated, and offered signed copies directly by mail for $22, occasionally at a discount.[3][4] An independent account from the Bolducs' own website dates the relationship further back: Longyard had visited them in person "several years" before 2003 to see their boat Little Cruiser, and had told them of his plans to publish the book about a year before that.[5]

David Beard's compliment on the book, and the loan of his photographs, drew a promise from Longyard to someday tell the story of tracking down Tom McNally in a rough part of Liverpool "during a dark and stormy night."[6] That promise became the City of Ragusa story, a research trip that took him to Liverpool and drew continuing interest from Croatia, New Zealand and a UK television company, its mysteries not fully solved as of the book's writing. By the time he told it the book was selling well enough that he wanted readers' suggestions for a next edition, hoping it might become, with its predecessor, "THE bible of small craft voyaging." He placed Paradox in the same lineage of small "capsule" voyaging boats that ran, in his telling, back through Gerry Spiess and Serge Testa to Bill Verity's Nonoalca.[7] He had, in fact, secretly hoped during his last year working on the first book to include a Paradox voyage of his own, holding the idea over instead for a possible next edition.[8] The hope for a sequel had been with him from early on: writing to thank Jack Gardiner for a mention of the book, he said interest from small-cruiser fans might let him write another, since there were not enough books about "the kind of boats we like."[9] He agreed a Swedish microcruiser builder known online as "Sebastian," an acolyte of Sven Yrvind, deserved fuller coverage than the brief mention given him in the first edition.[32]

Two readers wrote back with praise. One recommended it as an enjoyable read, citing the amphibious-jeep circumnavigation story among its highlights.[10] So did [Kjell Karlsson](/people/kjell-karlsson.md), who called it "fantastic for us microcruisers in spe."[11]

Away from the book itself, Longyard put himself on record defending [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md) during a dispute over banning him, calling him "a remarkably talented man" and urging a general rule against personal name-calling rather than singling Elliott out, while warning he would not stay himself if the bickering became the norm. Elliott, for his part, had generously let Longyard reproduce one of his own sketches, illustrating the Stephen Ladd story, inside *A Speck on the Sea*.[12]

## "Lucky Town 7," a boat of his own

Longyard designed and built his own small boat, [**"Lucky Town 7"**](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md), over roughly ten years, dissatisfied with the production "mini cruising" boats on the market and setting out to show what a genuinely capable 14 ft hull could do. She shares [Paradox](/paradox.md)'s central idea of a boat sailed and managed from below, worked out with a lifting keel in place of chine runners and a polytarp junk sail in place of a lug. Her full design, specifications and equipment are recorded at [Lucky Town 7](/similar-boat-designs/lucky-town-7.md).[13]

## The steel and aluminium debate

Longyard did more than anyone to carry the debate over whether a Paradox could be built in steel rather than plywood, arguing the whole hull, not just the bottom, could be built that way, and specifying gauge, framing and finish in detail across dozens of messages. His standing to judge the question was not purely theoretical: he had personally worked on homebuilt aircraft with chrome-moly 4130 steel fuselages, the same alloy and welding practice that popularized steel over wood in aircraft construction between the World Wars.[14] After days of argument over gauges, weights and precedents, his own closing word on the whole exchange was that it came down to a matter of taste, not of fact.[15] The technical substance, gauge by gauge and precedent by precedent, is at [Aluminum and steel Paradox](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md), which already carries nearly all of it de-attributed.

## Practical answers across the build

Longyard was a reliable technical correspondent well beyond the steel debate. He worked out a generic method for running an outboard off an external fuel tank, feeding a 12V electric pump and pressure regulator ahead of the carburetor, now part of the record on [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md).[16] Fifteen years of using Hammerite on freshly cast lead ballast convinced him it was "bulletproof," even though it was hard to source in the US at the time, a fact folded into [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md#casting-lead-ballast-into-the-runners).[17] He pointed builders to a high-volume spray-in foam supplier once canned foams proved unsatisfactory, on [plywood grade and foam flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md).[18] He worked out converting the Hull program's offsets into AutoCAD for panel nesting, at [panel layout and nesting](/construction/panel-layout.md).[19][20] An old boat's irrigation tubing, used at 3 in diameter, gave him a cheap, light mast, a method recorded at [mast](/components/rig/mast.md).[21] He favored electric auto-pilot over a wind vane for cost and simplicity, and worked out a Bowden-cable trim tab that preserved Paradox's folding rudder, both on [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md).[22][23] He judged a keel-less, dory-type hull resists tripping sideways down a wave face, pointing to the 1966 Atlantic row of the keel-less English Rose as precedent, on [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md).[24][25] And he worked out a flush anchor well and a drained mast-trunk arrangement recorded at [ground tackle and anchoring](/operations/ground-tackle.md).[26][27] He also steered a vague bibliography request toward specific, findable titles: three books by the English amateur boatbuilder Percy Blandford, out of print but findable secondhand, went into the build-manual bibliography compiled with [David C. Smith](/people/david-c-smith.md).[28]

## Small boats, before and since

A 1997 letter to *Messing About in Boats* reveals an earlier boat: prompted by a photo of his kayak ketch meeting the Dymaxion car on its arrival in San Francisco, he corrected a magazine claim about the aviation history of the Starling Burgess/John Dunne flying wing, a small early echo of the aircraft background he would later bring to the steel debate.[29] He was also a subscriber to Watercraft and Practical Boat Owner, judging them closer to his own boating and budget than the larger American glossies.[30] His interest in the design outlasted his most active years by well over a decade: as of November 2020 he was still following builders' sailmaking practice, wanting to know what weight of cloth others were using and for design and cutting tips.[31]
