# Oogway

**Oogway** is a [Paradox](/paradox.md) built by **[Michael Beebe](/people/michael-beebe.md)** in **Rockport, Texas** — his second, built to the stock cabin plan rather than the widened cabin and lengthened tiller of his first Paradox, a change made after judging the widening had not paid off as hoped.[1] Beebe sold her in January 2017 to fellow correspondent **[OkieBob](/people/okiebob.md)**, who towed her home to Oklahoma and named her "Oogway" after the wise old tortoise of the *Kung Fu Panda* films.[3][4][5]

## Built by Michael Beebe, and a 2015 dockside look

Beyond the stock cabin, a 2015 dockside visit at the Port Aransas PlyWooden Boat Festival catalogued several further departures from the plans: hatches cut into bulkheads #2 and #4, the bulkhead-#4 hatch doubling as the cockpit seat; the portside shelf widened forward to meet the vent trunk, making it triangular rather than rectangular; a tiller run above the aft deck; a metal mast, later confirmed as a length of round aluminium RV-awning pole;[2][8] a simplified tack-strop/spooling arrangement; an all-locker subfloor in place of water ballast tanks; seat cushions arranged to convert between a recliner and a bed; and a leather-look chafe guard for the boom and sail improvised from an ordinary boot fitted over the mast.[2]

## Sold to OkieBob, and a name from Kung Fu Panda (January 2017)

Beebe agreed to sell the boat in mid-January 2017 to OkieBob — the same correspondent who had toured her at the 2015 festival above, and who had tried, unsuccessfully, to buy Beebe's first Paradox years earlier.[3] A few days later OkieBob drove a 1,200-mile round trip to fetch her, reporting she "towed like a dream"; a slower look at her afterward found "stout construction" and a workboat finish he judged himself free to treat roughly or repaint without gelcoat's usual fussiness. Oklahoma's tag office had warned it could take up to four months to assign a homemade boat a hull identification number, but a state trooper issued the HIN the very next morning.[4] Days after that, prompted by his daughter playing a Disney game, he settled on a name: "**Oogway**," from the *Kung Fu Panda* films.[5]

## A first exploration, and the cabin's true length (January 2017)

A week after bringing her home, OkieBob crawled the length of the boat for the first time, including the tight forward compartment between bulkheads #1 and #2, worked headfirst on his side by flashlight; the effort cost him a wrenched back, an old trouble rather than a new one. Settling afterward flat on his back to rest, he found the cabin's length ran longer than expected against his own 5'9" frame — a discovery that surprised him, since the vent box splits the cabin floor and forces a sailor to keep both feet to one side to stretch out fully.[6]

## A first sail, and a memorable retrieval (May 2017)

Four months after bringing her home, OkieBob sailed Oogway for the first time. She trailered easily, and stepping the mast and rigging her was already a practiced, quick routine; with no yuloh mount fitted yet, an ordinary oar paddled her the roughly 35 yards from the trailer to a dock, made trickier than planned by a busy ramp full of powerboats coming and going. Clearing the dock downwind and rounding up onto a starboard tack went smoothly enough to surprise him, and a beam reach out past a marina, skirting a regatta already under way, built his confidence further; catching a gust in open water and watching how well she carried herself, he judged "damn Matt really designed a great little boat and Mike nailed the build" — crediting the boat's balance to Beebe's own care in setting the halyard-to-yard attachment point, exactly three feet (92 cm) along the yard and adjustable forward or aft to tune weather helm, and her balanced rudder as "balls on accurate."[7][11]

The first real tack failed outright — a fall-off and jibe instead, with three or four more failed tacks that day against several that went cleanly, no pattern he could identify. The return leg upwind proved the harder test: unable to point high enough to clear a leeward marina, a full 180 and a slow, sail-reduced approach were needed to bring her alongside the dock without overshooting in the gusty, shifting wind off the land. Retrieving her then turned into its own small drama — walking her by bow and stern lines across a jagged-bottomed inlet toward the trailer, only for a wind shift to push her onto a rocky jetty, then nearly miss the trailer's own guide posts as she slid home, wading in waist-deep water to catch her. A crowd gathered onshore for a fishing tournament, entertained all along by the odd sight of a motorless, gearless boat and her equally odd-looking skipper, gave a standing ovation as she was finally winched up — not a speck of paint lost the whole day. One conclusion came out of it firmly: a yuloh mount is overdue.[7]

## A second sail: windward performance, an improvised nav-light, and a confirmed mast material (June 2017)

A few weeks after that first sail, raising Oogway's sail proved no trouble at all, but the furling line was incredibly difficult to pull in and visibly wearing quickly — the account, and the fixes it drew from other builders, is told in full at [components/rig/furling.md — a 2017 recurrence, and three further independent fixes](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2017-recurrence-and-three-further-independent-fixes). In the course of that account, the mast was confirmed as a length of round, strong, light **aluminium RV-awning pole** — the same material Beebe used building her — with the mast's rotation, once set correctly on stepping it, keeping the halyard from twisting and clear of the furling line.[8]

A second sail out of the usual lake, Lake Thunderbird in Norman, Oklahoma, brought an unusual cold front through, turning the customary southerly wind northeast instead; launching from a different ramp with a dock much closer at hand let Oogway be shuttled to and from the trailer on 30 ft ropes — a yuloh mount was still not fitted. Windward performance was markedly better than the first outing: initially impressed by how high she pointed that first time, later realizing her actual path had run mostly sideways rather than forward and missing marks that should have been made, much like a conventional boat sailed with the centerboard up. This time she made real windward progress at intervals, sometimes seeming stuck like being in irons while still gaining to windward, with the yuloh brought in to turn her when she would not bear away on her own; the boat needs to heel for the chine runners to bite properly (see [chine-runners/windward-performance.md — pointing angles owners report](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#pointing-angles-owners-report)), and simply adding sail to heel her further helped regardless, "like they need more power to go up hill."[9]

Without navigation lights yet fitted, two inflatable solar lanterns tied together and lashed to the yard, one on each side of the sail, lit the white sail itself from within like a lantern or a projection screen — judged a nav-light substitute clearly visible from a distance; the same lanterns double as cabin lights, red ones preferred at night since they don't spoil night vision. A planned overnight at anchor was abandoned once storms were forecast after midnight and the lake emptied of every other sailboat but the powerboats drawn by a fishing tournament; with his phone dead and no way to check weather radar, the boat was hauled out for the night rather than risk the electrical storms visible to the north and west. Still admiring Beebe's own workmanship, the mahogany shelving boards fitted in the cabin were singled out for making the boat smell cozy — "I really lucked out on acquiring this boat."[9]

## A first overnight at anchor (July 2017)

OkieBob's first overnight aboard Oogway came on a light-to-calm night, furling the sail down to about two feet and flying that remnant as a riding sail to cut down on clocking around the anchor — the boat made a good little camper.[10] Beebe had built the boat's anchor-rode lead as a PVC chock, in the style of Glen Maxwell's own bow fairlead (see [ground tackle and anchoring — Glen Maxwell's bow-fairlead construction](/operations/ground-tackle.md#glen-maxwells-bow-fairlead-construction)); using a lead of this kind for the first time, OkieBob found the anchor line could be released from the chock, and the anchor set, from a standing position inside the cabin, whipping the line just right in three tries of about five seconds each.[10] In place of water ballast tanks, Beebe had built storage into the floor: a slot roughly 3 in x 3 in x 12 in aft of the opening on the port side of bulkhead #2, its original purpose forgotten, which OkieBob found comfortable for dropping his toes into while sleeping on his stomach, and useful under sail for stowing excess halyard line out of the way.[10] The tiller, run above the aft deck, is worked either by reaching through the uncovered rear window or by reaching over it; a bug screen made for that opening the night before proved its worth against thick bugs in the still air.[10] On a Saturday night on a busy lake, powerboats and their wakes ran through the small hours, and the boat's mass was credited with coping with the wakes both at anchor and under sail — the anchoring and handling growing more comfortable each outing, including keeping her heeled over.[10]

## Possible later ownership

Since Beebe is on record building only two Paradoxes, this sale likely makes OkieBob the missing intermediate owner between Beebe and **[Tim McKinney](/people/tim-mckinney.md)**, who separately reported acquiring "a Paradox built by someone else" in the fall of 2018 — a connection the archive itself never states outright, and "Oogway" is not a name that resurfaces in McKinney's own account.
