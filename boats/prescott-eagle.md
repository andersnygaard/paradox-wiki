# Prescott Eagle

**"Prescott Eagle"** is a Paradox begun by **Brian Prescott** in **Kincardine, Ontario, Canada**, and taken up and finished by **[Cory Sellars](/people/cory-sellars.md)** after Prescott's death, toward a planned touring trip on the **Great Lakes'** **Bruce Peninsula**; an August 2019 maiden voyage was the original goal, and by January 2021 the build stood roughly half complete.[1][2]

## A hull rescued from a burn pile

By the time Prescott died, the hull was complete but for epoxy sheathing and some cut-outs, and the interior had only just been started — not Prescott's first attempt at building a boat. Sellars rescued the hull from a burn pile and towed her home on a trailer in a snowstorm. Taking his own measurements afterward, he found she tracked very closely to the plans, with **screws used in place of nails** for the sides and bottom.[1]

Sellars, a carpenter with a CNC machine and a 2 ft x 3 ft duplicator among his own tools, named the boat "Prescott Eagle" for her original builder and set a goal of an August maiden voyage, sailing out of **Kincardine, Ontario**, on the Great Lakes for the time being.[1] By October 2019 the build stood about two-thirds complete.[3] By February 2020 it had reached the stage of having a sail made; local sail lofts proved hesitant about the design's roller-reefing lug rig specifically, leaving a Sailrite quote in hand as the fallback rather than risk an inexperienced loft on a boat meant, in her owner's words, for "fairly epic journeys."[4][5] By January 2021 Sellars described her as roughly half complete, still bound for the **Bruce Peninsula** touring trip that had been her purpose from the outset.[2]

## A first design question: snub nose versus sharp bow

Opening his own list of build questions, Sellars asked whether anyone had built a sharp bow in place of the plans' snub nose, and what effect it had on performance. The reply from an established owner: no difference either way, and a sharp bow may simply be more easily damaged — the boat spends most of her time heeled well over in any wind regardless of bow shape.[6]

Two further owners agreed without having tested the alternative themselves. One doubted a sharp bow would change speed much either, but flagged a practical cost: sailing with a yuloh means no reverse gear or brake, so the bow doubles as an impromptu bumper against a dock — some owners bond rubber matting to it to cushion the contact — and a sharp bow would be more vulnerable to that kind of routine bump than the stock blunt one; guessed the blunt bow's origin might lie in keeping the design under the [14 ft threshold for US registration](/design/hull-scantlings.md#published-design-particulars) rather than any speed consideration, though testing that simply showed no difference seemed just as likely.[7] A second owner did not know the reason for the bow's shape either, but had never considered altering it, finding the boat's handling as built fully satisfactory.[8]

The wave-handling and momentum question this drew is covered in full at [pointing and tacking-angle expectations — a 2019 exchange](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md#a-2019-exchange-sharp-bow-versus-snub-nose-and-momentum-through-chop), and a further owner's own becalmed-and-towed account at [Thor — a becalmed tow](/boats/thor.md#a-becalmed-tow-and-further-wave-handling-technique-february-2019).
