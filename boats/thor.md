# Thor

**"Thor"** is [Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md)'s Paradox, built in **South Australia** through 2014 and 2015 with a boat builder's professional help and launched on the **Murray River** in June 2015.

## A birdsmouth mast, built with professional help

By January 2015 the hull was about half finished. A boat builder helped fashion a **birdsmouth mast** — a hollow spar of eight hoop-pine staves tapered to the plans but circular in cross-section, rather than the plans' square box section — coming in at about **7 kg**, very light and very strong though expensive: roughly ten hours' labor to the glued-up stage, then a further full day spent planing and sanding it round, "a thing of beauty, but at a price."[1] The build was confirmed under way "in OZ" later that month.[2]

## Build progress and launch

By April 2015, working **5 hours a day, 5 days a week**, the hull was **7 months in** and almost ready to paint, with about **8 months** total guessed to see her in the water — a genuinely complicated build, judged by comparison with two earlier, simpler small-boat builds (one open boat, one Stevenson's Pocket Cruiser), that "any boat this good" takes time to finish.[3] Days later, in an unrelated autohelm-mounting thread, a photograph of the boat as she then stood was shared under the name **"Thor."**[4]

She was launched and tested on the **Murray River, South Australia**, in June 2015, with the ballast set to the designed waterline and sailing to follow once it was bolted down for good.[5] A fellow prospective builder in Christchurch, New Zealand, replying, asked where she would be sailed — the Murray Delta? — recalling having looked at a Bolger AS-29 in the same area some eighteen months earlier and judging it a nice cruising area.[6] Alastair Law's reply: "Congratulations! Now the fun begins."[7]

Answering a newcomer's question about hours to build, weeks after the launch, Nick Bell placed his own roughly eight-month full-time build against an accident that struck around the six-month mark; judged a faster build possible for someone with the tools and trade skills, and encouraged the newcomer to start right away and do something toward the boat every day.[14]

Before sailing could begin, the yuloh had to be learned — comfortable going backwards from the start, but not yet forward.[8] Coaching from a fellow owner distilled the technique into a four-action mantra adopted from then on — twist, push, twist, pull; the fuller technique lives at [sculling and yuloh technique — a four-action teaching method](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#a-four-action-yuloh-teaching-method-and-where-to-practice).[9] Lead ballast bricks went in next, long and thin, set against the inside of the chine on each side, as far out and down as balance allowed and clear of the storage areas; the sail was rigged the following day, each rope end sewn over to stop fraying.[10] The planned cruising ground was the **Yorke Peninsula**, South Australia, working toward a passage around **Wardang Island**, 12 km offshore — with the **Sir Joseph Banks** group and the **Spencer Gulf** shore, a couple of years off, judged "ideal Paradox country."[8][9]

## Materials cost, and a modified jet-ski trailer

Total build materials came to roughly **A$4,000** — A$2,000 for wood and the same again for epoxy — spent throughout on marine plywood, West System epoxy, and marine-grade fittings, wanting the boat "thorough, tough and seaworthy."[11] The bottom got **two thick layers of heavy double-bias fibreglass cloth**, laid with the boat builder's help, making it "extremely strong and very flat" — corroborating, from the build itself, the "20 mm of marine plywood under coat after coat of resin in double-bias heavy fibreglass cloth" [Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md) later described from the finished boat. The same boat builder who fashioned the birdsmouth mast (see above) helped throughout with reading the plans.[13]

Towing was solved with a **modified jet-ski trailer**, bought for A$800 and modified locally for a few more dollars: the boat sits level under tow and handles well behind the car, and the rear-most trailer beam is removable to allow launching in shallow water.[12][13]

## Post-launch rigging refinements (August 2015)

Two months after launch, with a shoulder injury still healing, "Thor" carried a deliberately temporary, doubled "crane jib" halyard purchase: from a snap shackle down to a double block on the yard, up to a single block at the masthead, back down to the yard, then up through the mast-top block again and down to the cabin — needing a lot of rope, most of it left occupying the cabin floor. The plan was to redesign it closer to the stock arrangement once the shoulder had healed and the sail's own trim was better understood, since "keeping to the plan is paramount" — it keeps discussion of typical problems and fixes relevant to other builders, and adds value to a well-built boat besides. In the meantime the mast-box pulley and sheave were both upgraded to the biggest sizes that could be found, since small pulleys drag on the rope and add real effort to raising the sail.[15]

The birdsmouth mast (see [above](#a-birdsmouth-mast-built-with-professional-help)) came up again in the same exchange: built round in cross-section for one simple reason — "because I could," having always wanted to make one.[15] See [components/rig/running-rigging.md](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#a-crowded-masthead-and-rigging-friction-lessons-2015) for the general masthead-layout and rigging-friction lessons this exchange prompted, applicable beyond this one boat.
