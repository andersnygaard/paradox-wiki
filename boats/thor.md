# Thor

**"Thor"** is [Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md)'s Paradox, built in **South Australia** through 2014 and 2015 with a boat builder's professional help and launched on the **Murray River** in June 2015. In 2022 she was sold across the country to **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)** of Newcastle, New South Wales, who renamed her **"Pamela B"** — the name she still carries, and the one under which the second half of her life is recorded (see [below](#sold-across-the-country-and-renamed-pamela-b-2022)).[1]

## Why she is named "Thor"

Not the Norse god of thunder — "I don't think my 13 foot long creation will worry him" — but the Norwegian explorer **Thor Heyerdahl**, whose countrymen pronounce the name "Tor." *Kon-Tiki*, *Ra*, and especially *Fatu Hiva* were the inspiration, in particular a passage recounting a night spent bailing an old, repaired whaleboat to stay afloat while island-hopping — a story that stuck for what it showed about a native raft outlasting a modern boat in bad seas.[2]

## A birdsmouth mast, built with professional help

By January 2015 the hull was about half finished. A boat builder helped fashion a **birdsmouth mast** — a hollow spar of eight hoop-pine staves tapered to the plans but circular in cross-section, rather than the plans' square box section — coming in at about **7 kg**, very light and very strong though expensive: roughly ten hours' labor to the glued-up stage, then a further full day spent planing and sanding it round, "a thing of beauty, but at a price."[3] The build was confirmed under way "in OZ" later that month.[4] Answering a cost question six years later, the reason for going to that trouble was personal: a mast made deliberately light "for my ageing old muscles to lift easily."[5] A shipwright's advice settled a different fitting: the plans' masthead lightning-rod and grounding plate were left off entirely, judged unnecessary outside a lightning-prone area — advice given directly in December 2023, when a Thai builder asked how the system was normally fitted; full exchange at [design/lightning-protection.md](/design/lightning-protection.md#skipping-the-system-entirely-and-a-jury-rigged-grounding-chain).[6]

## Build progress and launch

By April 2015, working **5 hours a day, 5 days a week**, the hull was **7 months in** and almost ready to paint, with about **8 months** total guessed to see her in the water — a genuinely complicated build, judged by comparison with two earlier, simpler small-boat builds (one open boat, one Stevenson's Pocket Cruiser), that "any boat this good" takes time to finish.[7] Days later, in an unrelated autohelm-mounting thread, a photograph of the boat as she then stood was shared under the name **"Thor."**[8]

She was launched and tested on the **Murray River, South Australia**, in June 2015, with the ballast set to the designed waterline and sailing to follow once it was bolted down for good.[9] A fellow prospective builder in Christchurch, New Zealand, replying, asked where she would be sailed — the Murray Delta? — recalling having looked at a Bolger AS-29 in the same area some eighteen months earlier and judging it a nice cruising area.[10] Alastair Law's reply: "Congratulations! Now the fun begins."[11]

Answering a newcomer's question about hours to build, weeks after the launch, Nick Bell placed his own roughly eight-month full-time build against an accident that struck around the six-month mark; judged a faster build possible for someone with the tools and trade skills, and encouraged the newcomer to start right away and do something toward the boat every day.[12]

Before sailing could begin, the yuloh had to be learned — comfortable going backwards from the start, but not yet forward.[13] Coaching from a fellow owner distilled the technique into a four-action mantra adopted from then on — twist, push, twist, pull; the fuller technique lives at [sculling and yuloh technique — a four-action teaching method](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#a-four-action-yuloh-teaching-method-and-where-to-practice).[14] Lead ballast bricks went in next, long and thin, set against the inside of the chine on each side, as far out and down as balance allowed and clear of the storage areas; the sail was rigged the following day, each rope end sewn over to stop fraying.[15] The planned cruising ground was the **Yorke Peninsula**, South Australia, working toward a passage around **Wardang Island**, 12 km offshore — with the **Sir Joseph Banks** group and the **Spencer Gulf** shore, a couple of years off, judged "ideal Paradox country."[13][14]

## Materials cost, and a modified jet-ski trailer

Total build materials came to roughly **A$4,000** — A$2,000 for wood and the same again for epoxy — spent throughout on marine plywood, West System epoxy, and marine-grade fittings, wanting the boat "thorough, tough and seaworthy."[16] The bottom got **two thick layers of heavy double-bias fibreglass cloth**, laid with the boat builder's help, making it "extremely strong and very flat" — corroborating, from the build itself, the "20 mm of marine plywood under coat after coat of resin in double-bias heavy fibreglass cloth" [Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md) later described from the finished boat. The same boat builder who fashioned the birdsmouth mast (see above) helped throughout with reading the plans.[17]

Towing was solved with a **modified jet-ski trailer**, bought for A$800 and modified locally for a few more dollars: the boat sits level under tow and handles well behind the car, and the rear-most trailer beam is removable to allow launching in shallow water.[18][17]

Looking back four years on, the thickest plywood was the single hardest material to work with — bending the bottom into shape was hard going, and if she were built again it would be as two thinner layers laminated together rather than the plans' single thick sheet; the chine logs, in stiff Oregon, were also hard to bend and were pre-bent before fitting. The plans' lightning-grounding rod and copper plate were left out as unneeded in Australian waters; otherwise she is a standard hull, built to plan.[19]

A further account the same week put a number on the bottom — 19 mm — and credited the boat builder's help for laying the double-bias cloth cleanly, calling it a "peculiar" material that wants an experienced hand or good advice to get flat and free of bubbles. The lay-up has proved itself since: she has run aground slowly and struck things more than once with no damage beyond a mark in the paint, and — sitting on a trailer rather than a mooring between sails — takes more wear from road towing than she ever has from the sea. A handful of fit-out choices have stuck: white paint throughout rather than a resin-coloured interior, specifically so a leak or a run of pipework shows up at a glance; an overhead hook to tip and hold the hull while sealing the bottom and lowering the lead ballast into the bilges; a custom-welded plastic water tank from a maker of off-road-vehicle tanks, strong and removable if awkwardly so, kept algae-free by running no clear pipe into the light and flushing it every trip; and, after a bosun's chair proved too hard and was scrapped, a webbing seat and a reinforced kids' bean bag doing double duty as both a seat under way and bedding at night.[20] The bean bag is sat on the low side of the hull rather than at the fitted seat, comfortable to sit almost across the cabin at this owner's height and avoiding the soreness a hard seat gives over time; the fitted seat itself, made more bearable with upholstery straps stitched across it, still sees only occasional use as a result, and the bean bag's own foam beads are kept double-bagged, since a torn outer bag would otherwise spill them.[21]

A further update came in September 2021, on a thread comparing the cost of building to buying: an itemized roughly **A$5,100** — A$2,000 for wood and resin, A$1,000 for a professionally made sail, A$900 for the jet-ski trailer, and A$1,200 for the Honda 2.3 hp outboard[22] — and, pressed for a fuller total, an **all-up A$7,000** for the whole boat, up from the roughly A$4,000 given for the same materials in 2015 above.[5]

Her joints were **screwed together dry** and "measured and measured" until the hull looked symmetrical, then glued; days later every screw came out and the holes were **backfilled with wooden dowels**, so no metal fastener was left driven into the wood to heat and crack the ply during cure. Any screw needed afterward for a fitting went into a hole **drilled oversize and backfilled with resin and powder**, then driven into the cured resin rather than raw wood: "the long way around but it never leads to cracks." The core idea, a temporary screw pulled once the glue cures, is [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md)'s own, from 2001 (see [fastening and gluing — a builder's alternative](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#a-builders-alternative-removable-screws)); the wooden-dowel backfill and the two-stage resin-plug method for a later screw are this build's own refinements on it.[23] The rudder gudgeon was **cast in a cut-out mould**, built up in glass cloth and micro filler and sanded to shape once it released; several years on it remained sound.[24] In years of sailing she **never shipped any water through the tiller hole**.[25]

## Post-launch rigging refinements (August 2015)

Two months after launch, "Thor" carried a deliberately temporary, doubled "crane jib" halyard purchase: from a snap shackle down to a double block on the yard, up to a single block at the masthead, back down to the yard, then up through the mast-top block again and down to the cabin — needing a lot of rope, most of it left occupying the cabin floor. The plan was to redesign it closer to the stock arrangement once the sail's own trim was better understood, since "keeping to the plan is paramount" — it keeps discussion of typical problems and fixes relevant to other builders, and adds value to a well-built boat besides. In the meantime the mast-box pulley and sheave were both upgraded to the biggest sizes that could be found, since small pulleys drag on the rope and add real effort to raising the sail.[26]

The birdsmouth mast (see [above](#a-birdsmouth-mast-built-with-professional-help)) came up again in the same exchange: built round in cross-section for one simple reason — "because I could," having always wanted to make one.[26] See [components/rig/running-rigging.md](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#a-crowded-masthead-and-rigging-friction-lessons-2015) for the general masthead-layout and rigging-friction lessons this exchange prompted, applicable beyond this one boat.

## A maiden voyage (November 2015)

Five months after ballast was first set to the waterline, "Thor" made her first sail. A custom-made plastic water tank had been overfilled before setting out; three miles offshore, water sloshing in the bilge from the overfill briefly caused alarm until a taste test confirmed it was fresh water rather than a leak, and confidence in the boat's tough, sturdy construction returned quickly even that far out on day one of testing. The tank itself, made by people who build water tanks for off-road vehicles and shaped to fit exactly under the floor in the space the plan shows for it, holds 70 litres and is topped up by filling a 2-litre bottle from it each morning before sailing; even with a baffle plate fitted it still makes odd sounds when half full, though it is strong enough to take the weight of a car driven over it. The same rot concern shaped how it was insulated: flotation/insulation foam of the kind surfboards are made from was resined and sanded to exact shape so no moisture could get in, and the gap on either side of the tank was cut to shape, resin-coated all over, and packed with foam of its own for extra flotation — brittle once cured, but enough to fill the void.[27] She sailed bullet-straight in light winds with minimal leeward drift, heavily ballasted as designed, stable and "great fun." A Torqeedo "Travel 1003" electric outboard, carried as a just-in-case backup, performed perfectly and was recommended to any Paradox owner considering one; see [auxiliary outboard power — a Torqeedo pointer, and its price](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-torqeedo-pointer-and-its-price).[28] Managing its limited range meant watching the motor's own time-remaining meter and stopping at about half charge, keeping enough in reserve to pick a way back through moored boats at a harbour mouth.[29]

On the first day out, in 10 kt of wind at best, she held 4 kt sailing to windward on a starboard reach for over an hour on the same track. Five miles out the wind died to a flat calm; after a rest, the return home was made under motor at 2 kt with a following tide, the yuloh not yet a comfortable tool. Adjusting the sail's tied-off rope ends — a bit tighter here, looser there — worked a crease out of the sail and picked up a little more speed, a fully set sail judged "a great sight." The verdict, a year of building and dreaming after starting, was that the boat is "everything people say it is," with further day outings, a heavy-weather test, and an eventual camping cruise along the Australian coastline planned to follow.[28]

The water tank itself may eventually be swapped out for the water-jug ballast arrangement already established elsewhere in the archive, alongside minor changes to the cabin seat and its padding.[28] A fellow prospective builder's reply credited the chine runners for the speeds such short hulls keep being reported to reach, and looked forward to the promised heavy-weather report.[30] The verdict, given directly to the list: "Well done Matt Layden, thank you for the boat's great performance."[31]

## A heavy-weather sail, a boom problem, and a Coast Guard tow (22 November 2015)

Five days after the maiden voyage, "Thor" met a genuine test: 25 kt gusting higher, in a short, close-packed chop 5 ft high and only 15-20 ft apart. The boat stayed stable through it but would not make headway to windward in the confused seas — hours of tacking from 9 am to 5 pm produced no real progress. Furling the Mk2 sail in the building wind was a struggle: the square-section boom's edges caught on everything and made it stutter and grab except in smooth water, prompting a decision to replace it with a round boom and a bigger thimble at its end. The Torqeedo electric outboard that had performed perfectly on the maiden voyage (see [above](#a-maiden-voyage-november-2015)) failed outright under the conditions — twisted and snapped parts inside an AU$3,000 unit, twice the cost of a petrol motor, undone by one rough day — leaving no choice but a tow from the Coast Guard into port. The plan afterward was to revert to a metal petrol outboard, reasoning that "a motor with plastic bits" may not belong at sea; see [auxiliary outboard power — a first heavy-weather failure, and a rebuilt mount](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-first-heavy-weather-failure-and-a-rebuilt-mount) for the motor's fuller repair story.[32]

The physics behind the stalled windward progress: reefing a sail in rising wind trades away driving force exactly as short, steep seas add braking force, and past a certain point the two cancel out — a small boat simply stops gaining to windward regardless of how well she is sailed. The practical answer is not to fight it: pick a destination that isn't dead into the wind, wait for a favorable tide, or duck into sheltered water with little sea until conditions ease.[33] Alastair Law's own view of it: "It is the waves that kill the speed in small boats like these... I try to be a gentleman and therefore resist sailing to windward."[34] A fellow prospective builder's reply praised the honesty of a report that broke from the list's usual fair-weather tone, recalled Phil Bolger himself once saying small boats cannot make progress to windward above about 25 kt, and suggested a sea anchor for next time.[35]

Reflecting on the outing days later, the decision to sail into worsening weather was described as deliberate rather than reckless: a calm morning forecast to build to 20-plus knots by afternoon, sailed close to a big city on a weekend so that help and a flat shoreline were near at hand if trouble came — which it did. The hull itself was never in doubt, and it felt "inevitable" that bad weather would eventually be met; better to test resolve and limited skill inshore, with services on call, than be caught out for the first time far from safety. Had the motor not failed, the day would simply have ended with an unmet destination rather than a disaster — sailing without a set destination, by contrast, is "just getting blown along." The verdict on the design itself: a Paradox "rocks around in heavy seas, but stays afloat easily," while a small flat-bottomed hull cannot gain ground to windward in short, choppy water — the answer is to find a bay, anchor, and wait it out, a point illustrated by recalling Joshua Slocum's own account of patience through a Magellan Strait storm as the model for long-distance sailing. Plans afterward were for weekly outings to build confidence for longer trips, now with a reliable petrol motor rather than one with "plastic bits."[36]

## Sail-shape troubleshooting: a yard rebuilt for the sail's actual cut (25 November 2015)

A visit to the sailmaker turned up the cause of a persistent crease running from the peak down to the clew: the Mk2 sail had been cut to fly on a straight, non-tapered spar, but the yard, built to the plan's own taper (which begins roughly where the halyard rope attaches), did not match it. Rather than unpick and re-sew the sail to fit the tapered yard, the fix chosen was to remake the yard itself as a flat, square, straight spar, slightly longer than before to suit the Mk2 sail, with the tie-off points along it free to be adjusted afterward to help furling and to get tension right — the same yard-length problem recurs elsewhere in the archive; see [Mk2 sail — a yard-taper/sail-cut mismatch, and a longer replacement yard](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#a-yard-tapersail-cut-mismatch-and-a-longer-replacement-yard). A new engine mount, fabricated in stainless steel, was worked out first with a temporary wooden mock-up to find the exact position clearing the rudder while keeping the motor tiltable and as close to the transom as possible; the Torqeedo itself turned out to have loose bolts straight from the factory, a manufacturing fault rather than storm damage, and was repaired and given "one more life" before an eventual switch to the trusted Honda 2.3 hp petrol outboard — an enthusiasm for electric power and solar charging traced to years spent running an entirely off-grid solar system at home.[37]

The wooden replacement yard was tried first as ordered; aluminium tube (5 mm wall thickness, by an initial guess) was floated as a fallback if a crease still showed once it was fitted — see [Mk2 sail — a yard-taper/sail-cut mismatch, and a longer replacement yard](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#a-yard-tapersail-cut-mismatch-and-a-longer-replacement-yard) and [rig and sail — yard material](/components/rig/index.md#yard-material-douglas-fir-remains-standard-aluminum-a-proven-alternative) for the fuller aluminium-yard exchange this drew.[38]

## An aluminium yard proven at sea, a planned aluminium boom, and a jibing lesson (December 2015)

By December, an aluminium yard — rather than the wooden replacement first tried above — had the Mk2 sail flying full: no taper, good and stiff, no folds even fully set, and lightweight. A boom in the same material was planned next, a length of high-tensile, 1.5 mm-thick, 50 mm tube sourced from a trucking-industry supplier; Alastair Law's caution in reply was that plenty of weight in the boom is what stops it lifting in a blow, a property the light aluminium tube might not supply.[39][40] Testing stability separately that week, in a following sea and dead tailwind close to shore, throwing the tiller hard over swung the boat quickly with a fair heel and no trouble — but the sail then lifted, the boom swinging upright alongside the mast and wrapping the sail into a "Y" shape at the masthead, fully folded out of shape; releasing the halyards and hauling in the mainsheet had it flying properly again in no time. The lesson drawn: tighten the mainsheet early, before turning, when tacking or jibing with the sail set full and by.[39]

## An aluminium boom confirmed, and the wooden boom retired (December 2017)

The planned aluminium boom above went ahead: two years on, an aluminium boom now flies alongside the aluminium yard, the sail itself recut for a parallel-yard rig rather than the tapered plan sail, and the wooden boom and yard built for it retired to the shed unused.[41] The change came alongside a run of further roller-reefing refinements — rope fed into the middle of the spool rather than to one side, a pulley in place of a bare guide, a further large pulley in the vent trunk, and reefing in small increments — the combination finally judged to make the gear "sane to use"; full account at [components/rig/furling.md — a wider topping-lift debate, and independent fixes](/components/rig/furling.md#a-wider-topping-lift-debate-and-independent-fixes-december-2017).[41]

## A mast weight restated, an aluminium-mast question, and a rudder-ropes answer (January 2016)

A year on, the birdsmouth mast (see [above](#a-birdsmouth-mast-built-with-professional-help)) was recalled again, more roughly, at about 20 lb — and prompted a further question only ever asked on record, not answered: whether a section of aluminum mast tube, readily available and not prohibitively expensive, would be lighter still, needing only a wooden foot shaped to fit the mast box; see [components/rig/mast.md — finished mast weights compared](/components/rig/mast.md#finished-mast-weights-compared) for the wider aluminum-versus-wood comparison this echoes.[42] Days later, replying to a new owner's confusion over which way a steering line actually turns the boat, gave the definitive answer directly: pulling the right-hand line aft turns the boat right, "easy peezy" — mastered within minutes. Full technique: [components/steering.md](/components/steering.md#citations).[43]

## A knockdown test and a ballast-placement debate (February 2016)

Weighing in on a newcomer's capsize-recovery questions, described testing "Thor" in rough seas to the point of being knocked down, though never rolled, the righting moment holding up as expected; ballasted her with lead bolted along the inside of the hull against the chines, as far outboard as it could be got, on the reasoning that it helps dynamic stability by keeping the ballast outboard — a question put, before building, to the engineering department of Adelaide University. The rounded deck and roof, together with the sheer weight in the bilges, gave enough confidence in her self-righting that stability graphs, found by a Google search and again when the plans were ordered, only confirmed what already felt solid and stable at sea — including falling asleep the first time aboard her, about five miles out from shore. Flotation/insulation foam, of the kind used for surfboards and sanded and shaped to fit under the decking and floor, was sited so a swamped boat would still sit proud of the water; its buoyancy, and that of the woods used in the build, were both measured and calculated rather than assumed, alongside accounts elsewhere in circulation of a Paradox swamped, bailed, and sailed on regardless. The measuring method itself, restated three years later to a fellow owner working through the same question, was a homemade buoyancy test: cutting a thin stick from each type of wood used in the build, standing it upright in a jar of water, and reading how much of the stick stayed proud of the surface against how much sank — a rough but workable way to weigh a hull's own materials against her ballast before trusting the calculation at sea.[44] The test itself: let over in about 25 kt of breeze, she sat on her side momentarily, untouched, then popped back up, turned into wind, and lay hove-to, drifting broadside across the chop until sail and tiller were adjusted to get going again, followed by a reef and a return home — "an experience that confirmed... I'm as safe as the design suggests," uncomfortable on her side but never unsafe.[45]

A correction followed on the ballast's own mechanics: placing lead as far outboard as possible can improve the roll characteristics and the dynamic "feel," but does not by itself help the righting moment, since each outboard weight balances the other — one side's righting moment is offset by the other's heeling moment as the boat heels.[46] The reply distinguished two separate effects rather than disputing the correction: once actually capsized, all that matters is that the heavier side ends up on top, awkwardly balanced on the boat's own curved deck; the dynamic balance and "feel" genuinely do improve with weight further outboard, but the **static** balance is unaffected by where the lead sits in the bilge, so long as it is placed symmetrically. See [design/weight-and-ballast.md — ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) for the fuller technical exchange this restates.[47]

A concrete follow-up two months later gave a real-world example of the fore-and-aft side of the same question: with no ballast weight yet in compartment two, "Thor" gave trouble tacking in anything but smooth seas, a trim issue expected to resolve once her battery was fitted in that compartment.[48]

## A second Coast Guard tow, ballast resolved, and a Wardang Island lap (April 2016)

The compartment-two trim gap noted above was closed that spring: a large tractor battery went in ahead of the mast, and the added forward weight was credited with easing the boat's earlier trouble getting through irons in a head wind.[49] Off the same stretch of the Yorke Peninsula coast where the maiden voyage had been sailed, an earlier outing had needed a two-hour tow from the Coast Guard — a passing fisherman had called them in, and they all but insisted on towing the boat back from 5-6 nautical miles offshore in a head wind and rough seas. A later, calmer return to the same water lapped Wardang Island, about 20 nautical miles in a day, the longest day sail yet, in a flat calm sea and 27°C — no challenge at all, unlike the tow-in trip. The long swell was no trouble and even welcome, but chop thumping the hull loudly was another matter. In rough water and strong wind, easing the sail a little off the mast on the starboard tack, rather than sheeting it in hard against the mast, made it fly fuller and pull harder; the port tack fared worse under the same conditions, slipping backward while ploughing into heavy chop, and turning the boat at all was only possible with the motor running. Winter's approach meant a plan to trailer the boat a three-day drive north to Queensland, chasing trade-wind sailing in warm water, with a request for tips on reaching out and back in the stronger 30-plus-degree-south trade-wind conditions expected there.[49]

Tempted by the tacking trouble to "mess things up" with a Bermuda rig instead — a triangular main and jib, just to see what would happen — the idea was set aside in favor of simply sailing as much as possible in warm climates for now.[50] A real precedent for that conversion already existed, and turned out to be a one-way trip: see [Tom Steinmetz's Paradox](/boats/tom-steinmetzs-paradox.md#converted-one-way-only) for the boat that made it.

Passing along a fix from his own sail's history to a fellow builder wrestling with uneven furling, recounted having replaced a wooden yard, originally cut to the plan's taper, with a straight, non-tapered aluminium one once the sail turned out cut for a straight spar — the creases mostly disappeared as a result (full story: [components/rig/mast.md — an aluminium yard proven at sea](/components/rig/mast.md) and [above](#an-aluminium-yard-proven-at-sea-a-planned-aluminium-boom-and-a-jibing-lesson-december-2015)), with the same fix planned for the boom once affordable — though a matched pair of spars fixes shape, not necessarily uneven furling. A sailmaker's own crease-check method came with it: take the sail off its spars, have three people pull evenly on each corner, and check that it lies flat and crease-free.[51]

## Preparing for a trip north (April 2016)

Ahead of trailering north for the promised trade-wind sailing, asked a fellow builder what to pack for the trip — mostly day sailing, but a long way from home with some overnight stops likely, planned or otherwise, and plenty of truck space to carry whatever was needed. Settled on resin and a set of tools and spares for the Honda 2.3 hp outboard as a starting list.[52] The reply settled on the basics: tools, spare cordage, some thin plywood and epoxy glue for an emergency patch, and — travelling solo for a stretch — simply having good food and drink aboard.[53] Preparations continued the same week with a battery box built and fitted into compartment 2 per the plan, wired in through a late evening's work, ahead of a last coastal sail before departing home for a couple of months.[2]

## A magazine feature invitation, and the mast maker named (December 2016)

Invited by a UK magazine planning a 2017 Paradox build feature to share photographs and experience, reported "Thor" heading out sailing again soon, with six trips logged so far, and offered photographs from various stages of the build. Named the maker of the birdsmouth mast (see [above](#a-birdsmouth-mast-built-with-professional-help)) for the first time as **Duck Flat Wooden Boats**, of South Australia.[54]

## A camping cruise in Spencer Gulf, and a freak-wave knockdown (February-March 2017)

"Thor" made her first overnight camping trip in late February and early March 2017, sailing north in **Spencer Gulf**, about 200 km from Adelaide, South Australia, through heat-wave conditions — 18°C at night, 36°C by day, under a big high-pressure system. The first day out brought light wind all day and a three-hour tacking duel to clear a harbor mouth against an incoming tide before a secluded, wide beach offered an overnight anchorage.[55]

The trip's real test came a few days later. Sunburn was the expected hazard, guarded against by covering up entirely — the actual danger turned out to be a freak wave that rolled the boat 90 degrees and put some water into the bilges, dried out that same afternoon. It happened while cutting a corner close to a cape in search of a favorable current, on an otherwise frustrating day of light wind averaging under 2 knots and some motoring: a wave broke just 5 metres away in very shallow water, running parallel to the hull, and rolled the boat quickly onto her starboard side while standing in the hatchway and holding on tight. The boat righted herself immediately — credited to the ballast and the design's ruggedness — and was pushed a little toward shore with the masthead briefly under water; even with the sail partly submerged, it sprang straight back up as the wave passed. The lesson drawn was that more careful map reading and patience could easily have avoided it altogether, and that close-in sailing in strong currents over shallows, where a strong current meets shallow water, is ripe for exactly this kind of changeable, breaking wave.[56]

The same passage included an hour of sailing after dark — unnerving, having never sailed much and never at night before — tethered to the hull by a light line clipped to a life jacket, with a length of floating, bright yellow ski-tow rope trailing behind as a safety line. A lighthouse eight miles across the bay provided a steer, and a sharp turn at first light, in the half-dark, narrowly avoided running over a fishing net set some three miles offshore. Despite the freak wave, the first camping trip was judged a success — fun and adventurous enough to want to do again soon, with the coming autumn expected to bring stronger, cooler weather.[56]

## A chine-runner hypothesis prompted by the freak wave

The freak-wave knockdown above prompted a technical hypothesis about the chine runners themselves from a fellow builder: a hull with a vertical foil (like a keel) loses its lateral resistance as it heels away from a beam sea, while a chine runner does the opposite — it bites in and becomes *more* effective as the hull heels down — so the combination could, in principle, keep a hull rolling on past 90 degrees as it slides sideways down a wave face, driven by the combined sideways acceleration on the wave face and the increasing dig of the runner into the wave body. Full discussion, including how this squares with the runners' more usual role in resisting a roll: [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a further hypothesis, prompted by a beam-sea knockdown](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-further-hypothesis-could-the-runners-amplify-a-beam-wave-roll-past-90-degrees-2017).[57]

## A further reflection: an open-boat comparison, and the righting margin quantified (5 March 2017)

Two days later, replying to Bourbeau's reply above, drew a comparison against a second boat also owned: a Stevensons Pocket Cruiser, the same length as a Paradox but a lot wider, with flotation tanks under the cockpit seats. In the same freak-wave situation, that open boat would have swamped as the wave turned her, then floated hull-up, bobbing against the rocks while taking further damage — the flotation tanks holding a survivor at the surface rather than keeping the hull itself afloat right side up, and only if they were not holed. Had never before considered that a freak wave could push a boat along "like a bulldozer blade," laying her on her side and driving her forward under wave pressure while scooping water into the cabin — concluding more sailing experience is needed before taking an open boat where "Thor" already goes. By contrast, "Thor"'s own recovery was immediate: as soon as the wave washed under the hull she popped straight back up, with a righting capability judged able to bring her back from "way way over 90 degrees to vertical," though hoping never to have to see that tested in earnest. A post-incident inspection, built strictly to plan, found her completely sound and untouched, just wet.[58]

## A deliberate capsize test, and mast buoyancy versus ballast in a knockdown (August 2018)

Answering a fresh posting of a Paradox stability diagram and the standing question of whether any owner has actually been knocked down or capsized, gave the fullest firsthand account on record of two separate knockdowns. The first was deliberate: sailing close by the judges' boat during a local wooden-boat regatta, set the boat up to roll and took a sharp wave abeam under full sail — she rolled to almost right angles, sat for a second, then abruptly rolled upright again, coming up quickly. The second, recounted in full for the first time here, was the same 2017 Spencer Gulf freak-wave knockdown already on record above (see [above](#a-camping-cruise-in-spencer-gulf-and-a-freak-wave-knockdown-february-march-2017)), retold with new detail: hunting for a tide race inshore for extra speed at day's end, hatch open, an idling outboard masking the sound of the breaking wave until the boat was already over the reef in shallow water — sail buried underwater, boat over 90 degrees, shoved sideways by the wave for five to ten seconds while water scooped in through the hatch, before the sail emptied and the boat came up "with a vengeance," bruising him against the hatch side; the anchor and a fishing rod went overboard in the process, and clearing the area afterward took motoring through four further large waves. Never been over since.[82]

Pressed on the mechanism — whether the square chine or chine runner make a Paradox more apt to trip when hit abeam, and whether the sail merely skimmed the surface (rather than filling with water and rolling the boat clear over), or the buoyant mast, is what kept her from going all the way over[83] — the answer drew a sharp distinction: nowhere near inverted, but far enough over for the hatch to scoop water in; the sail stayed underwater holding the boat in that pose only while the wave itself was actively moving the boat, and any hull shape gets shoved along by a genuinely powerful rolling wave regardless, the chine runners having little effect on that. Mast buoyancy is negligible next to the real reason the boat stayed recoverable: the weight held firm in the bilges. Ballast advice sought years earlier from the Adelaide University Engineering Department — outboard rather than central (see [a knockdown test and a ballast-placement debate](#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016)) — was restated as the deciding factor, trusted under way even though it made no visible difference sitting static in the shed; happy that under all conditions the boat will always roll upright after a knockdown.[84]

## A bow U-bolt, and a Coast Guard tow's rope-length lesson recalled (September 2018)

Has a U-bolt through his own bow; mounted too high, it would force the boat's nose down when winching her onto the trailer, complicating the final approach onto it.[86] The fitting has never given trouble under real strain: recalling the same Coast Guard tow already on record above (see [a second Coast Guard tow, ballast resolved, and a Wardang Island lap](#a-second-coast-guard-tow-ballast-resolved-and-a-wardang-island-lap-april-2016)) — three hours under tow in pounding seas after a passing fisherman spotted the boat well offshore and called it in, brought home the long way around a shallow but navigable reef — neither the U-bolt nor the bow was ever troubled by it, and the tow taught a lasting lesson: ask for a long rope between the two boats, since a short one snatches after every wave and slams the hull. ## A topping lift re-fitted, a season's refit, and a grounding on a shell-grit island (September 2018)

Re-fitted a topping lift that had been removed a few months earlier once it tangled; tried a lift on the tumbler thimble too, workable if kept from jamming, with a caution that a boat also carrying lazyjacks risks a second, similar tangle point. The same refit for the season moved the ballast forward, added the topping lift, revarnished the exterior wood, and flattened the rudder blade's face where it had gone wobbly bearing against the upper rudder — built up with a layer of thin plywood, resined over repeatedly, and sanded dead flat — plus a refitted under-floor water tank and its plumbing. A locking deck cleat holds about 30 ft of anchor line always ready to run; releasing it lets the boat skew into wind once the anchor digs in, leaving the boom centred for a full furl — tried five times at home, the topping lift bringing the sail down on the first pull every time, worked gradually rather than in one long haul, with the topping-lift line itself cleated inside near the helm. ## Another day of sailing: alterations proven, and ballast physics restated (October 2018)

A full day testing a season's worth of alterations in Adelaide's Port River — narrow, industrial, and busy with traffic, including commercial vessels that seemed reluctant to give way — bore out every change made. Roughly 30 kg of lead ingots and anchor chain, in stout canvas bags, now sit in the bow; tacking through calm water needed the oar on only about four turns in thirty, some ten strokes each time, to bring her firmly back across the wind. Hand-holding the mainsheet through a tack, rather than leaving it cleated as usual, gave a clearer feel for the load coming on and off the sail. With the ballast forward, the boat held a course across the wind and steered herself hands-free for about twenty minutes on one run, the rudder locked off on cleats fitted inside at the top of the rear shelves. Relocating the mainsheet's exit point higher and closer to the cabin ended the tangling it used to cause around the outboard motor. The topping lift, cleating the mainsheet central to the hull and furling in small, measured amounts on an unloaded sail, brought the sail down cleanly every time — twelve furls for twelve, on the water and in practice combined. The rudder blade's face, sanded dead flat and built up with resined plywood where it bore on the upper rudder, stopped its clunking and helped her track downwind, though moving the ballast forward helped more; only one gybe all day. A rope inspection at the two-year mark found one worn close to its end, simply reversed for another year or two of service; a new deck cleat lets the anchor cable be dropped in seconds, the boat then swinging into wind on her own to unload the sail for a quick furl; the boom was shortened slightly, having fouled the mainsheet over the outboard; a collapsible boat hook, reaching outside from inside the cabin, replaced crawling the deck at sea; spare rope and tools, and spare double pulleys for kedging off, round out a small recovery kit carried aboard. Moving the ballast that had sat under the main floor forward, running it the full length of the floor against the lower chine and the chine log, made the boat heel more but again bore out the outboard-ballast advice already worked out with Adelaide University's engineering department (see [a knockdown test and a ballast-placement debate](#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016)).[89] A reply to the traffic complaint above pointed out that a commercial vessel confined to a narrow channel is in fact owed room under the international collision regulations (Rule 9, narrow channels, and Rule 18, responsibilities between vessels).[90]

Asked separately, a few days later, whether the same forward-moved ballast had also cut the bow's slapping in waves under power, confirmed it had: before the ballast went forward the bow slapped noticeably motoring into a chop; afterward, "much better."[91]

## A four-day passage, and a last look at the inherited Bermuda rig (April 2019)

A four-day, roughly 70 nautical-mile passage that spring — dropped off and picked up again by car — ran before a good breeze the whole way, the same wind holding all week. Close to shore the sea stayed calmer, waves built by the wind coming off the land topping out around 4 ft with a steep face; further out, waves bigger than the boat itself made for an ominous view astern even though the boat rode over them without trouble. Reefing hard and early was the standing habit throughout, on the reasoning that the boat "seems to have a bigger sail than needed" for the conditions actually met.

The first night at anchor, with no hot food, brought broken sleep; going to windward in anything rough with the Mk2 sail was judged a waste of time, motoring instead to a safe anchorage to wait it out rather than press on. A broad reach proved far more relaxing than a dead following wind, needing much less work on the tiller, though the flat bottom slapped hard and made for a slow, uncomfortable ride running in a rough sea — fine again once the water calmed. Sailing in a warm climate meant condensation was no problem, and the roof stayed open most nights even so; closing it up felt more secure in big waves regardless, "a mental thing... being separated from the water." The second day ended beached at midafternoon to cook a curry, then drifting under bare poles until dark watching the sea floor pass beneath, before finding a wind-sheltered cove for the night. The third day, covering familiar water and sailing only half of it, brought a passing boat alongside to check all was well — small enough that Paradox draws such offers often, prompting the thought of painting "Go away, I'm OK" on the hull; a walk ashore that afternoon passed a reef that had "almost claimed me and the boat" two years before, a story left for another time. The last day brought mixed wind but a steady 4 kt throughout.[107]

Reflecting on rig choice in the same exchange, the Bermuda rig inherited two years earlier from a friend's own unfinished project (see [above](#a-second-bermuda-rig-temptation-and-a-windward-performance-account-june-2017)) came up again, still unfitted, and fitting it was raised once more.[107] A reply set out the size cost such a conversion would trade away: the stock sprit rig's mast, sprit boom, and yard each run to about 4 m, small enough that the whole rig strikes and stows entirely inside the hull — any longer, and the spars would have to ride outside — a compactness judged worth keeping, drawn from firsthand experience sailing in company with a Bermuda-rigged dinghy over two days (see [operations/hull-speed.md — a two-day comparison against a Bermuda-rigged dinghy](/operations/hull-speed.md#a-two-day-comparison-against-a-bermuda-rigged-dinghy)).[108] The idea stayed an idle thought rather than a real changeover plan — "It's just for fun really as I have the sails and they're new and idle" — alongside an unconfirmed, unlinked report of a Bermuda-rigged Paradox somewhere in South America; attention by then had shifted to building a 7 m by 2.3 m house boat ahead of winter, and the inherited rig remained untried.[109]

## A padded cockpit, and a third telling of the tow (May 2019)

Replying to a fellow owner's own Force 6 rescue account (see [design/blue-water-suitability.md — a 2019 test case](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2019-test-case-a-force-6-rescue-and-skipper-endurance-as-the-real-limit)), retold the 2016 Coast Guard tow already on record above (see [a second Coast Guard tow, ballast resolved, and a Wardang Island lap](#a-second-coast-guard-tow-ballast-resolved-and-a-wardang-island-lap-april-2016)) with a maxim drawn from it — accept a tow if sea rescue offers one rather than wait to be asked, "never say no at sea" — and confirmed the same tow's own lesson about rope length: the short line used let the two boats pitch out of step, snatching and jerking the hull under tow for over two hours. The cockpit around the helm position has since been **padded**, a direct response to the bruising taken in the August 2018 knockdown already on record above (see [a deliberate capsize test, and mast buoyancy versus ballast in a knockdown](#a-deliberate-capsize-test-and-mast-buoyancy-versus-ballast-in-a-knockdown-august-2018)).[110]

The same reply set out a standing technique for the slap of a flat bottom driving to windward into wind and waves — the real limiting factor, on this account, rather than leeway (see [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a 2019 doubt about leeway sufficiency](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-2019-doubt-about-leeway-sufficiency-and-a-bow-slap-rebuttal)): the bow carries extra ballast, buried forward specifically to cut down the slapping that slows her, with anchoring up or beaching the fallback if the wind turns unfavorable rather than pressing on regardless. In sheltered water the runners tack her up a narrow river without any trouble at all.[111]

Replying that September to a fellow owner's own account of a deliberate offshore trial, added his own experience of sleeping at anchor: noise-sensitive by nature, found the gurgling of small ripples against the hull took real getting used to, and set two nights against each other — one anchored in a tidal creek thick with insects, tolerable only with every light kept off, where the tide's own wash while it ran made sleep impossible but the slack water between tide changes was fine; and one anchored close to shore on a flat, calm night, which gave a full, deep sleep.[112]

Months later, restated the same confidence in general terms: he feels safe in her offshore, having followed accounts of people who have sunk far bigger boats — size alone, in his own view, is no guarantee of survival.[113]

## Sold across the country, and renamed "Pamela B" (2022)

The listing itself survives. In **September 2021**, Bell offered her for sale in South Australia, describing her as built to plan and "very sturdy," with Oregon chine logs and runners and a marine-plywood hull under a heavy fibreglass layup.[114] This is very likely the "old Paradox mailing-list post" Brothers recalls finding "the previous year" when he came across her, below.[115]

The listing drew a fresh inquiry the following week, from a correspondent in Tasmania.[116] Bell's reply is the fullest specification and condition report of the boat on record: riding on the modified jet-ski trailer, anti-fouled below, fitted with Ronson-brand pulleys and a professionally made sail; a removable PVC water tank custom-built by Abber Tanks of Queensland; the windows crazed but still intact; a deliberately oversized, custom-rollered anchor that "will hold in any ground" under any condition he had tried; every joint 100% glued with no gaps; a hull bottom of 19 mm marine plywood, in Kapur and Oregon, under two layers of double-bias fibreglass cloth; a birdsmouth hollow mast built from hoop pine; and buoyancy foam built into the fore deck, side decks, transom, and both the rear and front compartment floors. He had rolled her deliberately once, and she came back up on her own with no action needed beyond waiting; months later a breaking wave caught her broadside at sea and rolled her again, and she came up fast once the sail dumped its load of water. The build had cost **$8,500** and taken **eight months**, five years earlier — set against the **A$7,000**/six-years-earlier figures already on record above, a discrepancy left as stated rather than reconciled. She carried a Honda 2.5 hp outboard alongside the Yuloh oar, which he judged "useless" at sea, though fine in calm water "if you have the knack to use them." He had made two other boats before this one, with a shipwright's help on the harder parts of all of them, closing with "This boat is a genuine great built hardy example of a fun machine."[117] Asked in reply for exterior and interior photographs and where she had sailed,[118] Bell answered that same evening: mostly in the **Gulf waters of South Australia**, in trips of **three to seven days**, "a hoot, very tiring though," drawing interest, waves and photographs from the crews of visiting 12-metre-class yachts. The deliberate roll recorded above had been staged in front of the Sydney-Hobart race's own practice fleet off Adelaide, on the reasoning that plenty of boats were on hand to help if it went wrong; a further tow that trip, called in by a passing fisherman rather than requested, closed on the same lesson already on record above — accepting help unasked beats risking a second, worse call-out to the same sailor in one day.[119]

A further, more detailed round of questions followed the next morning: how many of the water tank's three visible pipes were for filling and how easily it came out; whether a boxed space where the seat would be was correctly identified; the sleeping area's length lying down; where the outboard's petrol was kept; the trailer and boat's combined all-up weight; and the jet-ski trailer's own make and length — offered, unprompted, that a wheelbarrow inner tube makes a good cushion.[120] Bell's answers filled out the boat's remaining domestic detail. The tank takes a fill pipe, an outlet and an air inlet, holds about 70 litres, is contoured to the floor (taller forward, shallower aft), and comes out through the main hatch once the middle floor panels are unscrewed. The fitted seat had proved too hard, so he built one with upholstery straps before settling on a kiddies' bean bag instead; the sleeping area suits his own 5'6" comfortably, and he sleeps deeply once beached, "warm and dry, if a bit messy." Petrol for the Honda 2.5 hp outboard, "great motor if a bit noisy," travels in 1-litre bottles rather than a can, five litres seeing out a week unused. The boat's all-up trimmed weight was never measured but is "damn heavy," beyond four grown men to shift; the jet-ski trailer was bought secondhand, its make unknown, and flexes slightly under her weight. Kapur, already used on the hull bottom, also lines the base of the interior bulkheads, "tough as steel" and heavy with it; a long chain kept in the very front locker helps trim the boat and doubles as emergency ground tackle, alongside the seven metres of chain already on the main anchor's own rode. His standing ballast advice was to use everything the plans call for and keep the weight down low regardless. Weighing the boat's stowage against a lifetime of backpacking and bushwalking, he closed: "It's amazing what will go in a paradox."[121]

Her builder's own later summary of that chapter is a single sentence — "I had a paradox, I sold it after years of adventure" — naming neither buyer nor date, and he went on to build a small pontoon houseboat and take it 1,550 km down the Murray instead.[1]

Five months after the sale, replying on a "Build or buy" thread weighing whether to fit a motor at all, Bell gave a retrospective verdict drawn from his years aboard her: the Yuloh worked well only on flat water and went essentially unused elsewhere — useless in choppy conditions and against the strong tidal flow of his home waters — and "looked like new" when the boat was sold. A motor solved the same problem but brought its own: it caught the mainsheet, and its weight aft upset her balance.[122]

The buyer, it emerges, was **[Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md)** of **Newcastle, New South Wales**, who renamed her **"Pamela B."** He stated the provenance plainly in the description of his own YouTube channel: "Pamela B was built by Nick Bell in South Australia and sailed there as 'Thor' for several years before I was lucky enough to acquire her and fit her out for luxury dinghy sailing."[123]

The purchase, told from the buyer's side without ever naming the boat or her builder, matches in every particular: in **February 2022** Brothers — looking for a boat he could launch and sail single-handed, and finding no Australian kit supplier or builder — came across an old Paradox mailing-list post suggesting one of the country's four boats had been offered for sale the previous year, and a message to the apparent owner got a same-day yes. The boat was in **South Australia**, some 1520 km and a week's round-trip drive away, so the seller towed her partway and the two met at the New South Wales border at **Mildura**. She was **seven years old** — consistent with the June 2015 launch above — and in generally good condition, and refurbishment began that same weekend.[115] A fourth agreement, recorded independently on both sides years apart, is the **trailer**: Bell bought a jet-ski trailer for A$800 and had it modified locally to carry her (see [above](#materials-cost-and-a-modified-jet-ski-trailer)), and Brothers describes the boat as riding on a trailer converted from a jet-ski trailer, its drawbar considerably strengthened to take her weight, the same trailer strengthened again by her second owner; he put Paradox's practical towing weight at around 1200 lb, more than the 1000 lb a fellow prospective owner had assumed when shopping for a trailer.[18][17][124][125] Her second life under Brothers, including the coastal and river passages she has since made and the rig changes made to her, continues below.

A final recollection came in January 2025, answering a newcomer's cost question: an all-up build cost of A$7,000, built with a shipwright's help at Duck Flat Wooden Boats, over roughly eight months at a pace recalled this time as six hours a day, four days a week — a different rhythm from the five-hours/five-days figure given in real time back in 2015, left as stated rather than reconciled. The same reply states she sold for A$7,000 — against the A$8,500 asking price on the 2021 listing above — and now lives north of Sydney, consistent with Newcastle, New South Wales, already on record as her second home.[126]

Under her new name she became the boat for which the [Mk3 sail](/components/rig/mk3-sail.md) was drawn: the larger, higher-peaked Michael Storer / Really Simple Sails plan of 2023, whose published account is illustrated by a video titled for her.

## A refit, and early cruising under her second owner (2022)

She came to Brothers with a rigid foam layer glued under the decks but otherwise no built-in insulation. Her main cabin's sides were retrofitted with EVA foam cut from exercise mats, applied over double-sided tape across the whole surface, the same technique already used successfully on her floorboards and chosen deliberately so the foam could be peeled back to inspect for rot, judged a low risk since the whole interior is epoxied; a small USB-powered dehumidifier was fitted to help control moisture inside whenever the hatch is shut.[127]

By October 2022 she was cruising. A 20-nautical-mile outing on Lake Macquarie showed up twist in the sail, made worse by a sheeting arrangement that led the sheet off the top of a post to keep it clear of the outboard through tacks and gybes, a fix already worked out that July for the original Honda 3 hp outboard's mainsheet-snagging problem (see [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md)); a roller bearing was fitted to the boom end to carry the extra load of a vang system, and Navionics logged a peak of 6.2 knots with consistent high-4 and 5-knot speeds.[128] The vang hooks onto a wire hawse running along the rubbing strake, adjustable fore-and-aft for maximum downward pull, and is tacked from side to side by unhooking, easing and re-hooking it, with a forward shockcord keeping the whole assembly clear of a gybe.[129] The same month she went onto open water on Broken Bay, handling two hours of bouncing on the open sea better than her skipper did.[130]

In December 2022 her sailing weight was cut by around 150 kg: a 150 amp-hour lead-acid gel battery gave way to a 100 amp-hour lithium iron battery, redundant gear was discarded, and only 16 litres of water were carried. The change was reported transformative, with the boat making over 4 knots downwind in 10 to 15 knots of wind and 3.9 knots upwind in similar conditions the next day.[131]

## Answering criticism of her windward ability (January 2023)

In January 2023, answering persistent criticism of the Paradox's windward ability, Brothers sailed her in a full circle without tacking around the Captain James Cook Memorial Fountain on Lake Burley Griffin in Canberra.[132]

## Further cruising in New South Wales (2023 to 2024)

She has sailed repeatedly with the **Sydney RAID group**: a weekend out in November 2022,[133] a Rathmines to Bolton Point to Pulbah Island raid on Lake Macquarie in December 2023 combined with local WWII history,[134] and another weekend away in November 2024.[135] Further passages followed: a 13-nautical-mile coastal leg from Swansea to Newcastle in March 2023, sailed entirely upwind into a breeze that built from 12 to 30 knots;[136] a trip up the Manning River on the New South Wales mid-north coast in June 2023;[137] an October 2023 overnight raid to the Sand Islands that, once his companions turned back, became his longest day-sail yet, during which he finally got her self-steering well enough to cook a steak and vegetables underway;[138] a run up the Hawkesbury River north of Sydney in August 2024;[139] and, in January 2024, a passage of more than 22 hours from Throsby Creek at Carrington, up Newcastle Harbour and the Hunter River through Tomago, Hexham, Heatherbrae and Raymond Terrace to the old river port of Morpeth and back the next morning, through winds ranging from a measured 28 knots down to calm and every point of the compass, with wind against tide in the narrow reaches.[140]

## The Mk3 sail and later tuning (2023 to 2025)

In April 2023 she was fitted with a new, larger sail built specifically to lift her light-wind performance without changing spars: the Mk3 sail drawn by Michael Storer of Really Simple Sails for her (see [components/rig/mk3-sail.md](/components/rig/mk3-sail.md) for the sail's own design account).[141] Tuning continued from there: engaging her electric outboard at idle when sailing upwind for roughly a full knot of extra speed without losing the quiet of sailing, cutting the water ballast she carries by half, and enlarging the sail further by lengthening the yard's leech; against a claim that a longer waterline alone would solve her speed, her roughly 13 ft waterline was worked out to give a hull speed near 4.8 knots, with a 25% longer waterline lifting that only to about 5.6 knots.[142]

## Anchoring and fit-out under her second owner

Her anchoring is worked entirely from inside the cabin: the rode runs from a bow roller, where the anchor sits ready underway, back to a snapshackle at the aft end of the cabin, with the rope stowed in a rope-tidy bag on the rear deck, so dropping anchor is just a matter of releasing the snapshackle pin without going on deck at all; a broken tack strop once forced a foredeck repair anyway, after anchoring in a sizeable seaway.[143] One of the smaller Porta Potti-style toilets was fitted, stowed against the transom, where its two halves, seat and water tank, and holding tank, fit the transom's internal beam almost exactly.[144]
