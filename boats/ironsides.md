# Ironsides

**"Ironsides"** is [Stephen Yahn](/people/stephen-yahn.md)'s [Paradox](/paradox.md), started in Manchester, Washington, on Puget Sound, in July 2006. Stephen bought exterior fir plywood in mid-July 2006, reasoning the boat would spend 99% of its life on a trailer, and began construction shortly after.

## A widened, steel-reinforced bulkhead 3

Stephen opened the "Bulkhead 3" thread proposing to narrow that bulkhead's vertical plywood webs for extra sleeping width, reinforced with steel rather than more plywood (see [bulkheads — modifying bulkhead 3 to gain room for sleeping two](/components/bulkheads.md#modifying-bulkhead-3-to-gain-room-for-sleeping-two) for the full builder discussion this proposal drew). By late July 2006 he had begun construction in earnest, building the modification as steel, not plywood: a vertical web widened to 1.5 in, reinforced with steel weldments (4.8 lb each) sealed in epoxy, fastened with hot-dip galvanized bolts likewise assembled and sealed in epoxy.[1] He planned cloth, rather than plywood, bins in place of the standard fixed bin panels, did the trial assembly of the sides and bulkheads, and found fitting the stem "character building."[1] Built without bins at all, the finished modification yields a **41 in** cabin sole width.[2] Restating the same steel-angle framing technique three years later as a general way to gain room within a Paradox's stock length, Stephen credited it directly for the boat's own name.[122]

## A side-mounted outboard, fabricated to spare his hands

With his hands too old and sore for yuloing, and having already fitted a 2 hp Honda outboard, Stephen floated mounting it outside the hull near bulkhead #4 rather than on the transom or through a cabin panel, so it could be started, refueled, and throttled from the hatchway and unshipped for stowage when not needed.[3] He went ahead with the side-mount plan and started fitting blocks inside the hull for the motor mount.[4] Keeping the fuel supply in an outside plastic jerrycan rather than bottles stowed near the cooking area, he set the outboard's anticavitation plate flush with the hull bottom to keep the propeller from running deeper than, and damaging, the chine runners.[5] He posted photos of the finished side motor mount, showing its full construction — a sized-to-fit wooden pad for the Honda 2 hp motor, a hitch pin, a ball valve against flooding, a bracing shelf, and a copper (rather than plastic) grounding pipe doing double duty as a cable groove.[6] Prompted by a caution from another builder, he realized fitting the mount afloat risked shipping water as he leaned over the side to align the motor shaft, and planned to start the shaft in only as far as the ball valve before opening it, plus a lanyard since the motor would not float if dropped.[7]

## Building the vent trunk drain

Stephen chose a **one-inch copper pipe** for the vent trunk drain, doing double duty as the lightning-ground conductor down to his mast.[8] For the vent box itself, he glued together blocks of wood with a groove cut to accept the pipe liner, bonded in place with thickened epoxy, and used copper tubing for grounding his crystal radio, the pipe again doing double duty.[9] The groove was cut with two saw cuts, then a chisel to flatten the bottom, and filled around the pipe with epoxy thickened with sawdust or flour — plain kitchen wheat flour gave good long-term results — leaving the tubing proud and trimming it flush once cured.[10]

## Rudder gudgeons, deck keepers, and a first attempt at powder coating

Stephen machined a pair of rudder gudgeons from thick aluminum plate bought at Boeing surplus, and made the arched deck keepers from steel — flat discs cut from 1/4 in plate brazed to 1 in-diameter tubing bent from electric conduit fittings. To stop corrosion on the steel parts, he tried powder coating for the first time. On a suggestion, he changed the arched deck keepers' shape to a lower, stronger profile less prone to snagging rope, settling on 4 in high.[11]

## A pendulum self-steering idea

Stephen floated a pendulum-weight self-steering concept: a horizontal shaft fitted aft of the rudder axis, above the waterline, carrying a movable weight that shifts to leeward as the hull heels, offsetting the round-up tendency created as the sail's center of effort moves to leeward of the hull's center of resistance — an idea he traced to a century-old model-sailboat tactic, and one he thought fit naturally with the lead weight already cast into the plans' own rudder blade.[12] Recalling a casting pool at Lincoln Park in Santa Monica where he had sailed model boats, he asked whether the big iron rudder straps seen on some traditional full-size sailboats served a similar self-steering purpose; they turned out to be plank-reinforcement hardware unrelated to self-steering.[13][14] Locking the steering line down while sailing, the normal practice, was pointed out as already negating the pendulum idea's effect; Stephen had not yet fitted his own rudder's lead weight, leaving the idea untested on the water.[15] See [self-steering — a pendulum-weight self-steering idea, and its limits](/operations/self-steering.md#a-pendulum-weight-self-steering-idea-and-its-limits) for the full exchange.

## Building the mast: glued 2x4s and a router-groove wire conduit

Not following the plans precisely, Stephen built his mast from two ordinary construction-grade 2x4s glued together, rough-tapering it by eye with a handsaw to about 1½ in square at the top — slightly fatter than called for. Before gluing up, he router-cut a rounded groove (¼ in deep in each half) running the full length to hold a 0.5 in polyethylene tube, giving a conduit for his 6-gauge solid-copper lightning-ground wire and masthead-light wiring that can be pulled and replaced without reopening the mast. Judging an internal halyard sheave too weak-looking for a small solid mast, and awkward besides with wiring already inside, he fitted an external swivel pulley on a small shoulder near the top instead, with the conduit tube exiting the mast's side just above the foot; Gorilla Glue held the whole assembly together. Full construction detail and the wider technique discussion: [components/rig/mast.md — a glued-2x4 mast with an internal wiring and lightning-ground conduit](/components/rig/mast.md#a-glued-2x4-mast-with-an-internal-wiring-and-lightning-ground-conduit).[16]

Wondering whether a heavier mast would slow the boat's rolling motion, Stephen was assured it would not — long, narrow chine-runner hulls already roll very little regardless of mast weight, borne out both on "Zoë" and, at nearly 40 ft, on the "Hogfish Maximus" sister hull. See [components/rig/mast.md — whether a heavier mast reduces rolling](/components/rig/mast.md#whether-a-heavier-mast-reduces-rolling-no) for the fuller answer.[17]

## Deck framing, ballast, and cabin fit-out, June 2008

Asked for updated photographs,[18] Stephen reported the mast made and fitted into its home along with its own hardware, and the boom and its hardware likewise finished, with work turning next to the deck framing. The cabin's small stove still needed a guard fence to keep pots from sliding off, and the sole locker in the forward stateroom awaited installation, its pieces then being painted. Thin plywood was on hand to panel the cabin's interior sides from the seat frame forward to bulkhead 2, backed by 1-1/2 in insulation foam. For ballast, **375 lb of concrete bricks** — bought at **5 lb and $0.20 apiece**, cheaper than lead or iron and a good fit for the water-tank space — were set aside, not to be installed until launch time. Fiberglassing remained to be done, requiring the hull to be rolled over first; a mold for laminating the cabin and hatch beams had not yet been built, and the deck beams themselves were simply sawn from 2x4 stock, left at 1-1/2 in thick for buoyancy and less work. Stephen remarked that the cabin framing's many angles "look like a headache."[19]

## Fiberglassing before the cabin, a tire-cushioned roll-over, and the rudder blade's lead cast

Stephen settled the fiberglassing-versus-cabin sequencing question for his own build: doing all the glassing before building the cabin, reasoning there would then be no delicate structure bearing the hull's weight while it was rolled over. For the roll-over itself, he planned to cushion the hull on old tires, a technique already proven on an earlier boat of his own. The same update reported a dreaded job finally tackled and finished — melting lead into the rudder blade, found to be easy in practice.[20] The tire-cushioning technique drew independent confirmation from a fellow builder, who had done the same and judged it easy; that builder's own practice of fiberglassing the deck before fitting the cabin — leaving the glass's edge tucked under the cabin structure rather than butting against it, a whole sheet of cloth stretched over the deck and the openings cut out only once the epoxy had started to cure — is recorded in full at [Little Jim — glassing the deck before the cabin, recalled](/boats/little-jim.md#glassing-the-deck-before-the-cabin-recalled-june-2008).[21] Stephen credited [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)'s own construction-photo website with directly shaping how he had sequenced this whole stage of the build.[22] A year later, prompted by a fellow builder's own rollover video, Stephen confirmed the method had worked exactly as hoped — small boat-trailer tires, no steel rims, cushioning the hull's corners with no resulting damage. See [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md — turning the hull over](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#turning-the-hull-over) for the fuller 2009 exchange this confirmation joined.[23]

## Yuloh pivot sockets: machined Delrin, and the hull's fiberglass finished

Fitting the yuloh's pivot, Stephen machined two pockets for plastic sockets directly into the yuloh's shaft, turned from **Delrin** with a 0.750 in dia. ball end mill, each pocket 1 in in diameter and 0.625 in deep, held in place with a single #10 brass flathead screw — brass chosen so it would not scratch the steel ball — with about 0.125 in of plastic wear expected before the screw head is reached. The 0.750 in steel ball itself was drilled with a tungsten-carbide bit to take a 0.375 in dia. stainless steel lag bolt "like the plan," then brazed together and polished, in the hope the high polish would also help retard rusting. The yuloh got its first coat of varnish the same week, by which point the hull's fiberglassing was complete, ready to be turned rightside up the next day with the cabin next on the list; a coming trip to Seattle for Lexan polycarbonate for the windows was expected to test the budget.[24]

## Laminating the cabin and hatch beams, and salvaged hardwood trim

With the hull's fiberglassing finished, Stephen turned to the cabin: built a mold for laminating the cabin and hatch beams and completed a first test lamination, checking its springback the next day.[25] The result came in at only about 1/4 in per end — better than expected. Advised to build the plans' sheet-10 drip rail rather than improvise an alternative (see [window glazing — cabin condensation](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#cabin-condensation-a-drip-rail-a-moulded-gutter-and-window-drain-holes) for the fuller condensation-management exchange this drew), Stephen settled on building it as drawn, and put reserved pieces of teak, mahogany, and an unidentified reddish wood — saved for something special — to use in the cabin's most visible trim around the hatch. The cabin framing's many odd angles proved manageable once taken one step at a time.[26]

## Painting the hull, and searching for a trailer (August 2008)

By August 2008 Stephen had moved on to painting the hull and was shopping for a good trailer, in the middle of reading a recommended self-steering reference (see [self-steering — a recommended self-steering reference, catalogued and freely available](/operations/self-steering.md#a-recommended-self-steering-reference-catalogued-and-freely-available)) but not yet finished with it.[27] The trailer question drew general advice not to skimp on the build, since a trailer that makes single-handed launching easy can be the difference between deciding to go sailing and staying home — see [trailering — single-handed launching as the real reason not to skimp](/operations/trailering.md#single-handed-launching-as-the-real-reason-not-to-skimp) for the fuller cross-link.

## Turn buttons for the sole hatches

Answering a question about the floor panels' turn buttons — the fasteners holding the sole hatches closed over stowed ballast cans — and whether they would survive a knockdown or full inversion,[28] Stephen reported having made two for his own boat, front and rear, and planned to fasten them with bolts rather than screws so a knockdown could not work them loose and spill the ballast stowed beneath.[29]

## Nearing completion: a September 2008 launch expected, and registry congratulations

Asked, in a general "who's still building" check-in from a longtime prospective builder,[30a] whether anyone was actively working on a Paradox, Stephen reported he was just finishing up and expected to launch **that month**, waiting only on a sail kit still to arrive from Sailrite, and pointed the newcomer to the photos section under "Ironsides."[30] The community registrar confirmed Stephen was the last builder added to the UK owners/builders registry and asked to be told once the boat was actually finished so her details could be amended and to hear how her sailing trials went — see [Small Sailboats / Paradox-UK websites — a UK and worldwide build count](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#a-uk-and-worldwide-build-count) for the fuller registry-keeping record this continues.[31] The announcement drew warm, unprompted congratulations: a longtime non-building well-wisher revealed his own life had since ruled out ever building a Paradox and that he had relocated to Nanaimo, BC — see [people/john-gilbert.md](/people/john-gilbert.md) for the fuller update — while a newcomer then mid-build of his own admired Puget Sound as an ideal cruising ground for the design's enclosed cabin, given the region's frequent light drizzle and short sunny season, and predicted "Ironsides" would draw plenty of envious looks; see [people/rodney-cannon.md](/people/rodney-cannon.md) for the fuller exchange.[32][33][34] The original correspondent who had opened the thread, a longtime prospective builder curious about the boat's interior, asked to see photos and hear more about Stephen's own interior modifications once available, being himself a big man who could use the extra room.[35] A further reply from the same correspondent, having weathered Hurricane Ike's much-reduced remnants well inland in the Dallas area, checked in again on whether the boat's launch was still expected that year.[36]

## The sail kit arrives, and the interior nears completion

Days later, the sail kit arrived by UPS from Sailrite. Stephen spent that day making the tack-strop hardware and studying the enclosed sailmaking instructions — the panels themselves cut and marked out by computer and plotter, with a generous allowance of scrap cloth included to practice on first. Windows were next on the list, to be cut and fitted the following day, with the cabin sole to follow; with the weather holding fine, he weighed launching before the sail itself was finished, purely to test the trailer modifications and try out the yuloh.[37] A few days after that, with the interior very nearly together, Stephen gave his own height and weight — **5 ft 11 in and 205 lb** — finding the boat did not feel small to him at all, and promised new photographs once there was something worthwhile to show.[38]

## A ballast correction: concrete bricks, not lead, and a gyrational trade-off

Fresh progress photographs — briefly hit by a hosting glitch that cleared within the hour[39][40] — drew feedback assuming the visible ballast was lead sheeting laid internally on the bottom.[41] It is in fact cast concrete: **79 bricks at 5 lb each**, just under 400 lb total — revising an earlier round estimate of 375 lb — bought at a garden store for about $20 all told, well under lead's going scrap-metal price of roughly $1/lb now that tire shops have stopped giving wheel weights away for environmental reasons. Wood packing and wedges were still to be cut to stop the bricks shifting, with the cabin sole to be screwed down firmly over the top to secure them for good. Concrete was judged to share about the same center of gravity as the plans' own lead ballast, but not the same **gyrational inertia** — a lighter, less concentrated mass expected to roll the boat faster than lead would, a trade-off accepted for now, with 150 lb of lead billets salvaged from an earlier boat project kept in reserve to shift later for trim. See [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast material choice](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives) for the wider lead-versus-concrete comparison this adds a real-world data point to.[42]

## Engine-avoidance by tide-planning, and a minimalist approach to power and stowage

Rather than plan around an engine, the intention became to plan instead around Puget Sound's own **12 ft tides** and its well-charted currents — a discipline observed elsewhere on the list and adopted as a personal goal — with a 2 hp Honda kept aboard strictly as backup.[42] The side-mounted motor-and-fuel sponson idea floated and set aside earlier in the build (see [auxiliary outboard power — a side-mounted motor-and-fuel sponson considered and rejected](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-side-mounted-motor-and-fuel-sponson-considered-and-rejected-a-three-position-transom-bracket)) was restated as still worth having for the rare trip demanding heavy motoring and little sailing — Oregon's Willamette River or Lake Powell in Arizona were given as examples — without meaning to fit it permanently.

General storage follows the same minimalist bent: plenty of room remains throughout the boat, with cloth side bins still planned as an addition only if they actually prove necessary, matching the plan set at the very start of the build[43] — the boat has no built-in lower bins of the kind some other Paradoxes carry for stowing heavy items like bottled drinks, flares, and a nautical almanac.[41] Keeping gear to a minimum generally — an MP3 player rather than a CD player, the same discipline as backpacking, hiking, or kayaking — extends to power: a large 12-volt wet-cell battery of the kind the plans specify was ruled out in favor of small, easily replaced batteries for a single LED masthead light, judging 200 amp-hours excessive to store aboard so small and combustible a hull; no solar panel was planned either.[42]

## The tack strop, untested, and a corrected understanding of luff tension

Having never used a tack strop and going only by the drawings and other builders' photographs, the fitting was privately doubted as possibly "too clever" for its own good — an assumption corrected once questioned directly.[42] Luff tension is actually held by the **furling line's own tension**, once the halyard itself is belayed, not by the tack strop, which only fixes the boom's height at the mast — Matt Layden's own instruction for it being simply to "make fast to cleat and forget it."[44][45] A further critique — that the strop, as built, carries no separate adjustment or downhaul running through it into the cabin, unlike the original plans' own drawing — was answered the same way: no separate luff-tensioning mechanism is needed once the furling line already carries that job.[46][44] See [tack strop — what the tack strop does, and doesn't do](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#what-the-tack-strop-does-and-doesnt-do-luff-tension-comes-from-the-furling-line) for the design clarification in full.

Feedback on the motor mounting drew on outside experience mounting a similar side motor on a canoe: an off-center thrust line resisted turning toward the side opposite the motor much more than toward it, a problem chine runners might make worse still — reinforcing the case, already under consideration, for keeping any auxiliary motor near the centerline for steerage.[46] A dedicated fuel container sized to the tank's own capacity, fitted with a long spout and an automatic shutoff, was suggested as a way to avoid spillage regardless of mounting position.[46] See [auxiliary outboard power — a second canoe account](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-second-canoe-account-off-center-thrust-and-a-fuel-container-safety-idea) for the fuller cross-craft comparison.

## Outside feedback: ballast leverage, storage bins, and a motor-mounting depth caution

Feedback on the progress photographs reinforced two established design points and added a caution for the side-mounted-motor question still under consideration. Moving individual lead pigs to trim the boat is a genuinely valuable feature, and windward ballast set up on the leeward chine gives real leverage once the boat heels onto that chine and the chine runner bites, matching the windward-ballast-leverage principle already established for the design generally (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered)). Built-in **lower bins**, of the kind this boat was built without (see [above](#engine-avoidance-by-tide-planning-and-a-minimalist-approach-to-power-and-stowage)), drew a counterpoint from another owner who finds them genuinely useful, kept full of items wanted within easy reach of the helm — binoculars, sun cream, snacks, even a rubbish bin.[47] Separately, firsthand experience with a similarly side-mounted electric motor on another Paradox warned that the **depth of the propeller and exhaust** matters more to a side mount than its exact position: leaning the boat to start the motor can push the exhaust down far enough to make starting difficult, while a mounting shallow enough to start easily risks lifting the propeller clear of the water on the opposite lean. See [auxiliary outboard power — a second canoe account](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-second-canoe-account-off-center-thrust-and-a-fuel-container-safety-idea) for the fuller cross-craft comparison this continues.[48]

## A tack-strop pin rework, and a final push toward launch

The tack-strop's original cotter-pinned pins had begun acting like fish-hooks, snagging the rope rigged for reefing; a rework using fewer pieces and **snap-ring** pins fixed both that snagging problem and an overlong-pin issue the earlier design carried (see [tack strop — a fastening caution for new hardware, and a design compliment](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#a-fastening-caution-for-new-hardware-and-a-design-compliment) for the fuller hardware record). The cabin sole was repainted from gray to tan and installed; foam insulation was fitted between the hull sides and screwed-down varnished interior liner panels, a snug, non-moving "floating" fit that leaves bulkhead 3 with no intrusion into the cabin's look or feel. An aluminum bracket was made to hold the mainsheet cam cleat, and a deck-block to hold the yuloh's forward end; the tiller was fitted with its blade-lifter jam cleat and fairlead (see [rudder and chine experiments — tiller construction](/components/rudder.md#tiller-construction-laminated-wood-versus-bent-metal-tube)); side-deck arches were mounted to hold the sail and yuloh in their stowed positions, and three rope fairleads were made from Delrin and fitted in their holes. New photographs went up: the end was in sight.[49] The update drew warm encouragement from an established UK builder,[50] and a thank-you in return, along with a reciprocal question — how that builder's own boat had held up after two seasons, and what, if anything, he would change building her again — answered in full at [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md#a-two-season-retrospective-antifouling-a-condensation-fix-confirmed-and-nothing-he-would-change).[51]

## Congratulations from a retired schooner mate, and a prospective Montana build

The same thread then drew a further, unprompted congratulatory reply: a retired First Mate and Relief Captain of the Puget Sound schooner **Zodiac** — a 160 ft vessel drawing 16 ft, home-ported in Seattle with a Bellingham spring/summer/fall base — who found the ship's size kept him from the small coves and beaches he wished he could explore along Puget Sound, the San Juans, and the Canadian Gulf Islands, admired Ironsides' construction photographs and metalwork, and intends to build his own Paradox, perhaps the extended version, once his own shop is finished at his home in the woods some seventeen miles west of Kalispell, Montana.[52][53][54] Told the location, Steve's one-word verdict was "Heaven."[55]

## Sailmaking progress, and a bulkhead-3 floorplan diagram

Sewing the arrived Sailrite kit sail continued: managing each long seam through the machine went by rolling the cloth around tubes and securing it with tape, heavy and clumsy work otherwise; the cloth resisted the needle enough that the machine needed to be run fast to keep the motor and flywheel's momentum up, with extra cloth tape edging built into the luff to resist the tension concentrated there. Sailrite's own YouTube instructional videos proved a useful resource throughout.[56] See [Sailmaking — ordering a Sailrite kit](/construction/sailmaking.md#ordering-a-sailrite-kit-contents-and-process-2008) for the fuller technique. A diagram illustrating the clear floor space gained by [the widened bulkhead 3](#a-widened-steel-reinforced-bulkhead-3) — captioned "increased room for sleeping" — was uploaded to the group's Files section and, after some discussion of exactly where a copy should also live, given a place on the group's front page alongside Ironsides' frame photos for one comprehensive view of the build.[57][58][59] Assembly of the sail itself then turned up a genuine fit problem: the head and tack sleeves as cut by Sailrite ran too large, the head sleeve especially, calling for a fix before the sail could be finished — a setback that drew sympathy from a fellow builder.[59][60]

## A grommet/yard mismatch resolved, and the sail's first hoist

Stepping the mast and raising the finished sail for the first time exposed a fit problem distinct from the oversized sleeves noted above: the sail's corner grommets, and the depth of its sleeves as sewn to Sailrite's own dimensions, put the sail out of alignment with the yard cut to the plans. Rather than unpick and re-sew the sleeves, the fix was to lengthen the yard itself, to 114 in (2900 mm), which corrected the lashing's lead angle from the holes in the yard to the sail's grommets. Given no prior experience sewing a sail — or much of anything, by his own account — the seams and draft still looked surprisingly good once up; genuine wind, not just a first hoist at the dock, will be the real test of it. The corner grommets themselves are aluminum, set with six rivets, supplied ready-fitted as part of the kit. A fellow builder, seeing the new photo posted of the raised sail, recognized the same grommet mismatch from his own sail — suggesting it may simply be how the plan's yard and a Sailrite-cut sail line up rather than a one-off kit fault — and had solved it differently, by adding two extra grommets partway down the sleeve's side. See [sailmaking — a grommet/yard lead-angle mismatch, and two independent fixes](/construction/sailmaking.md#a-grommetyard-lead-angle-mismatch-and-two-independent-fixes) for the fuller technique.[61][62]

Weighing the two fixes, Stephen stuck with the longer yard rather than adopt the extra-grommet alternative, trusting a commercial sail designer's conservative approach to reinforcement — the sleeve itself carries no reinforcement patches — over cutting into a finished sail on his own judgment; the outcome remains unproven pending a sail actually worked hard in wind. The longer yard proved noticeably heavier than the original and hard to raise, especially over the last stretch near the top, and a separate discovery from the same first rigging trial was that the hatch needs to slide back a little to let the boom lie down flat on the deck once lowered. Raising the further question of which way the sail should roll up on the boom, the stock plans — Sheet 9 — call for the sail and furling line to come to the port (outside) side of the boom, a convention confirmed working without trouble; see [furling system — a tacking-direction asymmetry with the furl direction](/components/rig/furling.md#a-tacking-direction-asymmetry-with-the-furl-direction) for the fuller cross-boat record on furl direction.[63]

## The yuloh's bend: springback, and a guessed compensation

Building his own yuloh alongside the sail, Stephen laminated its two layers over a bend clamped in from the pivot area to each end, working from a guessed deflection of about 60 mm to allow for the wood's own springback once released from the clamps — a springback that proved greater than expected, leaving less than 30 mm of actual curve, closer to 20 mm, once the glue had cured. Whether that shortfall matters would only be answered once the boat was launched and the yuloh actually tried. See [yuloh — building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket) for the fuller record of the different lamination techniques builders have used to hit the plans' 30 mm figure.[64]

## The launch, 15 October 2008

Ironsides launched on Puget Sound on 15 October 2008, two years and three months after construction began. Ballast aboard for the launch was the full 395 lb of cast concrete described above — Stephen judged it might not prove enough in a stronger breeze, but adequate for the wind actually on hand that day; the boat "did everything a good sailboat should do," tacking cleanly all the way around on each pass, with no sign of trouble from the lighter, gyrationally different concrete substitution (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast material choice](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives) for the fuller lead-versus-concrete comparison this launch confirms in practice). The trailer performed well launching and retrieving. Handling the roller reefing and topping lift still needed practice, and only a brief, few-minutes' try of the yuloh was made — too short a trial to settle whether the shortfall in its lamination bend (20 mm actually achieved against the 30 mm aimed for, [above](#the-yulohs-bend-springback-and-a-guessed-compensation)) makes any real difference; that question stays open pending a longer trial (see [yuloh — building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket)).

What struck Stephen most was **not having to hold the tiller continuously** — long stretches went by without touching the helm at all, despite the tiller rope's own friction being looser than expected — a first-water confirmation, on this specific hull, of the tiller-rope-friction self-steering already documented as typical of the design generally (see [self-steering — light-air self-steering via tiller-rope friction](/operations/self-steering.md#light-air-self-steering-via-tiller-rope-friction)). Sailing continued for about an hour in a light chop, the hull making a "nice gurgling sound," before dark clouds rolled in and the wind fell away to nothing. Retrieval onto the trailer went smoothly; the one thing flagged as needing a better fix afterward was the rigging of the boat's own fenders, wanting some small eyes and hooks rather than the arrangement used that day. Stephen judged the day a success and posted photographs to the group's gallery under "Ironsides."[65]

## Congratulations, and the tack strop's first, still-unproven test

## Sourcing ballast on a budget: concrete bricks against scarce, pricier lead

Church reported his own ballast search going less smoothly: after collecting only about 50 lb of lead so far, he found tire shops switching to steel wheel weights for environmental reasons and no longer willing to give up their old lead stock, itself fetching upward of $1 a pound where available — "far from what an old retired boat builder guy can afford." He was weighing pouring cement/scrap-steel ballast blocks instead, the method described in George Buehler's *Backyard Boatbuilding*, and in the meantime was picking up stray wheel weights left lying in tire-store parking lots.[91] Answering with his own solution, Stephen described buying **80 concrete bricks at Lowe's for 25 cents each**, 5 lb apiece — **400 lb of ballast for $20**, enough to get sailing right away — with wooden blocks and wedges made to keep the bricks from shifting, and a plan to reuse them elsewhere in the yard later if lead and money ever came together (see [design/weight-and-ballast.md — ballast material choice](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives) for the fuller cost/density comparison this restates). He judged Buehler's cast-in-place method might prove harder to move or remove later than loose, reusable bricks.[92]

The same thread drew two further ballast replies, and a further round of congratulations. Answering Ron Church directly, [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) suggested that if ballast is going to cost real money anyway, it may as well buy something with a second, heavier purpose — a dismantleable storm anchor of the kind Little Cruiser carries, or a good battery — rather than plain lead bought for no other reason; he reported his own ballast down further still, to about 36 kg (75 lb) of lead, after weighing "Little Jim" afresh with a folding bicycle now the last big addition of gear (see [people/alastair-law.md — sailing performance and weight](/people/alastair-law.md#sailing-performance-and-weight) for the fuller figure, set against an earlier, higher figure left unreconciled against it).[93] Further congratulations reached Stephen the same day from Jeff UK; the thanks that followed drew Jeff into an extended reply proposing to power the boat from inside the cabin altogether — a tangent settled, once more, in favor of the yuloh over a Hobie Mirage Drive, and carried in full at [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md — pedal power, and why a Mirage Drive does not suit Paradox](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#pedal-power-and-why-a-mirage-drive-does-not-suit-paradox) as a general propulsion question rather than one about Ironsides herself.[94] Separately, [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md) answered Ron's own ballast question with the alternative settled on for "Raffles": steel weights of the kind used for weight training, avoiding lead altogether to keep it away from his young son, at some cost in stowage space but with the advantage that such weights come in a range of sizes, easily rearranged and re-secured for trim; he also invited Ron to visit and see "Raffles" in person.[95] The Mirage Drive tangent itself continued a day further, with a misattribution corrected and a wistful regret at missing the chance to buy one, before Stephen's own inside-the-cabin daydream grew into its own "Silly Gadgets" thread proposing a home-built pedal-powered outboard for Ironsides — full exchange, including the alternatives it drew, at [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md — a pedal-drive outboard conceived for Ironsides, and the alternatives it drew](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-pedal-drive-outboard-conceived-for-ironsides-and-the-alternatives-it-drew).[96]

## A cabin-top window-fairing lesson, learned the hard way

Fairing Ironsides' own cabin-top forward beam and window with a straight edge, plane, and belt sander looked right by eye, but screwing the polycarbonate down afterward showed buckles and bulges rather than a true fit — a mismatch only the glazing itself would have caught if used as its own fairing tool from the start; the lesson passed on to a fellow builder wrestling with the same beam was to dry-fit the glazing, screwed down without glue, before committing to the shape. See [deck and cabin beam lamination — bending polycarbonate glazing around the forward and aft window curves](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#bending-polycarbonate-glazing-around-the-forward-and-aft-window-curves).[100]

## A rudder pin design, built into the stock's lamination

Answering a fellow builder's account of a rudder-stock pintle repair (see [Little Jim — a rudder-stock pintle repair](/boats/little-jim.md#a-rudder-stock-pintle-repair-and-the-folding-bikes-first-cruising-season)), Stephen described a different way of fitting the pins in his own rudder stock: **1/2 in stainless steel pins**, bent to shape with an oxy-acetylene torch, set into grooves cut into each plywood half of the stock before the halves were glued together, with a hole through the upper pin for a large cotter pin to keep the stock from falling out of place — thickened epoxy in the grooves, wet out with the rest of the lamination in the normal way. Cheaper than the plans' own fitting, though Stephen had not yet had much time in the boat, so judged it unproven.[103]

Restating the vent-trunk drain design already built into "Ironsides" (see [above](#building-the-vent-trunk-drain)) for the same newcomer, Stephen added two details not previously on record: the portion of the copper drain tube standing in the water must be left bare — no paint, no fiberglass — to make the electrical connection, and that connection to the mast itself is made at the tube's upper end, where a tab or finger runs across the mast-step pocket for the mast's own foot to stand on. Full record: [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md#a-2009-drain-routing-question-and-a-mast-step-electrical-contact-detail).[104]

## A first cruise: four days in south Puget Sound (July 2009)

Ironsides' first cruise since her October 2008 launch was a four-day trip to south Puget Sound. Anchoring in calm, settled weather predicted throughout, Stephen tried something he had long wanted to do: tie the anchor line straight onto the anchor's shackle, with no chain at all. South Sound's bottom there is nearly all sand and mud, forgiving ground for the experiment. He carried four 100 ft anchor lines and five anchors in all — a light Danforth, used exclusively while the weather stayed calm, plus four heavier castings of a modern ship's anchor (two 15 lb, one 20 lb, one 30 lb) that double as ballast. In the calm, he simply left the Danforth lying on the back deck to dry out; on the last, choppier day of white caps, the Danforth and its line went into the lower aft bin by the transom instead. He reserves chain for genuinely troublesome anchoring, judging wet chain through the boat unpleasant to handle otherwise.

Engine trouble forced him onto the yuloh, and gave him a direct comparison between the plans' standard pivot point and the handle-ward-relocated one [Al Law](/people/alastair-law.md) has described on the list: Al's felt noticeably stiffer, "like overdrive," where the standard position sculled relaxed and gentle — see [yuloh — a firsthand gearing comparison, forced by engine trouble](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket) for the fuller technical context this confirms.[105] The boat's speed under sail struck him most: she ghosted along in winds too light to ripple the water, and gave wonderful bursts at hull speed going to windward — "I'm stunned. What a wonderful boat." He made a bracket to hold the GPS, and used marine weather forecasts throughout the trip to judge what chances were worth taking. The new pipe-elbow vent scoop went untested — the evenings cooled naturally each time he anchored, so it was never needed.

Replying, Al Law was glad to see him "getting some quality time in the boat after all that building."[106]

Reflecting further afterward, Stephen confirmed the outboard trouble had been nothing worse than a dirty spark plug, and drew up a fresh punch list from the trip: a lock-clamp for the steering line, a better grip for the sole hatches, a better wind indicator at the masthead, and — the trip's most valued improvised fix — a small wooden clamp holding an umbrella overhead, "a godsend in the high heat of the day."[107] Al Law found his own umbrella clamp just as valuable, though "it has little to do with the sun" in his own climate, consistent with the sun-and-rain fix already established on [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md#an-umbrella-instead-of-a-topping-lift-awning); for the sole-hatch grip problem, his own simpler fix on Little Jim is a couple of 1-inch holes drilled straight through the hatch boards, rather than the [turn buttons](#turn-buttons-for-the-sole-hatches) fitted above.[108]

## A bioluminescent night at anchor

Anchored one summer night near the Tacoma Narrows south of Gig Harbor, Stephen found the water glowing after dark: a warm-season bloom of bioluminescent microorganisms, agitated by a good tidal current running past the hull. Leaning over the cabin side and rocking the boat, he found the chine runners churned the water into a fine whorl of glowing blue snowflakes streaming away aft — "you really need to see it. Just Magic!"[109] The display is a distinct phenomenon from "red tide," a bloom of dinoflagellates that makes shellfish poisonous; in some regions the microorganisms responsible for a bioluminescent glow are instead harmless comb jellies, as beautiful by daylight as after dark.[110] The sight recalled two much earlier memories of Stephen's own: watching waves glow ashore at a Santa Monica beach as a small boy, and, later, riding with his father in a 16 ft runabout at speed, watching the bow's spray glow and fish dart away from the hull in streaks of light beneath the surface.[111]

## An outboard-mainsheet tangle, and a line-fender idea

The same week, Stephen reported a "horrible" problem of his own — Ironsides' mainsheet tangling with the outboard motor — sometimes leaving the motor down while sailing just to keep the rope free. A day later he sketched a possible fix: a metal rod, brass or stainless, fastened onto the motor itself as a kind of "line fender," meant to make the tangles a little less complicated — untried at the time, pending a way to fit it without drilling too many holes into the motor. The exchange this drew ranged well beyond Ironsides, taking in the same problem on "[Auspice](/boats/auspice.md)" and "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," a recollection of an earlier deck-rerouted fix, and photographs showing "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"'s own sheet built to plan with only a small addition of piping — see [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md — a 2009 recurrence across four boats, and two new fix ideas](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-2009-recurrence-across-four-boats-and-two-new-fix-ideas) for the full record.[112][113] The same day, Stephen floated a further idea of his own — a junk rig's doubled mainsheet, one line to each side of the boom, illustrated in a reference book on the rig — wondering whether it might solve the tangle in a different way; the reply cautioned the sheet would still need setting well aft regardless, to keep the sail's centre of pressure correctly placed.[114][115]

## Losing the yuloh in the current, and the seat as an improvised paddle

Sculling near Gig Harbor one summer with the outboard not working, Stephen was distracted for a moment and lost his grip on the yuloh, which began drifting slowly away on the current.
