# The Edge

**"The Edge"** is the [Paradox](/paradox.md) built and sailed by [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md) — one of the first two Paradoxes known to have sailed, heavily built to Matt Layden's plans, and the source of the archive's most detailed lead-ballast installation technique.[1][2][3]

# Record

- By **July 2001**, Don Elliott's was one of only two Paradoxes known to be sailing, the other being Matt Layden's own prototype (see [original Paradox](original-paradox.md)).[1]
- The boat is **heavily built**, to Matt's plans.[2] Don Elliott became a primary technical resource on the list, authoring the ["Building the Paradox" articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) (including a coach-roof jig) and advocating building strictly to plan (see [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md)).[1][2]
- Its **lead ballast** is installed in hardwood blocks (not oak) epoxy-bonded to the hull and tapped for threaded fastenings after bonding; forward trim lead counterbalances the battery, strapped down with nylon straps; all lead is sealed in plastic with rubber padding.[3] See [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for the full technique.
- Loaded, she carries a full weight breakdown of about **1,365 lb**: roughly **930 lb** sits in the bottom 10 inches (400 lb of lead as ten 40 lb pigs, plus the water tanks, food, battery and ground tackle), with the rest above the floor; her waterplane sinks about 1 inch for every 200 lb added.[4] See [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for the general figures this hull's own numbers match.
- Don Elliott is believed, though not confirmed, to live in Wisconsin — the only location given anywhere in the archive for him.[5] See [Southern California sailing and the Catalina Island crossing question](/operations/southern-california-and-catalina-crossing.md).

# The name

The boat went formally unnamed on the list for roughly two years, referred to only as "Elliott's Paradox."[6] In November 2003 a UK builder posted launch photographs of her on his own website at a URL ending `edge.htm`, from which the name **"The Edge"** was inferred; the same builder then confirmed the name was correct, remarking that it is "quite appropriate" for how a Paradox can feel in a real rough sea, beating to windward or surfing downwind on large breakers.[7][8][9] Don Elliott himself is never recorded confirming it. The name was still in independent use as the boat's settled name eleven years later, in the [2014 builders' and owners' census](/references/builders-and-owners-census-2014.md).[10]

# Offered for sale (2017)

In late April 2017 "The Edge" was offered for sale on the mailing list — "a unique opportunity for some lucky sailor to own one of the best examples of this boat design to date" — with interested buyers directed to Wanda Elliott.[11] Fuller details followed a week later: the boat was in overall sound shape, needing only minor attention to the starboard toe rail and the rudder, with a fresh coat of paint inside and out recommended to make her look new again; the asking price was **$2,800 OBO**, including a trailer and some cruising gear such as anchors.[12] A prospective buyer asked where the boat was located; the reply confirmed **Tomah, Wisconsin** — Don Elliott's own longtime home.[13][14] The listing drew a reaction wondering how many years she had been sitting unused, hoping someone would soon get her back out on the water.[15] Within about two weeks of the original listing she found a buyer: the mailing list announced she had "found a new home," with the new owner looking forward to bringing her back into tip-top shape to go sailing.[16] See below for who that was, and the restoration that followed.

# Sold to Deric, and restoration begins (2017)

The new owner introduced himself to the mailing list as **[Deric](/people/frederic-tallman.md)**, of **New York State** — a longtime lurker who had bought Don's plans about five years earlier, then set the project aside to keep sailing an existing Herreshoff-designed cat ketch instead. He bought the boat directly from **Wanda and Josh Elliott**, Don's widow and son, and towed her home across five states from Wisconsin, the two families sharing a meal together at the handover; Wanda continued selling the Paradox building guide Don had developed. Deric recalled Don as an accomplished, prolific builder well beyond Paradox — a catamaran, a houseboat, and other boats, plus a wooden kayak Josh kept, alongside an interest in foiling boats, cartoons, and further guides and articles.[17] He repainted the mast Don had built when he first took the boat over, and — asked years later, in 2021, for mast-building advice by a newer builder — pointed to the mast's own soundness ever since as proof of how well Don had built it originally, recommending Don's own building-manual method for anyone cutting a mast of their own.[18] The mailing list's own registry was updated to record the new ownership.[19]

The boat came without a **boom or sail**, so restoration began there: Deric sought a builder to make a new boom, reconditioning the boat that fall and winter toward a **spring 2018** relaunch, at a stage in life where he preferred to get back on the water rather than develop the woodworking skill to build the boom himself.[20] Inspection turned up fiberglass **"zippering"** cracking (see [fiberglassing technique](/construction/fiberglassing-technique.md)) at the rudder's edge and the swim step, with rot in the block the rudder attaches to, needing the wood replaced or filled with epoxy and glass.[21] The original gudgeon itself, per Deric's own later account, was fastened with a sealant and bolts rather than epoxy — Don Elliott's own choice, and the technique recommended on its strength when another builder later asked the same question; see [construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md — fastening the finished gudgeon, not casting it](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md#a-may-2019-recurrence-fastening-the-finished-gudgeon-not-casting-it).[22] Progress continued into the fall: the fiberglass work needed little more, a wood source for the boom was still being researched, and he asked for detailed photos of a finished yuloh build — the plans alone were not detailed enough to visualize the part — hoping to hire local woodworkers for both jobs, with a realistic target of sailing by the following spring.[23] By late July a **Sailrite** kit quote for the original sail was in hand (a straight-stitch machine needs three passes where the instructions call for two of zig-zag), and a local woodworking-club member had turned up an old 14 ft round boom blank he believed he could reshape.[24] Weighing that stitch choice further, Deric put the straight-versus-zigzag question to the mailing list directly, drawing detailed technique from a professional sailmaker and several experienced home-sewers; see [construction/sailmaking.md — straight stitch versus zigzag](/construction/sailmaking.md#straight-stitch-versus-zigzag-needle-size-stitch-length-and-a-cheap-machine-that-worked-2017).[25]

A further restoration question drew answers from two established builders. The yard's end holes are not, as first guessed, for securing the yard against the boom when the sail is down — they carry short lines that pull the sail's own head tight — and a technique for drilling them without inviting rot, plus two ways of attaching the halyard without a pad eye, are covered in full at [components/rig/index.md — yard-end holes and halyard attachment](/components/rig/index.md#yard-end-holes-purpose-and-a-rot-prevention-technique-for-drilling-them).[26][27][28] The advice proved out in practice: within about a week the yard itself was nearing completion.[29]

## The sail ordered, and a launch checklist (June 2018)

The straight-versus-zigzag sail question above was eventually settled by going professional rather than sewing at home: a **Quantum Sails** loft in **Rochester, NY** (owner **Kris Werner**) was commissioned to cut the sail to the original plan dimensions in **6 oz cloth**, once too many other boat projects made the DIY route impractical.[30] By June 2018 the remaining punch list toward a first sail read: paint the boom; sand and paint the rudder block and the rudder; finish fairing, then prime and paint the cabin hatch; prime and paint the cabin rails; reassemble everything; bend on the new sail and test the running rigging; and float the boat to see what happens — worked on alongside a Gorfnik build and a separate cat-ketch re-gelcoating project running in parallel.[31]

## The boom and yuloh finished, and the sail arrives (August 2018)

By August 2018 the rudder and rudder block had been reconditioned, the boom and yuloh fabricated with a friend's help shaping the yuloh, and the new sail arrived that same week. Rigging the boat for the first time turned up the same furling/halyard difficulty widely reported by other builders, resolved by adopting the half-topping-lift fix already worked out on Iota and Pequeno — full technique at [components/rig/furling.md — a 2018 postscript](/components/rig/furling.md#a-2018-postscript-the-fix-confirmed-again-and-two-further-techniques). Sanding, priming, and painting the rest of the boat remained.[32][33]

The finished yuloh was tested in the marina days later, with satisfying results: the pivot-socket position was first estimated by standing in the water in sculling posture, then checked against the plans and found within three inches of the specified location. The stroke itself took a moment to learn — favoring the blade's lower face pushed no water at all — but switching to favor its topside sent the boat moving at once, to the applause of onlookers who had gathered at the marina to look at the boat and a paddle they had never seen before. Full construction and technique detail: [components/yuloh.md — building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket).[34]

Days later, having tested the yuloh with the rudder down, planned a further test that weekend with the rudder up: sealed the inside of the pivot socket with epoxy and fitted a length of rubber hose inside it per a fellow owner's suggestion (see [components/yuloh.md — a rubber-hose pivot fix](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket)), and planned to add weight to the boat and fill the water tanks to get a better sense of her motion when loaded. A YouTube video watched the same week, believed to show Dave Bolduc demonstrating yuloh technique with a U-shaped oarlock, showed master control of the boat at any speed as "second nature." Once the boat could be moved from the boat launch to the lake, a sailing test — the first tack and turn of a keel-less boat under this sail design — was expected to follow, and eagerly anticipated.[35]

## The first sail, and the water tanks in service (August 2018)

The anticipated sailing test came days later: the boat was sailed for the first time that weekend, an outing that taught a great deal about her all at once. Ahead of the sail, the water tanks were filled from a marina spigot — straightforward with a garden hose once the four-inch inspection ports were removed, though filling from sea water directly remained more of a challenge at this stage. Don Elliott had fitted four-inch inspection plates to each tank and, by his own account, could reach every corner of a tank through one with his own arm; the boat's new owner found the same four-inch opening too small for his own reach, and judged a five- or six-inch plate would suit a larger builder better (see [construction/transom-deck-plate.md — water-tank deck plates](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#water-tank-deck-plates-a-more-serious-leak-and-glen-maxwells-dowel-rod-fix) for the same enlargement already recommended elsewhere). Don had also fitted a clear hose to each tank; since the deck carries no dedicated filler opening and the toe rail is deliberately split to shed water rather than direct it to one, the hoses were judged likely meant for filling and draining via an adapter and a pump rather than for rainwater catchment. Draining after the sail used a Rule centrifugal pump to remove about 80 percent of the water, with the remainder squeezed out by hand with a rag into a bucket — a slow process expected to go faster with a siphon fitted to the filler hoses, and a proper pump for filling them in turn.[36]

A companion account of that same outing, titled "Being on the Edge," fills in the sail itself: sculled out of the marina on the yuloh, drawing courteous slow-downs from several large motor yachts sharing the marina; once clear, raised sail on a starboard tack, per André-François Bourbeau's own advice that furling and unfurling comes easier that way, and sailed on in a light, sub-10-knot breeze, reefing and changing sail size on every point to feel out how she responded from different positions in the cabin. About ninety minutes out the wind gusted toward 15 knots; she heeled and her chine runner gripped the water to hold steady on point through the building chop, handling like a larger, deep-keeled boat rather than the small craft she is. Closing the hatch and sitting on the cabin floor to feel what sailing without a visible helmsman was like, "The Edge" — after several years quietly waiting in a field — was judged to finally have a life of her own again, running free "in her natural habitat."[37]

## Yuloh hand position and body stance, and a next test (August 2018)

Testing continued days later: moving the hand's grip position along the yuloh's handle, and varying body stance between facing forward, 45 and 90 degrees off center, and standing centered or off to one side, turned up sweet spots for a sustained stroke without the blade popping off its pivot ball as often. Full technique detail, including a next planned test adding a further handle-ward socket: [components/yuloh.md — hand position on the handle, and body stance while sculling](/components/yuloh.md#hand-position-on-the-handle-and-body-stance-while-sculling-2018).[38]

## A further yuloh socket, and open-water testing on the Erie Canal (September 2018)

A second yuloh socket, bored about eight inches closer to the handle than the first, proved a marked improvement days later: the oar no longer popped off the pivot pin's ball head, delivered more power per stroke, and steered better. Yulohed a half mile through a navigation channel and along a stretch of the Erie Canal waterway in New York State, drawing a run of curious questions from onlookers along the docks, and worked the boat in to tie up between two large motor yachts under yuloh power alone. Yulohing out again the next day with a slight current confirmed it works better moving with the current than against it; attempting to yuloh-sail in a later calm — working the oar while under sail rather than becalmed — gave no measurable increase in boat speed, though the sail's shade was itself welcome. Full technique, including the further handle-ward "overdrive" socket this confirmed: [components/yuloh.md — hand position on the handle, and body stance while sculling](/components/yuloh.md#hand-position-on-the-handle-and-body-stance-while-sculling-2018).[39]

## A leak found in the water tanks (September 2018)

Preparing for a further sail weeks later turned up a leak: the dry storage space under the floor, dry the previous weekend, was found wet after the tanks had been filled and shown to a visitor. The plan was to dry the boat out on the trailer, then fill one tank at a time and watch for water outside it to localize the leak — suspecting the joint where the fill/suction pipe enters the tank, either a bad hose clamp or a deteriorated hose, with enlarging the 4 in deck plates to reseal the tanks kept as a worst-case fallback.[40] That plan was refined a few days later into a specific test: filling each tank in 25 percent increments, first below and then above the fill/suction pipe, to see exactly where water first showed.[41] The group's advice pointed first to the inspection hatches themselves rather than the plumbing: a well-established builder cautioned that his own hatches had needed replacing three times before he found ones that were genuinely watertight (see [construction/transom-deck-plate.md — a 2018 leak, and further builders' fill/drain routines](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#a-2018-leak-and-further-builders-filldrain-routines) for the fuller hatch-leak history this confirms).[42]

The leak was traced days later: filling the starboard tank a gallon at a time on an inclined trailer, water began seeping through the weep hole into the dry storage space after the seventh gallon; a mirror showed the seep coming from the joint between the filler tube and the tank's own inlet pipe, held by a single hose clamp, and replacing the tube while adding a second clamp stopped it for good — a small burr felt on the metal fitting, suspected to be hardened epoxy rather than a manufacturing defect, sanded away easily.[43]

## A near-miss under sail, and a cold-water squall recalled (September 2018)

A second sail, on Oneida Lake — the owner's home water, the largest lake entirely within New York State's own borders, and part of the canal system linking the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and, beyond it, the Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway route south to Florida and the Caribbean — tested how far the boat's small size could be pushed. Standing up from the bench seat and leaning against the cabin side while running downwind at 2 knots proved reassuring: nothing about it threatened to capsize her, a genuine benefit of the design not often remarked on, though standing blocked the forward view entirely behind the large sail, deciding the question against a plastic sail window as more trouble than it was worth. Freeboard is low enough that reaching the dock at the end of the two-hour sail meant climbing up from the boat rather than stepping off her the way a larger boat's crew would. The following day, sailing back at first light with no wind, yulohing let her be worked in close along the shoreline out of curiosity how close to shore she could go without touching bottom — grounding being, in any case, standard practice with every boat previously owned — and about half an hour in, the lake bed became visible through some twenty inches of sunlit water, close enough to watch small fish moving among the weed.[44]

A storm forecast for early afternoon left time to get off the lake first, informed by an earlier squall encountered six years before, aboard a 33 ft Cat Ketch on the same lake: wind rose from 9 to over 35 knots in under a minute, the temperature dropped 15 degrees in less time still, and rain turned to hail hard enough to redden bare forearms, the whole squall passing in about seven minutes. Reasoned through in hindsight, a Paradox caught in the same conditions could close her hatch and reef ahead of time, sheltered from the hail entirely, and her shoal draft meant any stretch of shoreline offered a workable refuge rather than needing to make a specific harbour.[44]

Beating home close-hauled on a freshening 13-18 knot southeasterly, digging her chine runner in and holding a hard bearing down, two other sailboats converged nearby — one to starboard, one crossing ahead to port on the opposite tack — close enough that, once a sudden gust forced easing the sheet, the line got away and the wind took the sail uncontrolled: it snapped across to the front of the mast and rode up, catching aloft with the yard on the wrong side of the mast entirely — a standing lug's characteristic "Chinese gybe" — leaving her unable to reef until it was cleared. The nearer boat, under a locally known and experienced racing skipper, held clear in good time, and once the immediate danger had passed the tangled sail was worked in by feathering close to the wind and pinching toward shallow water near the marina, declining an offered tow. A friend waiting ashore helped free the sail once she reached calm water in the marina's lee: releasing the furling line let the sail slide down unloaded, and between them they drew it free of the yard, re-threaded it on the correct side of the mast, and re-hoisted it, finding the cloth undamaged by the whipping it had taken — vindication for having specified the heavier 6 oz Dacron over the standard 4 oz when the sail was commissioned (see [the sail ordered, and a launch checklist](#the-sail-ordered-and-a-launch-checklist-june-2018) above).[44]

Reflecting on the gybe once other builders had shared their own experience of the same failure, the episode was framed as a first real lesson in how differently a lug rig wants to be sailed compared to a Bermudian one: a lug sail needs to be sheeted in tight when close-hauled, confirmed firsthand across the boat's first two sails, and habits carried over from Bermudian trim proved more hindrance than help — better served by staying alert to how the lug rig itself was behaving. A sail left untethered to the mast, rather than fixed to a boom the way a Bermudian main is, was judged to carry its own elegant freedom, for all the further learning it still demands.[45]

## A topping lift tried and set aside, and a first grounding (September 2018)

A topping lift, hooked up for the first time that weekend, proved more trouble than help: once the wind kicked up, its line got packed into the partially reefed sail just as she needed careful handling to navigate between docks and other boats jutting out into the lake from shore, and it came off again once untangled. The furling routine already in use instead — ease the release halyard a few inches, pull in the reefing line, lift the halyard a bit, and repeat — was judged good enough for now, with a taste for experimenting further with lines and technique together. The same outing brought a first grounding, found to be entirely undramatic: after reefing the sail and removing the topping lift, simply stepped over the side into less than knee-deep water, pushed the boat off, climbed back aboard, and sailed on to the marina under reefed sail, having learned how quickly lines can become a problem.[46]

## A trailer bolt considered, and answers from four builders (September 2018)

Unlike many other Paradoxes photographed on the mailing list, "The Edge" has no bow U-bolt or eye bolt for hoisting her onto her trailer — a fitting Don Elliott never installed. Without one, the trailer's cable is hooked around the bow cleats instead, which pulls the boat on but forces the bow down under the trailer's rubber block and off the straight line to it, costing extra time at the ramp. Four builders answered with their own bolt placements; full discussion is at [operations/trailering.md — a bow attachment point for the trailer winch](/operations/trailering.md#a-bow-attachment-point-for-the-trailer-winch-placement-and-going-without-one).[47] Thanked the group for the advice, and for the towing story that came with it, and resolved to add the fitting as a winter project, unhappy with how the bow cleats alone had aligned the boat onto the trailer; also put out a general invitation to sail together off the East Coast that season.[48]

## Removing "The Edge" from her trailer, and a beach-launch question (October 2018)

A further question in October asked more broadly whether a Paradox is ever taken bodily off her trailer for storage, rather than just launched and recovered at a ramp: "The Edge"'s own trailer has bunks rather than rollers, with a pivot and locking pin, and the plan was to remove her over the coming winter to store close to ground level in a garage.[49] Three builders answered with three different ways of getting a boat off her trailer entirely, and a further exchange gave practical technique for hauling a boat up a beach above the high-tide mark — relevant to a possible future Everglades Challenge entry, which requires a self-launch from just such a beach.[50][51] Full discussion of every method offered: [operations/trailering.md — removing the boat from the trailer entirely](/operations/trailering.md#removing-the-boat-from-the-trailer-entirely-for-storage-or-a-beach-launch-october-2018).

## A winch upgrade planned, and a trailer-length question (October 2018)

Had already tried, a month earlier, to reach Matt Layden directly through the Everglades Challenge's own founder without a reply, and wondered whether Matt might be off on another challenge of his own.[52] Continued the trailer conversation the same day: admired Alastair Law's own trailer, seen on his website, as "a work of art," described floating "The Edge" on and off her trailer at a ramp in fresh water as a workable practice so far, expected ramps and launch environments elsewhere to differ, and asked how far past the wheels the trailer extends — answered with the same trailer specifications already on record (see [operations/trailering.md](/operations/trailering.md#a-2016-recurrence-transverse-versus-longitudinal-bunks)), together with news that the winch itself had since been upgraded to a braked unit.[53][54] Days later, having ordered a new two-speed, 2,500 lb winch with a brake to replace a single, non-braking one, planned a longer winch stand — 24 to 32 in — to raise it to the height of the bow cleats, with the V-block set lower to rest the bow; the next task after that was set as lowering the boat from the trailer to the ground on small rollers, or a dolly with wheels.[55]

A pun on "non-breaking" versus "non-braking" drew a serious answer a few days later: the old winch still in use — not yet replaced by the new braked unit on order — was rusty and in terrible condition, trusted through six trips regardless, on a ramp so far wide and easy enough to float the boat on and off, though not every ramp was expected to prove as convenient. The same message reported starting to sand the trailer's own old paint for a two-part epoxy repaint, having stripped and epoxy-sealed the mast's paint the day before, ready for priming.[56]

## A no-motor sailing plan restated, and a poling technique tried (October 2018)

Replying to a fellow owner's own grounding story on the same "day things went wrong" thread, restated the intent to sail "The Edge" without a motor at all — unlike his other boats, which all carry some auxiliary help — expecting to be tested often by situations where a motor would be the easy answer, and flagged the chine runner's foot, sitting a few inches further out than instinct expects, as the one caution from the thread worth keeping in mind.[57] Replying separately to a canoe-poling technique described on the mailing list (full technique: [sculling and yuloh technique — poling](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#poling-a-canoe-poling-technique-for-shallow-water-groundings-and-going-upwind-without-sail)), reported having already tried poling with his own yuloh that past summer, sailing close to shore on a calm day with the sail up only for shade: not a difficult task, and it moved the boat rather than merely holding her, though after a few pokes with the pole he went back to ordinary yulohing, his own preference. He had first learned to pole visiting André-François Bourbeau in Canada, tried out in Bourbeau's own canoes, and recalled watching Bourbeau pole his own boat along a lake shoreline at a very fast clip.[58]

The plan held: by March 2021, "The Edge" had gone three full years without a motor, sailed entirely on fresh-water lakes with no current or tide to fight — the condition, on this owner's own account, that makes going without one practical. Full account of the wider thread it came up in: [auxiliary outboard power — a 2021 recurrence](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-2021-recurrence-two-owners-head-cut-trolling-motor-conversions-and-a-torqeedo-ultralight-considered).[59]

## Protecting the rudder's leading edge (2019-2022)

A year on from the launch, asked the mailing list for ideas protecting the rudder's leading edge from rocks and other hidden objects, since an unprotected edge too often becomes "the default depth finder." Five techniques came back inside a day — rope glued in with epoxy as a sacrificial edge, heat-formed PVC electrical conduit, a salvaged half-round bronze strip, Teflon rub rail, and epoxy-soaked webbing folded around the edge — full detail at [components/rudder.md — a proven copper-pipe leading edge, and a dacron rope alternative](/components/rudder.md#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative). Summarized the suggestions as three broad kinds — a Teflon rub rail, copper or galvanized pipe, and epoxy with tape or rope — and had the coming winter to make the improvement.[60]

Nearly three years later, reported the fix carried out: the leading edge built up with **G-flex epoxy**, thick enough that a hit on a rock no longer lets water into the wood, at the cost of the blade's own measurements no longer matching Don Elliott's original spec exactly, though with no noticeable loss of speed. Passed on two further details about the same rudder to a newer builder puzzling over Elliott's own shaping instructions: the trailing edge is finished as a flat about **1/4 in** wide, its taper worked with guide lines on the blade the way contour lines mark depth on a chart, and Don's own rudder comes out in practice **"basically a flat blade,"** not a dynamically shaped foil. General technique: [components/rudder.md — foil section](/components/rudder.md#foil-section-not-a-standard-naca-airfoil).[61]

## A planned 2020 Texas 200 entry

Registered "The Edge" for the June 2020 **[Texas 200](/merits/texas-200.md)**, inviting other Paradox owners to sail alongside her and pointing to `www.texas200.com` for details — noting Sean Mulligan's "Scout" and Michael Beebe's own Paradox had both already run the event.[62]

Two months later, agreeing with other owners about the cam-cleat angle for the lines coming into the cabin, described having to shift forward to lock them in as uncomfortable — at times pulling on a line with both hands while pressing it down into the cleat with a foot — and expected a new block and angle change to make locking the lines easier.[63]

Carried out, both of Alastair Law's own second-build tips proved out: a recess cut into the seat runners, tested over a six-hour sail, made a comfortable sloping backrest (see [components/interior.md](/components/interior.md#citations)); the cam cleat block was rebuilt at a more horizontal angle, "a huge improvement," with a further cleat added below it to share the load (see [components/rig/furling.md](/components/rig/furling.md#citations)). The single-line halyard-and-reefing idea was not adopted, since the boat is towed with the mast removed through the aft hatch and the boom lashed down on the port deck, the reefing line still cleated inside the cabin to hold the rigged, covered sail in place — a setup that needs two separately workable lines rather than one continuous length. A DC electrical panel and a solar system were fitted as an addition rather than a change, the panel switching a cabin fan, an autopilot, locker LED lights, a red night-time cabin light, and the running lights, with a GPS and a phone charged by plugging directly into the panel.[64]

The panel eventually settled into five switches — the fan, locker lights, red navigation lights, a bank of 12V accessory outlets for the autopilot and a water pump, and the autopilot itself — each on its own internal fuse, fed from a single positive and negative distribution bus in the battery locker; the DC panel carries a further two 12V outlets for a Garmin unit and a phone. Solar comes from a 75 W panel on the bow deck through a Victron smart controller, its state of charge readable over Bluetooth from a phone app. As built by Don Elliott to the plans with no modifications of his own, "The Edge" carried no permanent light at the masthead — Don's own practice was a portable light hoisted up the mast as needed — and there is little spare room inside the mast once it is packed for towing, a permanent fitting there being an easy thing to damage; the running lights above are additions made after the boat changed hands.[65]

By July 2020 "The Edge" was back on the water after a repaint — the outside redone the previous year, the inside that year — and had sailed the Chesapeake for the first time with the Shallow Water Sailing Club, a first outing beyond her usual New York fresh water. Deric's own belief places her construction among the design's early boats, around **1996**.[66] By January 2021 she was kept nearer the **City of Oswego**, on Lake Ontario's south-eastern shore, still carrying her original design sail shape.[67]

## Two water-tank fill and drain methods (October 2020)

By that October, two settled methods for filling and emptying the water tanks were in regular use. A **36 in hand-held pump** can do both jobs on its own, but emptying that way leaves too much water behind, needing a smaller pump to move the remainder into a bucket and a rag to finish; filling with it is easy. The alternative uses a **Rule bilge pump** connected to the fill hoses as the plans intend, straightforward for filling; emptying that way calls for the boat to be out of the water on her trailer, priming the siphon by pumping water from a bucket set on the ground beside the trailer through the bilge pump and hose until the air clears, then pulling the pump free to let the tank siphon out the rest on its own. General technique: [construction/transom-deck-plate.md — filling and draining the water tank](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#filling-and-draining-the-water-tank).[68]
