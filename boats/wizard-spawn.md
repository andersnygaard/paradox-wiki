# Wizard Spawn

**"Wizard Spawn"** is [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md)'s Paradox, under construction in **Venice, California**. Rodney announced starting the build on 7 August 2008, three months ahead of his own stated schedule — see [construction/build-effort.md](/construction/build-effort.md#practicing-epoxy-and-glassing-on-a-simpler-boat-first) for the practice-boat debate (Puddle Duck Racer versus a single-sheet 8-footer) that followed that announcement. The next day he moved from planning to the boat's first real parts: the deck and cabin beams.

## Starting on the beams: jigs, sizing confusion, and a lesson in plywood

Rigging the beam-laminating jigs took a full day's work, after which the plans' instruction to laminate all deck and cabin beams to one long length and cut each to size later — adding 3 in of working length to each end, per Don Elliott's construction manual — proved hard to picture in the abstract; a short break away from the plans was enough to make the answer click without further help.[1][2][3] The jig design itself was credited, three weeks later, to another builder's own build-photo website — [Cachivache](/boats/cachivache.md), under construction in Patagonia — judged considerably easier to follow than the construction manual's own method.[4] Along the way came a small realization common to first-time builders working from a stock materials list: beams are conventionally laminated from thin plywood rather than solid timber, leaving a stock of nice mahogany, bought before that was understood, without an obvious use in the beams themselves.[3]

A concrete worked example from another builder's own boat answered the sizing question directly: laminating all beams, deck and cabin, from **9 mm plywood**; ripping the deck-beam strips the full length of a sheet (about **1,210 mm**) and cutting each strip in half, giving plenty of margin over the **~1,190 mm** the longest deck beam actually needs; a full set of **seven deck beams at five laminates each** (35 pieces); and roughly **ten cabin beams**, of varying height and width, all laminated to a uniform **900 mm** length before individual trimming. Two extra sheets of 9 mm ply covered these beams plus other small parts such as lazarette bins. The glue-up itself followed the standard two-step process — wet out both surfaces with unthickened epoxy, let it soak in, then butter on epoxy thickened with a cabosil-and-cellulose-fibre blend and clamp to squeeze-out, left a full 24 hours to cure.[5] See [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md) for the general technique this worked example confirms.

Fitting a laminated-plywood beam carries one further caution, repeated independently to Rodney from firsthand experience: a beam can delaminate within the plywood's own glue lines, quite apart from the epoxy bond holding the lamination together, and is more of a risk on a free-standing beam than on one bonded directly to a bulkhead. See [fastening and gluing — a delamination caution from experience](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#fastening-laminated-ply-panels-a-delamination-caution-from-experience) for the full account, first reported from [Faith](/boats/faith.md#a-laminated-hatch-beam-that-delaminated-in-the-ply-itself)'s own hatch beam and repeated here to a new builder just starting his own.[6]

## Mahogany for the interior, inspired by another builder's finish

The mahogany Rodney had already bought — the stock left without an obvious job once he learned the beams themselves are laminated plywood — was in fact bought for the interior joinery, not the beams: having admired photographs of [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md)'s finished interior enough to e-mail him directly and ask what wood he had used, Rodney settled on the same mahogany for his own boat. Whether to rip the wood into strips or bend it in as a single piece remained an open question even after weighing a document on the choice already circulating in the group's files section.[7]

## Encouragement from established builders

Starting a build drew encouragement from three builders further along. [Stephen Yahn](/people/stephen-yahn.md) framed epoxy as simple and nearly foolproof, with nails and screws providing a permanent mechanical backup to the glue joint regardless, and suggested the rudder, mast, boom, yard, and yuloh as good parts to build first; he also passed along his own solution for keeping hands clean without resorting to solvents on bare skin — see [epoxy safety and personal protection — surgical gloves as an alternative to solvent hand-cleaning](/construction/epoxy-safety-and-ppe.md#surgical-gloves-as-an-alternative-to-solvent-hand-cleaning).[8] Pete Martin, sixteen months into his own build with no particular hurry (built as a retirement boat), pointed to Jimmy Lu's rapid build of "[Auspice](/boats/auspice.md)" — finished in under eight months — as proof a Paradox can go together quickly, while noting his own more leisurely pace was a matter of choice rather than difficulty; he recommended studying other builders' progress photos as one of the most useful resources available, naming Little Jim, Faith, Auspice, and Cachivache by name.[9] Answering the same thread, Al (Alastair Law) restated the roughly 18-month rule of thumb for a typical build and added a pointed piece of realism for anyone hoping to launch on a compressed schedule: chasing a fast finish leaves no time for side projects like a practice boat, so a builder aiming for a near-term launch should set the practice-boat idea aside entirely.[10]

## Locating the mainsheet, and adopting Glen Maxwell's rerouted lead

Working from the plans and Don Elliott's construction manual side by side, Rodney could not find where the mainsheet was meant to run: the vent-box drawings show only two lines passing through, while interior photographs of "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" show three, and a link that once illustrated Glen Maxwell's own routing had gone dead.[11] The mainsheet itself is shown on Drawing 1, in the lower right-hand zone, not among the vent-box lines at all; a separate rope on Drawing 11, run along the tiller, lifts the rudder blade. Zoë's third vent-box line is not part of the stock design — it exists only because she carries an added topping lift, a widely adopted owner's fix documented in full at [furling system — what the topping lift does, rope choice, and use](/components/rig/furling.md#what-the-topping-lift-does-rope-choice-and-use).[12]

Rodney acknowledged the answer good-naturedly, having overlooked the one drawing sheet that actually carried it.[13] Pete Martin filled in the practical choice behind Zoë's own arrangement: rather than the stock lead, follow the reroute Glen Maxwell made to keep the mainsheet clear of his self-steering wind vane — a turning block fitted under the deck, water draining to the baffle below and out through the tiller opening — a lead that also cuts down fouling for a boat carrying an outboard, as Rodney intended to fit. See [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling — the rear-deck-entry question resolved](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#the-rear-deck-entry-question-resolved-attachment-point-not-entry-point-is-the-real-hazard) for the reroute itself; photographs of both the new mainsheet lead and the topping-lift arrangement are in the group's "Zoe's Vane Gear" and "Topping Lift" photo folders.[14]

Rodney adopted the same lead for his own boat, having already downloaded the group's whole file and photo archive early on, and asked in turn whether the self-steering vane-gear plans were finished yet.[15] They were not: light-air performance was still unsatisfactory, so the vane blade — now built of plywood — had gained more forward area to make it easier for the wind to turn it, rather than enlarge the vane further; testing continued intermittently over several years before the gear was confirmed working reliably. See [Zoë — a patient self-steering project](/boats/zoe.md#continued-vane-refinement-an-undersized-blade-and-the-autopilot-coupling) for the fuller, ongoing story of that gear.[16] Concluding that he had badly underestimated his own build time and that a wind vane was likely many months off, Rodney chose to keep running his mainsheet as Glen and Pete had described, preferring a complete picture of the whole project before committing to any one system.[17]

Passing the fix on from memory a year later, Rodney recalled it to two builders new to the same recurring outboard/mainsheet tangle problem, crediting the reroute to "Bill S. and Glen M." together — see [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md — a 2009 recurrence across four boats, and two new fix ideas](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-2009-recurrence-across-four-boats-and-two-new-fix-ideas) for the fuller 2009 exchange this prompted, including photographs showing "Faith" herself never adopted the reroute at all.[18]

## Choosing an epoxy: MAS for general work, System Three reserved for the tanks

Having already used **MAS** low-viscosity 2:1 epoxy resin, bought locally at marine stores, for his deck beams, Rodney weighed using it for the rest of the boat too, reserving **System Three** specifically for the water tanks, and asked whether anyone had run into trouble with the MAS brand.[19] [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md) recommended System Three from firsthand experience, having built the whole of "Raffles" with it for its price, easy mixing, and customer service, and pointed to **Aircraft Spruce** in nearby Corona as a Southern California supplier stocking both a wide range of structural epoxies and System Three itself.[20] Rodney judged he would have used System Three from the start had he known of a local source, admitting an impatience with waiting on mail orders even while content to take his time on the build itself.[21] A further, independent voice found nothing wrong with MAS either, reasoning that a brand already proven on the beams should work fine elsewhere in the build too, and passed along a further eBay-sourced generic-epoxy tip from a San Francisco builder.[22] Weighing Pete Martin's own MAS-to-Low-Viscosity switch (see [people/pete-martin.md](/people/pete-martin.md)) against the mail-order hassle of System Three, Rodney settled on finishing the bulk of his build with **West System** instead — more readily available and cheaper near him — while keeping the System Three he had already ordered specifically for the water tanks, to be done strictly per the plans and manual; the MAS used on his deck beams had in the meantime proved very strong, leaving him satisfied that brand matters less than following the mix and use instructions correctly.[23] Full brand comparison: [construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#mas-epoxy-validated-for-general-use-and-a-california-supplier).

## Sourcing plywood locally

Weighing the two 2008 Sailrite sail listings alongside his own build, Rodney also reported on the more mundane search for materials close to home: after visiting five lumber yards over the course of a month, he finally found one carrying the "good stuff" needed for the build. See [Sail — choosing between the two 2008 Sailrite listings](/components/rig/sail.md#choosing-between-the-two-2008-sailrite-listings) for the sail-plan comparison this update accompanied.[24]

## A build-sequencing strategy: the sail kit before hull assembly

Working in a limited build area, Rodney planned to complete as much of the boat's separate sub-assemblies as possible before joining the hull together, sewing the newly ordered Sailrite sail kit among them — reasoning that finishing every piece he could in advance would let him go sailing the same day the hull itself was complete, weather permitting.[25]

## Starting the yard, boom, and yuloh: reading Drawing #12

A month later, Rodney turned to the yard, boom, and yuloh, working through Don Elliott's builders manual appendix for the mast and puzzling out much of the yuloh, but stalled on Drawing #12's squared-off spar sections: he could read the length and width given at the top and bottom of the yard and boom drawings, but not the square of dimensions in the middle of each. The answer, that each square is a section through the spar at that point along its length, with the yard measuring 42 x 38 mm (12 mm radius corners) 700 mm from one end, the boom 47 x 38 mm 1,850 mm from the tack and 43 x 38 mm 950 mm from the tack, and the yuloh's flatter side sitting uppermost in use, is recorded in full at [references/building-plans.md — reading the plans](/references/building-plans.md#reading-the-plans-a-first-time-builders-backlog-of-questions).[26][27]

Thanking Al for the explanation, Rodney's next question turned to shaping the wood itself: working only with hand tools — a skilsaw and jigsaw, both with fences, a belt sander, a drill, and a good set of chisels — he asked how the yard, boom, and yuloh's curved and rounded-corner sections are actually cut and rounded to the dimensions given.[28] The answer — bulk removal with a hand plane or a coarse sanding disc in an angle grinder, then a strop of 1-inch sandpaper worked alternately over each corner for a smooth curve, checked against cardboard templates cut to each section — is recorded in full at [components/yuloh.md — building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket); Rodney thanked it as an easy idea he hadn't considered.[29][30]

## Rudder-blade questions, and a dado explained

Turning next to the rudder blade, Rodney found a "rudder blade" photo folder in the group's files section showing a rudder being fitted with lead ballast in a method and placement unlike the plans and Don Elliott's manual, and separately asked about the aerofoil's undimensioned end, whether the blade is shaped only to the waterline, and whether the lead end is shaped down to the same 6mm too.[31] The pictured rudder turned out to be Little Cruiser's, not Paradox's own; the answer also covered the shaping technique in full — photocopying the aerofoil section to full size for cardboard templates, a square trailing edge for less drag and robustness, keeping the stock-bearing face flat and blended into the foil shape, and leaving the uphaul-groove radius at full thickness — plus a proven leading-edge/bottom wear fix (split copper water pipe hammered round the leading edge and bottom, bonded with epoxy, sound after three seasons aside from the paint wearing off) and a Duckworks-sourced dacron-rope-in-epoxy-groove alternative. See [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#rudder-ballast-without-a-lead-casting) and [components/rudder.md — a proven copper-pipe leading edge](/components/rudder.md#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative).[32]

Thanking Al for the templating tip, Rodney asked a further question — what a "dado," mentioned on Drawing #8 and elsewhere for the tank dividers, actually is — which drew two independent answers: a recess a divider's end fits into, comparable to the mortice in a mortice-and-tenon joint, and, separately, the fuller woodworking definition (a groove sawn or routered to a set width, cut with a dado blade on a table saw or a router's straight bit). See [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md — bulkhead construction details](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#bulkhead-construction-details-not-spelled-out-on-the-plans).[33][34][35]

## Abandoned in California, and a fresh start in Louisiana (2012)

By February 2012, Rodney reported the California build had come close to completion — "almost finished" — before a life-changing personal move to **New Orleans, Louisiana** forced him to abandon her unfinished. Starting over there, he found the building process markedly easier the second time; weighed a Welsford Fafnir for the new build but chose Paradox again, judging her shallow draft still the better fit for his sailing conditions. No further report of either hull's fate is on record.[58]

## Two outside names, six years apart

Nothing in Rodney's own record of this build gives the boat a name; he speaks of her only as his own boat, or simply "Paradox," throughout. Two outside sources name her instead, six years apart, and they disagree. Six weeks after his 7 August 2008 build start, a UK builders' registry recorded "Rod Cannon" of Venice, CA as building a boat called **"Magic-Hat."**[36a] By 2014, [Bill Serjeant's worldwide builders' census](/references/builders-and-owners-census-2014.md#the-table) lists the same "Rod Cannon" of "USA, Venice CA" as still "In build," his boat now named **"Wizard Spawn."**[36] Given the 2012 move above, the census's California location is either carried forward from his last-known address or describes a hull he resumed after returning there; nothing settles it, and no update from Rodney himself follows either registry entry. Neither name appears anywhere in his own messages, and nothing on record says which, if either, he actually kept. Both are fitting labels, in their own way, for a builder who once earned a living making things vanish: a conjurer's Magic Hat prop, and a Wizard to match a line from his own teenage years about going "to be a wizard" (see [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md)).

## A Torqeedo pointer

Between build sessions, Rodney found a boatersTV-seen review of the Torqeedo electric outboard, a possible pinch-hitter for when the yuloh proves difficult to use — see [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md — a Torqeedo pointer, and its price](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-torqeedo-pointer-and-its-price) for the fuller exchange it drew.[37]

## A missed bulkhead curve, and a cleat-extension fix

Working through the plans' bulkhead drawings that November, Rodney realized only after already epoxying bulkhead 1 that its side edge was meant to carry the plans' small stiffening curve rather than a straight line — the same detail a fellow builder had just asked the list about. Rather than re-cutting the plywood, the fix was to extend the bulkhead's own side cleats out over the panel's edge by the missing amount, then fill the resulting gap with epoxy at assembly: the curve's real job is stiffening the side panel against oil-canning, and since cured epoxy is stronger than the plywood it replaces, the corrected joint comes out stronger than if the curve had been cut correctly from the start. Full technique: [construction/panel-layout.md — bulkhead side curves](/construction/panel-layout.md#bulkhead-side-curves).[38][39][40][41]

## Learning how the mast is actually stepped

New to sailing, Rodney worked through the mast/vent-trunk drawings but could find no clamp or pin holding the mast down against a gust, and asked for a plain step-by-step account of stepping it.[42] The answer he got — lift the mast onto the foredeck, swing it upright, seat the foot through the deck, and trust the halyard's own tension once the sail is hoisted — he found almost suspiciously easy compared to what he had expected.[43] See [components/rig/mast.md — stepping the mast](/components/rig/mast.md#stepping-the-mast-a-first-timers-question-answered).

## A planned relocation to Florida, and continuing progress

At the end of December 2008, Rodney reported an unexpected personal turn: a relocation from Venice, California to Florida within the following eight months, expecting to finish the boat there instead of California. Construction by then had reached **bulkheads 1 and 2 complete**, the remaining bulkheads cut out, and the yuloh and mast partway built, with a 2010 Bahamas cruise still the hoped-for goal.[44] The news drew a fellow builder's congratulations and his own envy of Florida living, tempered with a joking nod to its rain and hurricanes.[45][46] By mid-January 2009 the planned destination had narrowed from the Tampa area first named to **Fort Lauderdale** specifically, drawing a further joking warning about Florida's storms and wildlife from another builder.[47][48]

## A build-transport strategy: finishing parts before the hull, for the move to Florida

By March 2009, construction had slowed: rather than press on with the hull sides and bottom, Rodney chose to complete as many of the boat's separate parts as he could first, planning to transport the disassembled kit to Florida for final assembly there once his move to Fort Lauderdale went through that September — the same build-sequencing logic already applied to the sail kit (see [a build-sequencing strategy: the sail kit before hull assembly](#a-build-sequencing-strategy-the-sail-kit-before-hull-assembly) above). He had by then ordered a Sailrite sail kit and begun looking for a sewing machine.[49]

## Still building in Venice Beach, over a year on (October 2009)

By October 2009 — past the planned September target for the move to Fort Lauderdale — Rodney was still building from the same small Venice Beach apartment; the relocation had evidently not yet gone through. Encouraging another newcomer weighing whether so involved a design could really be tackled from a single one-car garage, he offered his own build as a case in point: a bare passing grade in a high-school shop class was the whole of his prior woodworking background, every snag along the way had been worked out through the archive or by studying the plans a little longer, and small parts were made ready for assembly in his own bathroom — cut and sanded in the bathtub, easy to clean up afterward — in a small flat space behind the apartment building, built there with his landlord's permission. Once the boat existed complete in his own head, he found, the physical building itself came easier; a boat like this, he told her, could not simply be bought — it had to be built.[50]

A fuller inventory of the same small-space strategy followed a month later: a completed bulkhead occupied every closet in the apartment, and the spar, once finished, was to hang from hooks screwed into the ceiling; hand-sanding continued in the bathtub or bathroom at night, with power-sanding and cutting done outdoors by day, exactly as before. The plan for final hull assembly was to move the hull onto a dolly and occupy his own car's parking space with it, parking the car on the street instead and keeping the hull under a tarp between sessions. Whatever the boat's own eventual fate, he judged the project already worthwhile for everything it had taught him unrelated to sailing or boatbuilding themselves, and recommended the experience of building a boat to anyone — win or lose on the water, he would not regret having built her.[51]

## A second, unrelated boat begun during a pause in the hull's finishing work

By April 2010, finishing the hull's glassing and finishing stages needed a lump of cash not yet in hand, ruling out a hoped-for launch that May. A folding, kayak-inspired boat design pointed Rodney to the Yostwerks folding-kayak site, where he began building a "Sea Bee 13" from scrap wood on the side while the Paradox waited on funds.[52]

## Nearing completion, and a reflection on building "on faith" (October 2010)

Replying to a fellow owner's trust-the-designer testimonial, Rodney reported being in the end phase of his own build and finding the endorsement reassuring, given how much controversy surrounds the design.[53] He had chosen Paradox on faith — the word of actual owners rather than the untested experience of others — and found building her easier than the Sea Bee 13 folding kayak he built alongside her, despite having no real woodworking background beyond a passing grade in high-school shop class. The plans felt overwhelming at first, but building one step at a time let the design "begin to talk to you," a feeling hard to put into words that may hold true of any DIY project rather than being unique to boatbuilding. He thanked the mailing list for its help throughout.[53]

## A November 2010 update: a winter hiatus, and a completed kayak

Answering a newcomer's inquiry about Paradoxes on the US West Coast,[54] Rodney reported the build about two and a half years along and very slow due to "Life" intrusions, but almost finished; he had taken a hiatus from it for the winter, having just completed the Yostwerks Sea Bee 13 kayak, which he planned to paddle through the season, and pointed to [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md) as another West Coast builder, though he did not know whether Waters' own boat was still on that coast.[55]

## A January 2011 echo: still "almost done"

Two months later, congratulating "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)"'s new owner, Rodney again called his own build almost done, still building from Los Angeles.[56] See [Zoë — a new owner, and an offer of Bay-area company](/boats/zoe.md#a-new-owner-and-an-offer-of-bay-area-company) for the West Coast Paradoxes news this drew.

## An April 2011 echo: a hometown recognized, and a status update

Replying to a different Kansas City builder's decision to sell his own unfinished Paradox's parts and supplies (see [Steve (Kansas City)](/people/steve-kc.md#selling-the-build-2011)), Rodney recognized Kansas City as his own hometown, mentioned a planned May visit to his grandmother there, and offered to look at the parts if still unsold by then; asked in passing how his own build stood, he answered only that most of it was together, "but you never know."[57]
