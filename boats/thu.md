# THU (Eerik Mee)

**THU** is [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s Paradox, built in **Estonia** from **July 2015** and **launched on an inland Estonian lake over the weekend of 1-2 July 2017**, reported to the mailing list the same evening.[1][2][25] It is unlike any other boat in the archive in that it departs from the plans in **two** large ways at once and was still **built and sailed**: it is scaled up **20%** on every dimension, and it carries a **purpose-designed cambered Chinese junk rig**.[3][4]

Those two departures put THU under the two longest-running "argued but never built" threads in the record. It is the archive's **only built-and-sailed example of the [larger Paradox](/design/larger-paradox.md)** — the scale-up the mailing list discussed roughly forty times without a completed build — and, alongside [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md), one of only two junk-rig Paradoxes on record actually built and sailed, and the **only one with a junk rig designed from scratch** rather than converted from the stock lug.[3][4] Its whole story is on [Eerik Mee's blog](/references/eerik-mee-blog.md), captured as this bundle's source.

## The 20% scale-up

Mee sized the boat up **uniformly, ×1.2 on every plans measurement**, to fit himself: "I multiplied just all measurements with 1.2, so yes, it is also wider and higher. LOA turned out about **5.05 m** and width about **1.5 m**."[3] Those figures land within centimetres of the worked 20% scale-up the list had carried since 2008 — LOA 5.04 m, beam 1.48 m — so THU is, in effect, that paper boat built (see [larger Paradox](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement)). The **stock scantlings were kept**; only the **spars scaled** with the hull, the mast growing to **6 m**.[4][5] Mee recalculated plywood consumption before buying material and noted a little creativity was needed to optimise it — using 3×12 stock instead of 2×18 for the rudder, for instance — but found the drawing "easy to follow" once every dimension was multiplied through.[1]

The larger hull is heavier and stiffer to handle ashore: launching solo, Mee tied the boat to his car and reversed to push her the last 50–150 m into the water, and he judged the heavier mast a possible "hassle in heavy wind and slippery cabin top," part of why he intended her to be **moored rather than trailered**.[5][6]

The build's own milestones were also reported on the **Paradox builders mailing list** itself, not only the blog: in August 2015, with the hull already assembled, Mee "locked" the boat's name and posted his own "going 3D" photo — a tradition he noted every Paradox seems to observe — confirming on the list, in real time, that the second Paradox under way in Estonia was a 20% larger version. Truing the hull used the same section-by-section diagonal-comparison method recorded generally at [hull assembly — truing the hull](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method): the diagonals were checked and fixed separately at every section, since different sections can be out of true by different amounts.[15] Asked directly on the list three days later whether the scale-up ran through every dimension or just length,[16] Mee confirmed she was "fully scaled up for 20%"[17] — the earliest on-the-record confirmation of that point, four years before the blog comment above restated the same LOA and beam figures.

## The junk rig

THU's rig is the archive's only **purpose-designed cambered junk sail** on a Paradox. Rather than convert the stock lug (as was done on [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)), Mee designed a **six-panel cambered junk sail** from scratch, following **Arne Kverneland**'s method and drawing on the **Junk Rig Association**: **8% camber** built into the panels, the three lower panels identical, sewn on Odyssey sailcloth with the mast-side batten pockets in wear-resistant PVC truck-tent cloth.[4] He rejected a flat-cut sample plan on both looks and performance — "The HML is actually flat cut sail with not so good winward performance. It's the AK's camber that improves it" — and modified the shape toward a Reddish-type roach.[7] The battens and boom are 30×2 mm aluminium tube, the yard 50×2 mm.[4] The mast itself is round, built in birdmouth style and tapered towards both top and bottom, with the halyard sheave mounted inside so the halyard runs on and off it from the best angle.[8]

Carrying that taller rig in balance drove the boat's most consequential structural change: the **mast moved from aft of bulkhead 2 to as far forward of it as possible**.[7] Mee wanted more mast below deck for the 6 m spar and clearance between the transom and the upper sheet (about a 10° angle), and ran the halyard and throat/yard parrels down into the ventilation trunk aft of the mast.[7] The sheeting geometry itself had already been reasoned through before that mast move: though the boom reaches over the transom, the sail's own leech line curves inward enough that amidships it sits above the deck rather than beyond it. Mee chose the resulting **vertical-leech** sail plan over a fan-type alternative specifically to hold the centre of effort as far aft as possible and keep a lower aspect ratio, moving the **chine runners forward** to match the centre of effort's own resulting forward shift — and named a real cost of the choice ahead of finishing the boat: losing the stock rig's easy on-deck lowering of a reefed sail, since a junk's batten barrels have to be loosened first.[23] The junk rig also needs **more control lines than the stock boat**: "We have 5 camcleats in total, because of the junk sail. Most Paradoxes have 2 or 3."[9] A three-sheave deflector on the mast keeps the lines clear of the lower sail on the starboard downwind tack.[9]

Where the long list-era junk-rig argument (see [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md)) had gone untested, THU supplies data: the sail was given the **camber** the theory said flat panels lacked, the **mast was moved forward** to hold balance, and the boat sails. But the same record shows the balance and sheeting problems were **not fully solved on the water**: the boom twisted too much under sheet, Mee experimented with block positions without settling it, and one tack showed noticeable **lee helm**, which he corrected by adding water ballast forward and tightening the tack parrel to move the centre of effort back.[6][9]

## Ballast, and the systems boat

THU carries about **240 kg of ballast**. Mee first sailed her on ~240 kg of mixed ballast — "about 100 kg of lead and the rest … water" — then cast the lead proper as **twelve pigs of ~20 kg (45 lb) each**, poured into drywall-panel moulds buried in earth, under EU rules that by then required buying the lead on a company's account.[10][11] He later shared a video on the [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) showing the casting process, offered as a reference for other builders facing the same job — the mould itself built from drywall-panel offcuts glued together with hot-melt glue, buried and backed with soil on the same reasoning as a buried bread-pan mould, an approach he devised, by his own account, without finding any earlier record of it, and found successful; the pour fell between the 2017 and 2019 sailing seasons.[12][30] The pigs split **six to a side**, sized to clear the ballast compartment's own hinged lid; two of the twelve needed recasting to a shallower mould after coming out too thick to let the lid close.[31] The main ballast compartment sits **aft of bulkhead 3** (rated by Mee at ~220 kg), with water ballast available forward for trim — the same forward water ballast he used to cure the lee helm.[10][9]

Unusually for a Paradox — where many builds are motorless or sculled — THU is a **fully powered and instrumented boat**. She carries an **electric outboard** on a scratch-built GRP transom bracket, **two 50 W flexible solar panels** — one on the foredeck, one on the cabin top under an opening, acrylic-topped hatch so it keeps working whether the hatch is open or closed — a **LiFePO₄ battery**, a charge controller, an inverter (Mee "pumped my tires up from the boat battery"), a **depth sounder** with an internally bonded transducer, a fish finder, a handheld VHF radio chargeable from a car socket or AA batteries, and **LED cabin lighting** made from strip inside transparent hose sealed with silicone.[11][33] A masthead light runs up a hose inside the mast, with a copper-tube lightning conductor grooved into the mast's front face.[4] A cast-and-webbing **PVC sea anchor** (800 mm) and a bow anchor complete the outfit.[13][11]

## Build details worth noting

Several of Mee's methods are recorded in enough detail to be useful:

- **Hull and rudder.** Hull sides scarfed and milled in pairs to match, bottom glassed with peel-ply, chine runners shaped and glassed; the rudder blade was **laminated with lead cast into it**, and the stock built up from **three layers of 12 mm plywood** to ~40 mm finished, hung on a **cast-epoxy lower gudgeon plus a bought upper pintle/gudgeon set — both used, because the scaled-up rudder is heavy**.[1][9]
- **Windows.** Side windows are **triplex laminated glass** (two 3 mm layers with a film between); the curved front windows were **cut from a car windshield** at a windscreen-replacement shop.[13]
- **Antifouling and insulation.** The faired bottom was coated with a **copper-powder/epoxy** mix; the interior is insulated with **XPS foam**, faced with light glassed fabric and glued in with polyurethane.[1][13]
- **A sealable cabin hatch.** The sliding hatch was built with its front edge lower than the back, so it compresses a gasket harder as it closes and barely touches when cracked open — a home-worked take on a watertight companionway.[13] Measured precisely after fitting: the front end sits 1 cm lower than the back, matched by the structure beneath; closed, the gasket gap runs just under 15 mm under slight compression, opening to about 25 mm fully open; the gasket meets the carlins about 10 cm before fully closed, and silicone oil keeps it sliding smoothly, with plastic strips under the hatch-runner grooves to stop them scratching the cabin top. See [components/hatch.md — an asymmetric hatch slope and a measured gasket-compression system](/components/hatch.md#an-asymmetric-hatch-slope-and-a-measured-gasket-compression-system-2017).[24]
- **Plywood species and rot protection.** The hull used **okoume marine plywood** — a species Mee noted is not well regarded in some French boatbuilding circles, though any exterior-grade plywood shares the same glue and differs mainly in void count — treated before epoxy sealing with a **50% monoethylene-glycol solution** to protect against rot and improve the epoxy bond,[18] the same base ingredient as automotive antifreeze without its additives.[19]
- **Fitting the bottom.** A straight **aluminium bar** sighted along the chine logs checked them level to each other and to the bulkheads over the full length, corrected with a block plane and finished with a sanding block; before the permanent glue-up, witness-dowel holes drilled through the dry-fitted bottom into bulkhead #3 let it be relocated in exactly the same position — a further truing check alongside the section-by-section diagonal-comparison method noted above. See [hull assembly sequence — truing the hull](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method).[20]
- **Dry-fitting bulkheads without full disassembly.** Rather than taking the whole box apart again to correct a bulkhead's bevel during dry fitting, pressing the sides a little way apart freed a single bulkhead at a time to be pulled out, adjusted, and reinstalled immediately with thickened epoxy — found easier than a full teardown. See [hull assembly sequence — truing the hull](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#truing-the-hull-the-diagonal-comparison-method).[21]
- **Glassing the bottom and sides in one session.** Done in a single go with a second pair of hands, judged too much work for two people to repeat willingly — the chine runners' top sides were the exception, covered separately with peel ply to save some fairing work later.[22]

## How she sails

The launch itself, over the weekend of 1-2 July 2017, was as much a celebration as a shakedown: friends came aboard to mark the occasion, three or four at a time, everyone getting a turn at the helm, more entertaining the crowd than finishing the rig's tuning — conditions that weekend ranged from nearly no wind at all to a breeze strong enough to reef the sail down a panel.[25] Days later, asked by a fellow owner for photographs showing how the junk rig was fitted and sized, Mee promised more once friends' own photos came in, cautioning that the sheeting was not yet in its final setup and that he meant to try another option next time out — an early, contemporaneous confirmation of the boom-twist-and-sheeting problem the record shows was never fully solved on the water (below).[26]

A week later, Mee posted photographs of the sail and its current sheeting arrangement, still a work in progress: the boom was running too twisted in, and a session in very light, dying wind left little chance to test it further.[27] A fellow correspondent, who fed his own boat's balanced-lug sheet through a comparable arrangement, reported it had worked well for him too, provided the panel count was right.[28] Mee confirmed the panel count was correct and that this had been his first setup — leading the sheet through the ventilation chamber with two blocks on the boom — but the boom still pulled in too much; he changed the arrangement, took her out once more in very light wind, and had further options still to try once suitable weather returned.[29]

The launch trials were promising on the one count the theory most feared. Paradox is normally awkward to tack; THU **tacked without tricks**. Mee first tried the tricks other Paradox sailors use — turning slowly into the wind, shifting weight forward and to windward — to "No success," then tacked "without any tricks … and it was a success. As were all next tackings."[2] She sailed under 3 to 5 of her 6 panels on the first day and was out about **four times in 2017**, with more sailing in 2019.[2][11]

What the record does **not** yet settle is her performance to windward. Boom twist and sheeting were unresolved, and Mee was explicit: "It is not yet possible to make any conclusions on her pointing ability, more trials will have to be conducted for that."[2] She was **day-sailed on a lake**, never cruised, so — unlike [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md) or the [Dave Skaife](/people/dave-skaife.md) blog boat — THU documents that a scaled-up, cambered-junk Paradox floats on her lines and sails, but not how she holds up over distance.[2][6]

Posting to the [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) at the end of his 2020 season, Mee reported that sandy-beach beaching had mostly not happened in practice — conditions were either too windy and wavy or too rocky, so he stayed on anchor or in a marina instead, and hoped to have more courage with beaches the following season.[14] Sailing in Estonia was mostly a weekend activity for him, and he found less socialising along the way than he had imagined.[14] Manoeuvring in and out of marinas in wind was a recurring problem because the wind blows the bow off course, and he considered adding a small fin under the bow to counter it; he also found the electric outboard a bit weak in wavy conditions and wanted to move to a bigger motor and more batteries for a longer motoring range.[14]

Two gear failures needed fixing that season: the rudder-blade lifting line had jumped off the side of the rudder more than once, jamming the rudder to one side — a dangerous situation in strong wind — and the halyard had once come off the masthead sheave and stuck to one side, which he planned to cure with a new, wider sheave.[14]

The electric outboard's own mounting was described the following March: its control head cut off and the speed controller moved inside the boat, adopted after the difficulty manoeuvring in and out of the marina in stronger winds recorded above, and — restating the same wavy-water underpowering already found — with a Torqeedo Ultralight (about 3 hp equivalent) named as the planned upgrade, rather than the general "bigger motor" left open the previous September.[32]
