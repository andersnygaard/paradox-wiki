# Mini

**Mini** is [Rob Kellock](/people/rob-kellock.md)'s Paradox, built at his home workshop in **Kirwee, Canterbury**, near Christchurch, New Zealand.[1] Construction began in **September 2014**[2] and she went for her first sea trial in **December 2017**, three and a half years later than her builder's own eighteen-month guess.[3] She is built to the plans at hull scale, with one major departure: the standing lug rig she carried for her first four months afloat was replaced outright by a four-sided sprit rig in April 2018, tested and compared against the lug for years before the sprit was adopted for good.[4][5] She is still owned and sailed by her builder, last confirmed active in a February 2025 comment,[6] and works Lake Ellesmere and Lyttelton Harbour/Quail Island as a recurring winter ground, with cruises further afield to Nelson and Tasman Bay in 2018, Abel Tasman in 2020, Tauranga Harbour in 2021 and Queen Charlotte Sound in 2022 and 2023. She carries 70 L of water ballast as designed, plus lead,[7] cost roughly **NZD 6,500 all in** at 2018-era prices,[8] against her builder's own **NZD 8,000** estimate made mid-build in 2015,[9] and has never carried an outboard motor. Her six-year run of self-bounded, often GPS- or video-documented speed and handling data, including a test course built specifically to check exaggerated hull-speed claims circulating on the mailing list against measurement, makes her one of the fullest single-hull performance records in the archive.[10][11]

## The build, 2014-2015

Her builder's home town is precisely **Kirwee, Canterbury**, the nearby city of Christchurch being how he identifies himself on the list.[1] Having bought the wood for the mast in late July 2014, he weighed keeping the plans' internal halyard sheave against the external-sheave-and-wiring-conduit approach other builders had used to run a lightning-ground wire and masthead-light cable, and floated an untried idea of lining the mast cavity with tinfoil for radar visibility, reasoning it might also stop the internal wiring slapping about.[12] By early September the build was under way: the mast, yard, boom and yuloh roughed out, and a masthead light built from a waterproof LED strip wrapped around a circular plywood form.[2] By late September the spars, yuloh and stem were complete too, the stem cut with a chain saw.[13] Clear Perspex was chosen over tinted glazing, after tinted glazing had worsened a salt-smearing problem on an earlier boat.[14] By early November the boarding step and a hinged reefing rod were under way, worked at alongside the rest of the fit-out components in an effort to finish as many of them as possible before being forced to assemble the hull.[15]

Hull construction began in late November 2015: bulkhead 3, the first bulkhead cut, came out with straight sides only, missing the plans' 5 mm curve; the fix was overlapping cleats glued onto the bulkhead's aft face, so the filled gap would not show from the helm.[16][17] By late January 2015 the bulkhead cleats were still under way, and to her builder's own knowledge only one other New Zealand Paradox existed at the time, his the second.[18] By early April 2015 the rudder and stock were finished, one and a half times heavier than an earlier boat's solid stock and rudder, with six layers of woven roving wrapped fully around the lower gudgeon fitting, reasoned to work the same way steel bands work around a barrel, holding even once the epoxy bond had aged, so long as they went all the way round and the bolts and hull friction did the rest.[19] A full build-order list at that point ran: masthead light, mast, yard, yuloh, boom, lower gudgeon, a stainless-steel tack downhaul, the stem, the rudder cheek and rudder, three of the four bulkheads, the transom, the deck beams and the mast step, most of them taken only to 90-95% complete so each piece could still be checked against the assembled hull before final fitting; the mast step alone depends on bulkhead 2 being finished first.[20] A dry fit of the whole hull confirmed two hopes: the mast, spars and yuloh all stow neatly inside, and the cabin comes out just the right length to fit an air bed exactly, without sliding around in rougher conditions, though reaching the forepeak, already a known tight fit, was expected to get harder still once the deck went on.[21] The paint plan settled on white throughout for maximum light; the wiring plan called for permanently wired LEDs on the forward faces of bulkheads 1 and 2 and the aft face of bulkhead 4, plus one under the cabin hatch, each separately switched from the switchboard.[22] By June 2015, after a further dry fit, the mast was fitted into the vent box while it could still be positioned horizontally rather than dealt with vertically at the end; the internal wiring was secured against slapping with cable ties every 25 cm, drawn through an intentionally oversized 16 mm bottom hole so a draw wire could later pull fresh wiring back up the mast; the planned tinfoil lining for radar reflectivity was abandoned once tin foil turned out to be aluminium, which corrodes in salt conditions.[23]

"Mini" was assembled and trued in August 2015, most things fitting together as expected, with a fair amount of epoxy needed to fill gaps where the chine logs pass through the bulkheads.[24] Sheer clamps were fitted that September, alongside reasoning about her future cruising grounds: deep harbours, sounds, fiords, open water and large alpine lakes around Christchurch, quite unlike UK tidal slipways.[25] One sheer clamp broke loose overnight before it had been glued, at the bulkhead-1 crossing;[26] soaking a replacement length in wet towels for a few hours fixed it, though whether the same trick would work on the thicker chine logs remained an open question.[27] Days later, the chine logs were bent into place using the alternate-bulkhead-and-transom stem-fitting technique: unscrewing each side in turn to splay the hull open for the stem, then reassembling and gluing stem-first back toward the transom, with drywall screws driven in front of each clamp to stop it slipping on the stem's curved timber before gluing.[28] The chine logs themselves went in after sitting outside in the rain for a couple of days and being bent into place with clamps and a heat gun; a week's drying in place left them with more curve than intended, easily fixed by re-gluing.[29] The bilge and tank dividers were confirmed to need fitting before the bottom went on, since fitting the bottom first would have made it impossible to fit them afterward; even before the bottom was on, the hull was already, in her builder's words, "one hellava overbuilt boat" next to an earlier, much lighter boat that had "sailed over the water rather than through it."[30]

The bottom went on in early November 2015: suspended above the hull on G-clamps and tie-downs, wetted out with epoxy minus hardener while upside down, then bonded to the hull with thickened, hardener-added epoxy. Lowering the bow, a tie-down released without warning and the bottom rocketed toward the front of the shed, held only by hanging on to its front edge as tools crashed off shelves; both boat and builder came through unhurt. Getting the bottom on marked a watershed for the build: everything left, fitting out, fibreglassing, painting and getting her onto a trailer, was finishing work rather than further construction.[31] Bending the 19 mm bottom plywood around the rocker was recognised as some of the hardest work of the whole build.[32] Stem protection was fitted next, following a technique already established on the mailing list.[33] Late that November, researching the question and concluding that woven roving was old technology and nearly impossible to find locally, two layers of 400 gsm (14 oz) bi-axial cloth were substituted for the plans' three layers of 24 oz woven roving on the bottom glassing, coming out at about 3 mm rather than the recommended 4 mm, an acceptable tradeoff since bi-axial cloth's straight, uncrimped fibres are held to be stronger for a given thickness than roving's woven ones.[34]

A hailstorm while the hull sat outdoors unfinished was repaired with newsprint laid over the crack on both sides, clamped with plywood scrap squares screwed through the hull side and removed once cured, then covered inside and out with epoxy fabric.[35] By November 2016 the interior was finished, painted and fitted out, and work had moved to the cabin top. The sole hatch between bulkheads 1 and 2, housing the ballast-tank pump just forward of bulkhead 2, was kept for raising the sailor off the floor enough to reach the battery and see into the forepeak. A foredeck hatch was weighed and declined: with no hatch fitted at all the forward compartment stays reasonably dry, an advantage on a long trip, and a foredeck hatch would have ruled out a planned solar panel there instead.[36]

Four months later, in March 2017, fit forced a departure from the plans that had not been anticipated: at 5'10", it proved impossible to sit comfortably on the seat with the hatch open without hunching forward; either the rear cabin posts needed to move back another 150 mm or so, or the hatch needed a different arrangement. With the posts and three layers of 9 mm deck laminate already glued in place, the hatch was redesigned rather than the posts moved, drawing most of its inspiration from Sven Yrvind's own string-operated hatches.[37] Together, the stock cabin's floor area and hatchway suit a sailor up to about 5'10" without hunching; a builder any taller would need to move the posts instead.[36][38]

Her home town precisely stated, cost figures on both sides of the ledger: a mid-build guess of **NZD 8,000 (USD 5,500)**, made in mid-2015, nearly half of it for a bespoke trailer, reasoned from an earlier boat's lesson that the trailer makes the real difference to whether a boat gets used; and, once complete, an actual **NZD 6,500 all in**, including the trailer, at roughly 2018 prices, with a home-made polytarp sail and no outboard motor.[9][8] Built in nothing but marine plywood and West System epoxy despite a deliberately rough "work boat" paint finish, on the reasoning that a Paradox is a boat meant to last, and that if she were ever sold the materials underneath the paint should still be worth something.[9] Her own cost lesson points away from the big-ticket items toward what really adds up: litre upon litre of epoxy, the solar panel and controller, battery, switchboard, lighting, a pump, a stove, ground tackle and the rig's hardware, with the interior fit-out judged the item that most changes how much the boat is enjoyed and insulation the one most often left off such lists.[8] A build begun with no tools purpose-bought for it beyond what an earlier, simpler boat had already required ran, by her builder's own account elsewhere on the record, to at least two years even working full time, "for me it took four," a comparable US build and fit-out reckoned in 2022 at about **USD 4,000 plus another USD 2,000 for a proper trailer**.[39]

## The rig: from standing lug to sprit

An untried idea for a stronger, 2:1 halyard purchase, a second block fitted on the yard with an attachment point just below the masthead sheave, was floated during the build and independently already proven on "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)".[40] Matt Layden's own Mk1 sail was chosen for "Mini" over a Chinese-style rig, drawing a comparison from an earlier boat's forward-sloping mast and balanced lug.[41] An off-centre masthead pulley was solved by cutting the halyard slot as per plan but leaving it open rather than capping it, with a home-made LED masthead light mounted on the mast's side using a single extra-long bolt that does double duty as both the halyard block's bearing and the light's own mount, letting the halyard be lifted out entirely for a quiet night at anchor, relying on reefing tension to keep it seated in the slot under way.[42] A rudder-cheek notch was later cut for the sheet fairlead, chosen over a deck-and-baffle reroute already carrying enough blocks and sheaves.[43]

In February 2018, a firsthand windward-performance theory was set out: below about 10 knots of wind "Mini" made essentially no progress to windward, but performance improved steadily as wind speed rose, and in the strongest wind sailed to that point, 20 knots, she went to windward very well even reefed down slightly and into chop, a marked contrast with an earlier boat that made no headway upwind at all in the same conditions. The theory offered was that the hull shape itself, not the sail or rudder, does most of the work pulling the boat to windward, and only begins acting as a foil once heeled significantly and moving at 2.5 knots or better; the rudder was doubted to resist most of the leeway, since the boat only resisted leeway at the same significant heel angles where a rudder would be expected to work least well.[44] A hatch-mounted plexiglass view dome was declined on windage grounds: it would sit right where the sail comes when close-hauled with no reefs in, adding windage no sailor depending on the sail rather than a motor can spare.[45]

Ten days later the below-10-knot data point was retracted: the apparent stall had coincided with sailing up-harbour against an incoming tide, not a genuine hull or rig characteristic. The Mk1 sail was modified meanwhile to remove a persistent leech hook, and a second, 102 sq ft four-sided sprit sail was designed, sized to match the Mk1's own centre of effort rather than raise it, and sewn, with a plan to sail both and report which was preferred.[46]

**The sprit rig itself was built, sailed and adopted outright in April 2018**, in place of the standing lug rather than kept only as a side-by-side comparison. Performance improved on every point of sail, most markedly to windward in light air; tacking became reliable in all conditions and gybing stopped being an issue. The rig still stows entirely inside the boat, mast, sprit and boom each cut to 4 m, and its lines can be left permanently rigged in the vent box, with every line load, including the sheet, lighter than the standing lug's; the stowed spars also project less far behind the boat than before. The sail's centre of effort came out balanced, much as the standing lug's had been. The one real trade-off is reefing: rather than a graduated series of reef points, the rig has only three states, full sail, scandalised and fully struck, workable for sheltered-water sailing but a caution for open water; the other cost is four control lines to manage rather than two, leaving noticeably more rope inside the cabin than before.[4][5]

**A systematic, first-hand four-point comparison against the standing lug** followed in June and July 2018, checked against years sailing the lug beforehand. Tacking from starboard to port, once difficult, was no longer a problem at all. Roller reefing, which had deformed the standing lug's own block cheeks under the force needed to operate it, now took far less effort, the sprit's snotters still needing some force but nothing like the lug's roller reefing. A standing lug's characteristic "Chinese gybe" cannot happen with a sprit boom, removing gybing as a concern altogether. And pointing improved: a low-peaked standing lug depends on the sheet alone to hold leech tension and is prone to leech hooking, where the sprit's boom, yard and luff, all borne against the mast, give a more aerodynamic shape and noticeably better performance, particularly in light air. One respect still favoured the lug: in very strong wind a partially reefed standing lug keeps making some progress to windward on port tack, the yard pressing against the mast, where by the same point a sprit rig would already have been scandalised and could do no better than a beam reach, itself reachable only after building boat speed downwind, since scandalising moves the centre of effort far forward. With only three strong-wind outings under the sprit rig at that point against one under the lug, and the scandalising technique gotten right only once, the comparison was offered as provisional.[5]

The rig conversion turned up a further, unplanned benefit in December 2018: an unexpected self-steering effect from the sprit-rig reroute, which prompted early sketches of a trim-tab/wind-vane concept using a rod inserted into a hexagonal socket on the rudder's trim tab, with six position stops.[47] It was a considered turn: three years earlier, in May 2015, reading Sven Yrvind's own account of a downwind self-steering rig and weighing a "weather staysail" concept, a tiller pilot had been concluded the simpler solution to either, in her builder's own words at the time "a wild goose chase."[48]

A 3-day, 2-night camping trip to Lake Ellesmere in April 2018 tested a 12V computer-cooling-fan ventilation idea, refined yuloh technique, and found that the snotter on the new sprit boom can be adjusted to significantly improve light-air windward performance, alongside a 30-plus-knot survival account.[49] A further systematic set of five numbered sprit-rig tuning lessons followed that July: keep the sprit boom near horizontal; mount the yard and the sprit boom on the same side of the mast; the snotter is continuously adjustable for sail draft; a peak halyard controls sag and twist and doubles as a topping lift when scandalised; and the halyard's attachment point can be moved partway along the boom to shrink the sail deliberately for winds above 30 knots.[50] The same summer's sailing named Lyttelton Harbour, alongside Lake Ellesmere, as a recurring winter sailing ground.[18][51]

A furling-avoidance philosophy runs alongside the rig work: there is little call to furl while actually under way, since the pipe hoop the sail sits in creates virtually zero windage even unfurled; the real need to reef comes at anchor rather than mid-sail. Three anchoring scenarios were set out around dumping the whole rig into the hoop and tidying it up by hand once stationary: downwind of an offshore breeze, easing the halyard and letting the sail swing forward over the bow before snubbing the anchor to the rear cleat; into an onshore breeze, sculling ashore by yuloh to set a line before pulling forward against a dropped-back anchor; or simply near any hazard worth taking the whole rig in for. An earlier junk rig, sailed on a different boat, was judged only a "half pass" by the same test: quick enough to douse, but its lazy jacks left a reefed sail still draped over the cabin top directly above the sailor's head, with real windage.[52] A full junk-rig conversion was judged not worthwhile for a boat this size, at only about 100 sq ft too small to justify the added weight and windage of battens and running rigging, and the standard sheetlet arrangement would not work at all, since a Paradox's centre of lateral resistance sits so far aft the leech would fall behind the transom; a hybrid idea, lightweight flexible battens down the leech of the existing sail with fixed batten parrels, was floated but doubted worth the loss of the stock rig's clear boom at anchor and cabin-controlled sheeting.[53]

## Ballast and trim

The lower gudgeon was left unlined, on the reasoning that a lower gudgeon left completely unlined since 2008 elsewhere in the fleet had shown no noticeable wear; water ballast was chosen over the bottled ballast an earlier boat had carried, filling, emptying and stowing bottles under the seats there having been "a real hassle."[54] The planned electrical load was modest: a few LEDs, a 4W VHF radio, and an occasional laptop and floodlight, with an awareness that such loads "have a habit of multiplying."[55] The ballast-tank fill/vent/drain system went through several iterations as a thought experiment before settling: a single large-diameter pipe fixed flush to the tank wall, filled and drained through an ordinary garden hose, was floated first, omitting the plans' separate breather tubes on the reasoning that the wider pipe alone should pass enough air;[56] the pipe was then refined to exit near the tank's inside wall at the bottom on a slight downward angle, so it would clear the water once heeled 90 degrees or more;[57] and, a week later, having weighed the replies this drew, the plans' own breather-vent routing into the mast vent trunk was kept after all rather than deviated from, while a closer read of the plans found they nowhere actually route a fill/vent pipe onto the deck at all, the rainwater-filling idea likely coming from Don Elliott's building manual rather than Matt Layden's own plans; the final scheme settled on a garden-hose snap-attachment fitting and manual on/off valve inside the boat, plumbed through a further pair of garden hoses and a 12V self-priming pump, reversed to fill or empty.[58]

That correction was independently confirmed in June 2016: the rain-filled-ballast-tank concept sits in Don Elliott's building manual, not in Matt Layden's actual plans, which show only deck-mounted fillers, and filling from a salt-laden deck would in any case contaminate the fresh-water supply.[59]

Ballast placement followed the plans' own default: 400 lb of lead aft of bulkhead 3, with a 40 lb deep-cycle battery just forward of bulkhead 2, and a heavy anchor and chain stowed forward of bulkhead 2 to starboard specifically to balance the battery, an everyday anchor kept in a bucket to port behind bulkhead 4.[60] Casting that 400 lb of lead into ingots that April took two days and yielded 300 lb of usable ingots once the rudder's own lead pocket had been topped up and the dross skimmed off; low-pressure LPG alone could not melt it, a brick-and-frypan forge with a vacuum-cleaner draught and dry firewood nearly did the job unaided, and a propane torch finished the largest lumps, with nothing heavier than 15 lb safely poured from a 7 lb frypan; the fumes off painted lead demanded a proper vapour-filter mask, goggles and heavy gloves, one ingot picked up too soon burning fingers even so.[61]

The ballast pump system was built and proven: a reversible, self-priming 12V diaphragm pump, moving 7.6 litres a minute (2.6 amps), filling or draining both tanks in about ten minutes; hoses and valves stow in a side bin, a 6 m hose still able to self-prime from a bucket on the ground, the same plumbing rigged the other way by reversing a set of Y-valves.[62] A separate fresh-water pump, chosen for its gentle flow, was mounted under the forward stateroom floor, filled using a bucket as an intermediary, likened to a header tank feeding a low-pressure hot-water cylinder.[63]

At her first sea trial she sat deeper in the water than expected under **135 kg of lead ballast** and trimmed a touch stern-heavy, perhaps a little too much lead having gone into the rudder.[3] Trim theory settled afterward on a correctly trimmed boat sitting slightly bow-down rather than level or bow-up, the rudder-cheek notch cut for the sheet fairlead confirmed working, as a side effect, as a tiller lock instantly overridden when running.[64] Photographic evidence of that bow-down trim, the transom just kissing the water, the waterline at the rudder bolt, the bow fully immersed, was achieved by moving about **30 kg of lead forward of bulkhead 2**.[65]

A ballast-tank rot-risk question was clarified in May 2019: the tanks themselves are low-risk, but the real risk sits at bulkhead 3's area and at the bulwarks, which catch rainwater when the boat is trailered at the wrong angle.[66] The ballast-pump plumbing was refined in January 2021: the centrifugal pump was moved ahead of the manual diaphragm primer rather than behind it, since the diaphragm proved self-sealing plumbed the other way round, and every hose joint was changed from quick-release rings to permanent clamps for reliability.[67]

Water ballast capacity is **70 L** as designed.[7] Years on, the tank water was judged drinkable at a pinch, the epoxy having cured for at least a couple of years, though it has never actually had to be relied on; towing with the water ballast in gives a smoother ride than towing empty.[68]

## Systems, fit-out and the smaller departures

Storage proved tight after fit-out was nearly complete, once the stove, battery, anchors and pump plumbing were all in place, ruling out an ocean crossing on stowage grounds alone even while the build overall was judged a success.[69] No foredeck hatch and no plexiglass view dome were both declined, on dryness and windage grounds respectively.[36][45] Toileting practice, offered as a plain practical fact rather than anything private, runs to a bucket the one time far enough offshore to use one, and otherwise holding on, or a single-use sealed container emptied ashore.[70] A yuloh, while it was carried, seats on its pivot only under way and floats off the moment the boat stops, a coordination that took a while to learn and still fails occasionally, but that works even in fairly rough conditions once mastered.[71]

The yuloh itself was eventually abandoned. In January 2019, the kahikatea (New Zealand white pine) yuloh warped and lost its built-in curve; a conventional paddle suited the boat's narrow-hull, alternating-side technique better and replaced it for good.[10] A tiller pilot was found necessary after long open-water runs, sheltered-water sailing not needing one.[72] The mainsheet was rerouted from the plans' downward-sloping tube into the cabin to a small hole through the back window with a clam cleat just forward of it; the reason for the change, established years later on a 2022 cruise, was that the plans' route let sheet moisture drip onto the stove and into the bilge, rusting stored cans.[73][74] The vent box is singled out as the cleverest single detail in the whole design.[75] Moving the hull around the shop settled on a low-tech answer after trying a wheeled platform: a square of carpet dragged across the floor, with 25 mm electrical conduit used as rollers to get her out of the shed and onto the trailer.[76]

Trailer bunk layout weighed transverse bunks under bulkheads 2 and 4, the approach an earlier, unballasted boat had used, against a transverse bunk under bulkhead 1 paired with two longitudinal bunks centred under bulkhead 3 and the chines, reasoning that U-shaped transverse bunks would self-secure the boat once loaded where longitudinal bunks would need tie-downs.[77] New Zealand's unbraked-trailer weight limit, under 2,000 kg, proved far more generous than the UK's 750 kg; a custom-built trailer priced out as unrealistic, so a secondhand one was modified instead.[78] A neighbour's free, if well-used, secondhand trailer solved the problem shortly after.[79] Towing weight is about **1200 lb**, the trailer's springs the limiting factor per its manufacturer.[80]

## How she sails

Hull speed has been measured, not guessed, across six years on the water. A 4.5-knot average has been held for long periods, but 5 knots has never been sat on continuously; in this builder's experience it takes about 12 knots of wind to reach hull speed, and by then the sea state means the boat is surging and pausing as the waves overtake her, since almost every wave in a seaway travels faster than 5 knots. Six knots has been exceeded only while surfing down a wave front, and once, caught in a gale under full rig running downwind in 25-plus knots, the boat hit 6 knots several times while "verging on being uncontrollable."[11] From that experience comes a speculative theory, offered explicitly as speculation: that Matt Layden drew the tightly tucked-up stern specifically to stop the boat surfing; an earlier boat "would surf big-time" and broach, where a Paradox by comparison "is very sedate," holding her line, accelerating briefly and then squatting as the wave face passes under her.[11]

No bottom stringers have been added for beach protection, judged unnecessary on a hull that is "anything but light... as heavy as hell," where the extra drag would only pile onto what the chine runners already cost; she is thought of as "a miniature keeler, not a dinghy" rather than a dinghy hull needing that kind of protection, and, having beached her many times, anchoring out is now increasingly preferred instead, since a beached boat left unattended is an invitation to vandalism rather than a structural risk.[68] The bottom does not pound or slap, attributed to the bluff bow, the massive stem, the submerged forefoot and the chine runners, in contrast to an earlier, lightly built sharpie that pounded badly.[81]

A second sea trial ten days after the first, in stronger wind, found that tacking from starboard onto port proved impossible, catching in irons every attempt, where gybing onto port instead lost very little ground; settling from that trial on into tacking only onto starboard and gybing onto port. Past four knots the boat made no pounding at all, only a rushing sound as the bow wave built, and sitting so deep in the water gave the sensation of going through the water rather than over it.[82] A cold-weather overnight trial the following July tested how cold a closed cabin could get and stay comfortable: waking to frost on the ground and feeling a little cold, though the back rather than the temperature was judged the real problem; condensation covered the windows by morning, but the air stayed dry inside, with a light draught felt over the face while sleeping. The one design shortcoming the trial found was the cooker sitting beneath the mainsheet: no trouble while cooking, but the sheet, wet from sailing and hauled in through the transom's sheet-entry hole, sheds most but not all of its moisture, some still dripping onto the cooker's edge. The same trial exposed a second wear point: the transom baffle's edge takes heavy abrasion loading and unloading the mast, spars and yuloh, and a single layer of fibreglass tape laid there in anticipation proved nowhere near enough, needing serious reinforcement, perhaps a layer of tin.[83] The fix adopted was a length of plastic tube, slit and glued on over the edge.[84]

A full two-day comparative sail against a fibreglass Whiting 16 dinghy on a lake, in April 2019, outpointed the dinghy to windward in light air on port tack; the second day's results on starboard tack were less clear-cut. A tow was offered and given during the same outing.[85] The same trial produced third-party praise for how fast the sprit rig assembles: its spars, mast, sprit and boom, 4 m each, were confirmed to stow entirely inside the boat.[86]

The Lake Ellesmere Triangular Race Course, run in April 2020, was a deliberate, GPS-tracked, one-mile equilateral-triangle test built specifically to check exaggerated hull-speed claims circulating on the mailing list against measurement: "I say codswallop... let's examine the facts rather than the fantasy."[10] Sustained 5.5-6.1 knot downwind speeds were recorded on video at Lake Ellesmere in June 2023, exceeding the nominal 5 kt hull speed without planing.[87] Engine-free sailing changes how the whole day on the water is spent, trading some range for improved sailing ability: "you get much better at ghosting!"[88] Lyttelton Harbour has been named specifically as a recurring winter sailing ground in both 2018 and 2023.[18][51]

## Launch and sea trials, December 2017

After three and a half years' building, thought at the outset to take about eighteen months, Mini went for her first test sail in mid-December 2017, on a small lake about an hour's drive from home, with a night planned aboard as part of the same trip.[89][3] Impatience moved the actual outing forward a day or two ahead of schedule.[90] Advice offered ahead of the trip cautioned that a first-time sailor should expect a Paradox to feel lively and tip more than a wider-hulled boat on first boarding, and that keeping notes on an early outing helps set the trim as wanted.[91] Time was already short before a longer cruise planned over the New Year, so the plan was to work on the rig ashore over the following few days and see what could be improved before setting out again.[92]

The trip report that followed was candid in both directions. Under **135 kg of lead ballast** she sat deeper in the water than expected and trimmed a touch stern-heavy, but felt notably sedate at the helm against an earlier, lighter boat: left untouched, the rudder held a course for a long stretch rather than rounding up into the wind almost at once. She proved bone dry inside, the only moisture brought aboard on sandals. Several systems worked exactly as built: the gimballed alcohol cooker, the ballast-tank filling and emptying pump, a push-up ceiling hatch on ropes that opens both fore and aft, the electrics and lighting, the rudder and its lifting crane, the trailer, and stowing the yuloh, rig and mast inside the hull, including an open masthead sheave that lets the halyard be lifted out at anchor to stop it slapping overnight. Fit proved tighter than roomy: at 5'10", the floor area was a little too small, ruling the stock cabin out for anyone 6 ft or taller; there was nowhere comfortable to sit except against the tilted seat back with the hatch closed; and the underfloor hatches were fiddly to reach. Two things needed sorting: yuloh technique, the figure-eight stroke taking some finding, and roller reefing, which proved a real struggle.[3] The second sea trial's precise location was **Lake Hood, South Island**.[93]

Advice on the trim and the roller-reefing struggle followed quickly: to check trim, sit in the boat and have someone read the waterline against the plans' own marks; the floor area can be eased by cutting out the bottom of the hatchway to the forward stateroom flush with the floor; splitting the hatch lid just forward of the seat lets the forward section alone open under way; the yuloh needs practice, "you know you've got it sorted when some stranger says 'that looks easy'"; and a topping lift supporting both ends of the boom, keeping it off the cabin top while hoisting or reefing, takes most of the hard work out of roller reefing.[94] Further exchange on the same struggle worked through dropping the topping lift for a pulley in place of the stock thimble, feeding a further pulley into the vent trunk, and reefing in small stages; the stock reefing thimble was eventually removed entirely without real ill effect beyond a cosmetic jump near full hoist, and a short lazy jack was trialled above the reefing roller hoop instead, with promising early results.[95]

The second sea trial, ten days after the first, addressed nearly all of the first outing's concerns. Tacking from starboard onto port proved impossible, catching in irons every attempt, so the boat settled into tacking only onto starboard and gybing onto port, which lost very little ground. Past four knots the boat made no pounding at all, only a rushing sound as the bow wave built, and sitting so deep in the water gave the sensation of going through the water rather than over the top of it (see [How she sails](#how-she-sails) for the fuller performance record this trial began).[82] Settled yuloh-pin practice followed from the same exchange: a pin raked 20-30 degrees toward the stern, with a second hole closer to the handle for a deliberate "overdrive" gear, and a tacking technique of sailing more aggressively before the turn, some temporary forward ballast or shifted body weight and timing the turn with a wave crest.[96]

## The Nelson expedition, January 2018

In early January 2018, Mini made her first extended cruise: six days and five nights away, with a video walkthrough posted first and the sailing footage following once the seasickness the first days brought had passed.[97]

The written report, covering 28 December 2017 to 2 January 2018, opens with a 450 km drive to Nelson and a free visitor's berth. The first leg, a night crossing of 25 nautical miles that a predicted eight hours stretched to thirteen, was the first-ever night sail: out of the harbour by yuloh at 9:30 pm as the wind died, then a bout of severe seasickness through the dark hours, unable to read without retching, with no VHF training to call the harbourmaster and a rising breeze forcing a course north instead of turning back. By dawn, some seven nautical miles further offshore than expected and awake 27 hours, a sea breeze allowed a run down through the Astrolabe Roadstead to anchor at Tinline Bay, where the falling tide left the boat high and dry to a small crowd of curious day-trippers. Yulohing away from Adele Island on 31 December, a 20-knot nor'wester brought the roughest sailing of the trip: a wave breaking clean over the boat confirmed the hatch modification kept the interior bone dry despite a small leak at the top of the front window, and the seasickness did not return. The same day surfaced a marked tacking asymmetry, good pointing to windward on port tack, unable to point at all on starboard, matching a pattern seen before on other lug rigs where light air favours one tack and heavy air the other. A difficult gybe from starboard to port produced a bad tangle particular to a standing lug, the gybing yard's own upward pressure letting wind get under the reversed sail and throw it into a vertical mess up the mast, first floating the idea of a conversion to a bald-headed sprit rig with a loose-footed boom (see [The rig](#the-rig-from-standing-lug-to-sprit) for the conversion this idea eventually became). New Year's Eve was spent anchored in an estuary, undisturbed until midnight fireworks and shouting from ashore. On 1 January a 15-knot northerly, met on a broad reach with an already-rigged gaff vang, added about half a knot of boat speed and cut the tendency to roll, before the wind died to a two-knot ghost across Tasman Bay for hour after hour. An evening breeze carried the boat back into Nelson Harbour at 4-5 knots over the ground with a fair tide, tacking up the harbour and ghosting straight into the visitors' berth under sail; cooking dinner that evening under the closed hatch in the rain confirmed a cabin-ventilation shortfall. The boat came out and was trailered home on 2 January.[98]

## A grounding on the Christchurch Estuary, October 2018

An October 2018 outing on the Christchurch Estuary became, in her builder's own words, "the day things went wrong." A sail-raising mistake and a failed tack into the bar led to an overnight dry-out, then an 18-plus knot headwind departure that grounded the boat on a sandbar. Ballast was pumped out to lighten her enough to free her, but once light she could not point without it, a finding that clarified how the centre of effort moves forward once the rig is scandalised, worsening pointing precisely when ballast is light. She was ultimately freed and brought ashore by wading.[99]

## An offshore trial, September 2019

In September 2019 the blue-water question was tested with a deliberate overnight trial. An initial plan to go 100 miles offshore, then 50, was scaled back to a single night 10 nautical miles out once the real time involved (three or four days for the fuller versions) and the exposure to six-metre-plus Southern Ocean swells beyond Banks Peninsula's shelter sank in; even at 10 miles, onlookers predicted capsize, structural failure and death by exposure or drowning. The passage ran down-harbour past Quail Island to a waypoint off Kaiapoi at an average of **2.5 knots over 8 hours**, a figure generalised as the real planning speed for any Paradox passage, since the boat's 5-knot maximum is rarely sustained. The night was ridden out lying ahull under a homemade drogue, 50 m of line between the two stern cleats threaded through 5 m of garden hose; the drogue's motion caused seasickness within five minutes and left the boat's sailor incapacitated on the cabin floor until midnight, worried about shipping traffic and drift. Woken at half past midnight to find the boat already 4 nautical miles closer to Southshore beach, a scrap of scandalised sail raised with the drogue still trailing let her broad-reach clear at about three-quarters of a knot and settle enough for sleep to resume. In 36 hours the passage covered 40 nautical miles including the overnight drift, averaging just over a knot, with paddling needed only twice, once for a missed tack and once to clear a windless patch near Godley Head. The conclusions: a drogue only earns its keep together with active sailing, not lying ahull, and a Paradox cannot make way to windward under one at all; the open sea itself proved simply boring, seasickness the only real discomfort, at about half a day; and despite the exposure, the boat itself never once felt unsafe, only the traffic of other vessels did.[20]

A video of the passage followed the same week.[100] The scandalised rig went up only once seasickness had already set in, the boat "bouncing around all over the place" beforehand, which is also why no footage exists of that roughest stretch; sleep came as a surprise even while seasick, needing two days ashore afterward to catch up on what was lost. On ordinary trips to sheltered anchorages the first night at anchor is typically slept badly and "like a baby" from then on, and the enclosed cabin proved comfortable specifically for keeping draughts away.[101]

## A week cruising Abel Tasman, January 2020

In January 2020, Mini spent a week cruising **Abel Tasman National Park**, a stretch that ran from a 6.5-hour drive down on 12 January to the drive home on the 18th.

Launching into the **Marahau Estuary** as the tide began to ebb, she grounded on a sandbank while still weighing whether it was safe to go out into a 20-knot southeasterly, in a region with roughly a 4-metre tidal range where "things happen very quickly"; the sandbank made the decision instead. The wait was used to map the channel on the free OpenCPN charting app, which paid off on the return leg. Underway on the next high tide just after midnight, she paddled out of the estuary by masthead light and chartplotter, sailed clear of Sandy Bay as the wind eased to 12 knots, then ghosted the last of it into the Adele Island anchorage at 2:30 am, again navigating by masthead light through the moored fleet, two hours' passage at 2 knots.

The next day supplied the trip's real test. A light southerly died within the hour, leaving the boat becalmed and seasick for two hours off Adele Island, before a 15-knot southeasterly arrived without warning and sent her charging along at hull speed, the front deck regularly washed as she ploughed into the backs of the waves. Shifting body weight from side to side made no difference at all, "such a heavy craft," and once the day's tourist boats fled the building swell, the coast was left clear, if for two keelers, one of them motor-sailing past at 7 knots in what were judged perfect sailing conditions. Off Totaranui with surf up on the beach and only 30 minutes of high tide left, the gap was shot rather than waited for, the wind behind judged strong enough to beat the ebb; surfing over the bar the boat spun to starboard with the sail still to port, until the deep rudder dug in and brought her straight, sailing on sedately to anchor off the shore. That leg took six hours at 2.2 knots.

After a rest day, the passage back turned into an all-nighter: leaving Totaranui on the evening high in a dying 8-knot southeasterly, the boat was embayed off Awaroa Bay by early evening, working every puff offshore against a leftover swell too big to paddle into, and spent four hours in the dark with winds coming from every corner of the compass and dying within minutes. A 15-knot easterly finally filled in off Tonga Island at 11 pm and the passage resumed at speed in total darkness with no moon, the final approach into the rock-strewn **Astrolabe Roadstead** flown entirely on the chartplotter. The wind eased in the island's lee, and only the sight of moored boats' masthead lights signalled arrival; ashore at 12:30 am, the leg had taken nine and a half hours at 1.4 knots, three and a half hours longer than the same passage run the other way.

Visiting a friend's 33 ft steel junk-rigged ketch moored nearby, a turn in the ketch's inflatable dinghy left a newly sharpened sense of "the exceptional stability of a Paradox" by comparison, and hearing out everything the ketch still needed done to her left a growing disenchantment with the idea of a keeler: Mini does the same job for far less expense, and sits in the garage, ignorable, between sails, not so a keeler, with a maintenance task always waiting. She was sailed off scandalised for the friend's benefit, then deliberately upwind scandalised too, only slightly worse for it than fully rigged, passing the ketch as its owner weighed anchor and keeping pace alongside at about 4 knots down the roadstead long enough for the two boats to film each other under way. The trip back up the Marahau channel was flown by the waypoints logged into OpenCPN at low tide on the way out; the ramp itself proved so shallow that the tow vehicle had to be unhitched and the trailer pushed the last 20 metres into the water by hand.[102]

Two further videos followed the written account, and, pressed on the disconnect between what a video shows and what a sailor actually feels aboard, the motion was called "just one of the many trade-offs when Paradoxing": a boat this short pitches and rolls even in quite small chop, a fact not appreciated before the build began. It happens at anchor as much as under way, which is why the most sheltered anchorage available is always sought; taking morning tea aboard a friend's 32 ft keeler moored in the same anchorage, the wind was assumed from below to have dropped, only to find on deck that it had not, the felt calm being the bigger boat's own steadiness rather than a change in the weather.[103]

## The Tauranga Harbour cruise, February 2021

A four-day cruise of Tauranga Harbour in February 2021, sailed with a companion in a junk-rigged Monarch 17, was caught overnight in a rain-swollen river running about 7 knots, with real concern about logs in the current. Third-party footage from the trip revealed the boat sitting lower in the water than expected, "a sailing submarine," with the bow wave running higher up the bow than expected too.[21]

## The Queen Charlotte Sound cruises, 2022 and 2023

A five-day liveaboard holiday to **Queen Charlotte Sound**, 28 February to 5 March 2022, refined the trailer-launch technique further, the bunks now aligned to the chine-runner track, and introduced a provisioning checklist system distinguishing day-trip from overnight lists. Water ballast was used as drinking water as a deliberate test during the same cruise. The trip also finally explained the cause behind the earlier mainsheet reroute: the plans' original under-deck route let sheet moisture drip onto the stove and into the bilge, rusting stored cans.[22][73]

A second Queen Charlotte Sound cruise, five days and four nights, ran from **Picton to Blumine Island** in August 2023, described afterward as the most relaxing sailing holiday on record; a drop-off arranged specifically at Picton removed the usual worry about leaving a tow vehicle unattended.[23]

## The 2024 refit

By 2023, five years' regular use had left the boat needing paint.[104] Getting her off the trailer without a crane used four 2000 kg truck tie-downs, two doing the work and two as back-up, eased onto old carpet.[105] Paint stripper removed 90% of the old paint, an orbital sander taking the rest; the finding worth keeping precisely is what the stripper did not touch, the underlying **West System epoxy proving completely impervious** to it even after sitting more than a day, the plywood beneath "just like new" where exposed. Another layer of epoxied cloth was applied properly this time, the boat on her side rather than upright so the fabric stayed wetted out, followed by eight coats of paint.[106] The whole refit's own honest verdict: "not even close to gold plated, definitely work grade, but still a very solid build."[107]

## Disambiguation

"Mini" is not to be confused with the informal "Mini Paradox" nickname once used for [Matt Layden's own Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md), a naming episode from 2005 that predates this hull by nearly a decade, nor with an unrelated, unbuilt scale-down proposal also informally called "mini Paradox." Neither carries any material about this boat.
