# Little Jim

**"little Jim"** is the name [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) (Yeovil, England) gave his own [Paradox](/paradox.md), which he had been building and reporting on in detail since **July 2001** (see [Alastair](/people/alastair-law.md) for the full build-progress narrative: garage logistics, panel layout, hull assembly, spars, rigging, and trailer planning). The name surfaced once the boat was far enough along to be shown off with a photo gallery, over a year into construction.[1]

## A year into the build: topping lift and furling (autumn 2002)

In September 2002, over a year into the build, Alastair posted a set of **construction photographs** to his own website, `www.asjg.clara.co.uk/little_jim/little_jim.htm` — a fuller, purpose-built gallery distinct from the CATIA CAD-model renderings he had posted earlier that year (see [notching the stem for chine logs and sheer clamps](/construction/stem-notching.md)), and it became a standing reference build log other UK builders returned to throughout their own projects.[1][2]

Fitting a **topping lift** before the deck goes on is the recommended sequence, since reefing without one had already proved difficult on other boats (see [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md) for the full topping-lift history).[3] Alastair also floated an untested **theory** for how [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) furls without a topping lift: if the radius where the furling line comes off the drum is significantly larger than the radius of the sail rolled up on the boom, the boom will naturally want to roll upward and support itself during reefing (see [furling system — field test](/components/rig/furling.md)).[4]

About a week later, Alastair collected his sail and gave the boat its first hands-on rigging trial, hoisting it with a temporary furling arrangement of his own. It worked but was impractical — lowering the sail had to be done in 3 in steps and gave him blisters — confirming, on "little Jim" herself, the same three-hands/no-topping-lift difficulty already documented from other builders' sea trials.[5] Alastair then fitted a topping lift of similar but simpler geometry: a single line over a masthead block, splitting to each end of the boom, using **1/8 in** line for the lifting portion and **1/4 in** soft yacht braid for the hauling portion and the rest of his running rigging, for easier handling.[5][6] See [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md) for the fuller cross-reference to the wider topping-lift history. That October, a fellow list member asked to see the boat in person; Alastair pointed him to the website instead, and the two would not actually meet face to face until 2004 (see below).[7][8][9]

## Decking over, and a side-deck plans error resolved (November 2002)

By mid-November 2002 the **fore and aft decks** were fitted, with the **side decks** due on next. Fitting the mast partners raised a protection question, resolved by a no-trouble report from another builder's boat and a leather-collar fix (see [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md)).[10][11][12]

Alastair also flagged a genuine **plans error**: the two side decks and two cabin end panels did not appear to fit a single 4 ft-wide sheet of ply, since the deck width itself exceeds 4 ft.[10] Closer measurement narrowed the mismatch to **2 of the 6 end pieces**; re-measuring against sheet #3 of the plans confirmed the foredeck's maximum width comes to 3 ft 11 in, and once the deck crown is added a builder following the plans exactly will probably go over 4 ft. This confirmed the tight fit as a genuine plans issue rather than a misreading, and left a proven fix — a small scabbed-on addition at each side, reaching the gunwale edge — for Alastair and any later builder reaching the same stage. See [panel layout and nesting — resolved](/construction/panel-layout.md) for the fuller technical write-up.[13][14]

## Weigh-in, and a full build-cost accounting (January–March 2003)

In January 2003, with her **hull finished and glassed** and cabin work just starting, "little Jim" weighed **just under 600 lb** against a **1,400 lb** designed displacement — meaning roughly half her finished weight was still to come as stores, ballast, and crew.[15] See [weight, ballast, and displacement — a fourth weight thread](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for the fuller thread this figure touched off.

Two months later, in March 2003, Alastair reached a major landmark: all the actual **making** of "little Jim" was complete, with only paint and fitting-out left to do. He gave the fullest single **cost and schedule accounting** on record for a Paradox build: plans ordered April 2001, tools and materials bought two months later, steady but unhurried work throughout; **boat cost £2,751.16** (including most of the power tools used); a **custom-built trailer, £1,502.12**, designed to his own requirements — simple single-handed launch/recovery without wetting the trailer, launching on a flat beach at low tide, and severe width limits for home storage; a **total build cost of roughly £4,253**, the fullest combined cost figure the archive records for a single Paradox project from plans purchase to launch. See [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md) for other builders' cost/time data points. Alastair hoped for a launch over Easter 2003.[16]

## Launch: "It floats! It's watertight! It keeps bumping into things!!" (19 April 2003)

Alastair launched "little Jim" from the slip at **Ridge Wharf boatyard**, on the river Frome off **Poole Harbour**, on 19 April 2003. His **custom trailer** proved a success at both launch and haul-out, needing only "a few mods" to be "well nigh perfect." The weather did not cooperate — a hot, light-wind day beforehand gave way to a cold force-6 blow for the launch itself.[17]

Testing her **stability** on deck, hanging onto the mast and leaning out, Alastair could not tell whether he'd reached 10 degrees of list or something closer to 45 degrees — the same ambiguous on-deck lean-out test other builders had also run without a clear result (compare [Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)'s own inconclusive dockside lean, where the mast could plausibly be dipped in the water)[18] — and decided to wait for calmer weather to test further. He also tried the **yuloh** for the first time: with the wind about 45 degrees off the bow, the bow kept blowing off toward the next berth (no damage, the berths being empty), and he judged coordinating sculling and steering simultaneously difficult with a pontoon rapidly approaching — he planned a leg-operated tiller extension to free both hands for the yuloh. He wondered whether his boat's center of gravity, known to be 1.8 m forward of the transom, sat too far aft.[17]

The stability question fit **the common Paradox pattern of an unballasted launch**: "not enough ballast to sink the paradox to its lines… bobbing on top with no chine leverage for directional stability and no immersion to cause stability" — consistent with the design's known sensitivity to ballast and stores for proper trim (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — an unloaded launch and a stability diagnosis](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)).[19] "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," sailed light with no water aboard, likewise "did just fine" though she looked "a little low in the water" (see [Zoë — sailing light, and a tiller stop](/boats/zoe.md)).[20]

The day after launch, Alastair worked out from bathroom-scale readings and calculation that "little Jim" had been only about **20 kg (44 lb) lighter than her design weight** at launch — a much smaller shortfall than the general "not enough ballast" diagnosis suggested — and now suspected the stability trouble owed as much to **conditions as to trim**: "I think the main problem was just too much wind. I am not sure I could have rowed my dinghy very easily in those conditions." This is the most precise known-shortfall ballast figure on record for an unloaded Paradox. He planned to carry the 20 kg as movable ballast on his next trip, and described his trailer as **"a simple design of parallel rollers."**[21][22]

## First sail: "Whooee!! what a boat!" (23 April 2003)

Four days after launch, Alastair took "little Jim" out under sail for the first time, giving the fullest single day-sail account from any UK builder to date. He sculled out of the marina in light wind, got the hang of the yuloh heading down the river to open water, and found the trick was not to try to go too fast, sailing with the rudder half-up — with the rudder fully raised he had no steering control at all and kept rounding up into the wind, but with it half up (horizontal) he had full control. He noticed a crease across the sail's top corner and correctly diagnosed it as sitting too far aft on the yard — the same kind of yard/sail-fit issue **Matt Layden** had diagnosed on another builder's sail weeks earlier (see [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md)).[23]

He sailed to the ferry terminal, across to Pottery Pier on Brownsea Island, up the Upper Wych Channel, and ghosted on a falling tide into the marshes of Middlebere Lake, anchoring for the night. A frost formed on deck and sails by morning despite only a thin sleeping bag — "after all I had the insulation of a Paradox around me" — and a misty, nearly calm morning still yielded 2 kn on the GPS. Later, with wind returned from the NE at F3-4, he clocked **over 5 kn on the GPS** (unconfirmed until measured with a log line) circumnavigating the harbour, before reefing for the final stretch home up the winding River Frome.[23]

**Verdict:** "I am delighted!" The boat held a course for up to 2 miles with the tiller locked, showed no visible leeway, heeled about 20 degrees at maximum (more usually 10), and was forgiving of a badly-set sail. He got hung up tacking twice and planned to move ballast further aft. Internally he found "ample room" for his own frame, though access forward of bulkhead 1 was tight once his bed-roll was laid out.[23]

On tell-tale use: ease the sheet until the luff just flutters or the belly just luffs, then sheet in only enough to firm the sail — over-sheeting, a common fault, was the likely cause of his tacking trouble.[24] This is the fullest account yet of a Paradox actually being sailed, as opposed to launched or sculled, by a builder other than Matt Layden, and confirmed "little Jim" handled well under sail from her very first outing.

## Third outing: a two-day cruise to Brownsea and Bournemouth (May 2003)

Two weeks later, Alastair gave the fullest single cruising account yet for "little Jim." Over two days on Poole Harbour he: launched at Ridge Wharf, sculling out and hoisting sail in open water at 4.7 mph / 4.1 kn by GPS; reached Brownsea Island in under an hour, rounded the south side through "Blood Alley," and anchored in the shallows while scouting conditions beyond the harbour entrance; sailed close-hauled to **Green Island** in a freshening wind at a steady **30 degrees of heel** — "did not feel dangerous," though he reefed out of caution, noting to himself "must do that capsize test!"; anchored off Green Island overnight and nearly ran aground the next morning after misjudging **Poole's unusual double-high-water tide pattern**, using the yuloh to push clear as the flat bottom simply slid over the mud; and ran a deliberate **speed trial** to Bournemouth Pier and back — a precise 4-mile GPS leg each way, close-hauled in about F3, averaging **3.7 kn** over the full 8 miles. A detour into Studland Bay for an ice cream (none found) cost him a foul wind and adverse tide home, arriving "only five and a half hours late." He closed with a now-familiar observation — passersby everywhere stopping to ask about the boat and its designer — and reflected the trip had been genuinely educational as well as satisfying and "great fun."[25]

## The landmark voyage: a 27-mile passage to Portland and back (May 2003)

Ten days later, Alastair announced "little Jim"'s first long-distance cruise: a 4-day, **27-mile-each-way** trip from Poole Harbour to Portland Harbour and back, choosing Portland partly because it has no significant tidal entrance window — a useful safety margin given he still had little sense of what average speed to expect in open water.[26] Forecast wind looked to be on the nose with rain expected, but he was not deterred.[26] Ahead of departure, a weather and tidal-race briefing counselled staying at least 2 miles south of the Portland Race or, alternatively, cutting inside close to the Bill of Portland with the tidal slot timed right;[27][28] Alastair delayed his own departure a day to wait for a better wind angle.[29]

Alastair returned four days later and posted the full write-up — the fullest single cruising narrative on record to date — opening: **"This is the story of the landmark voyage of Little Jim. It is a story of high emotion, of elation and despair."**[30][31] Per the Shell Pilot to the English Channel, the passage was 27 nautical miles each way; the wind held a steady F3-4 S to SW for the whole trip.[31]

- **Day 1:** Launched into the river, ran aground briefly on the bank before reaching open water. A rain shower let him test "the main bad weather feature of the Paradox design" — going below and shutting the hatch — which steamed up the windows in the hot, humid cabin, "a bit of a worry" in a busy harbour; he avoided other traffic by ducking into **shallows where other boats couldn't go**, using the design's shoal draft as a practical collision-avoidance tool. He anchored for the night near Brownsea Island.[31]
- **Day 2 (the passage):** Underway before dawn, past the chain ferry, running a broad reach past Handfast Point and the Peveril Ledges before turning close-hauled off Durlston Head and Anvil Point — where he was seasick, an outcome he was careful to note was "not a reflection on the Paradox," merely "an arrangement I have with Neptune." Passing **St Albans Head's tidal race** about 2 miles off, near neaps but with wind against tide, green water broke steadily over "little Jim" — enough to wash loose the boot-topping tape on both bows — but she "never really felt in any danger and were still sailing well," and not a drop of water came through the hatch; the only water below came in through the deck vent and drained straight back out, or was tracked in on his own shoes. The tide turned against him within 2 miles of Portland, costing three hours, before he anchored for the night in the SW corner of Portland Harbour.[31]
- **Ashore at Portland**, Alastair made contact with a local who had an interest in a Paradox, and the two talked for half an hour — the only recorded in-person meeting prompted by the [UK Paradox interest](/people/jeff-doryman.md) then circulating on the list.[31]
- **Day 3 (the return):** Left Portland with the wind freshening from the SW and the tide against, staying close inshore to avoid a strong offshore current. The tide turned favourable again by St Albans Head, giving an easy, dry passage back. Off Chapmans Pool, a **Drascombe Lugger** that had motored out ahead hoisted sail — and "little Jim" **overtook her**, "the first time we have overtaken anything!" — before timing the chain ferry exactly right and anchoring for the night back in Poole Harbour.[31]
- **Day 4:** Sculled up the river against the tide, hauled out at the boatyard, and was home in time for lunch.[31]
- **Verdict:** **"We did it! The feeling of satisfaction is incredible. I now know that we can go anywhere we like around the British coast."** The seas off St Albans Head were rougher than he'd have liked, especially while queasy, but "as Little Jim looks after itself most of the time it got us through safely." He closed, as always, **"What a great boat, thanks Matt."**[31]

The St Albans Head passage stayed the reference point Alastair reached for whenever a later, unrelated question touched on rough water. Answering a newcomer's general rough-weather questions three years on, he cited it again as the worst water "little Jim" had ever shipped: green water breaking over the cabin with the hatch and tiller both shut, an audible rush through the vent trunk as it happened, and no water reaching the cabin regardless.[238] The same passage's sheer line — a strip of PVC tape along the join — washed off entirely and ended up wrapped round the mast and cabin, yet not a drop of water got inside; asked whether that was down to the design or his own workmanship, he credited the design, singling out the cabin hatch seal.[239][240] Retelling the episode once more in 2011, he was franker about the state he had been in that day — "when I didn't have any idea what I was doing" — seasick throughout, but still not a drop came in; he now recommends a door-mat just inside the hatch to keep the little water that does track in off the sole.[241]

The write-up drew the list's longest single reply thread to date, on **window fogging** (an anti-fog optical cleaner used aboard "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)"; a chamois technique aboard "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)"), **stability confidence**, and **cabin cushions**.[32][33] On stability, Alastair reported that even close-hauled through the St Albans Head race at roughly 20 degrees of heel, with gusts pushing her briefly further, "it never felt twitchy… It all felt quite solid," and that even with green water washing over the boat, she "kept on powering through the waves" — the clearest single statement on record of confidence in "little Jim"'s seaworthiness under real offshore conditions, six weeks after her uncertain-stability launch (see [above](#launch-it-floats-its-watertight-it-keeps-bumping-into-things-19-april-2003)).[34]

On the **helming position** — whether sitting facing forward with a lean to port or starboard grows uncomfortable, and whether the helmsman braces against cockpit sides — Alastair answered that there is no cockpit on a Paradox: he usually sits in the top corner of the cabin, feet braced against the lockers opposite, with the boat's own ballast giving "scope for moving about without upsetting the boat."[35][36] After 15 hours, though, he was "beginning to think of wedge shaped seats and bean bags and small inner tubes," having even tried kneeling on a garden kneeling pad "to get the blood circulating again" — which raised the question of what **Matt Layden** himself uses on longer voyages.[36] The authoritative answer: always closed-cell foam pads/cushions, or alternatively a small vinyl-covered sleeping cushion — **"This is what Matt has aboard Paradox."**[37] Alastair, already using closed-cell cushions himself ("surprisingly comfortable for a few hours" but not for 15), planned to look into an inner-tube alternative, which could double as "a fender and a life ring."[38]

This full Portland write-up, and the reply thread it drew, gives the archive its most detailed single offshore passage account for any Paradox besides Matt Layden's own — covering a genuine tidal race transit, seasickness, window fogging, a chance shore contact, and Matt Layden's own cushion practice as a direct answer to a question about long-passage comfort.

## Cabin-cushion solutions: Glen Maxwell's foam-by-mail system (June 2003)

Following directly from the Portland write-up's cushion question, a practical foam solution emerged. **1 5/8 in closed-cell Ensolite** ($128 from Defender) proved too hard to sleep on.[39] **Lux Foam** camping-pad stock from `foambymail.com`, by contrast, worked well: two 1 in × 39 in × 75 in pieces at $14 each with free shipping, cut to custom patterns and made into **2 in cushions**, slept on for two nights at the **Cedar Key Messabout** without any hard spots — "the most comfortable thin mattress I have ever slept on."[39] Custom-cut cushions covering the entire cabin sole — one piece over the water tanks, two more over the aft storage compartments, liftable individually for access — allow a nap at will while sailing offshore.[40] Covers were made from pack cloth, following the sewing instructions on the [microcruising website](/references/microcruising-website.md).[39]

An alternative is **Therm-a-Rest** self-inflating pads — light, insulating, low-bulk when rolled, the Classic about 2.25 in thick, though pricier than foam-by-mail — though they run too narrow individually and too wide to lay two abreast to cover a whole sole.[41][40] An inner tube covered in Sunbrella, secured to a single centerline point on the helm-seat/sole so it can flip side to side by tack, was also floated as an option, since foam cushions otherwise tend to stick to bare skin on standing up.[41][40] This exchange gave the archive its fullest technical record on cabin bedding, and happened to include the `paradoxbuilders` list's 1,000th posted message.[42]

Alastair later added his own camping-derived alternative: a thin sleep mat with two shaped foam blocks at the shoulder and hip pressure points — see [crew size and cabin fit — sleeping two aboard a Paradox-family cabin](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#sleeping-two-aboard-a-paradox-family-cabin).

Over a year later, the same foam-by-mail system was revisited to replace Little Cruiser's own bottomed-out cushion foam.[43] The source and technique were confirmed: **Lux Foam** from `foambymail.com` (`BasicTwinFirm`), inexpensive with free shipping; when the original cushions were built, only 1 in camping-pad stock was available, so two layers were laminated to reach the preferred 2 in thickness, though the same foam was later found sold directly in 2 in stock, needing no lamination at all — and it proved durable, with no hard spots found over time.[44][45] Compared with the old soft open-cell cushion foam, Lux Foam is soft yet firm enough that 2 in sleeps comfortably without bottoming out overnight, though not firm enough to sit on directly (weight concentrates too much in one spot); Foambymail will typically send a sample piece on request, and its firmness falls squarely between Lux Foam and open-cell foam for comparison.[46][47][48] Lux Foam was judged a good replacement for their own old open-cell cushions, with David's offer of a sample kept in reserve should Foambymail's own never arrive.[349]

## Interior photos, a water-tank leak fix, and the transom port cleared (June 2003)

Alastair posted interior photographs of "little Jim" to his long-tracked website, which turned up a minor **water-tank leak** — the same problem seen on other builds (see [transom deck plate — a dowel-rod fix for tank-port leaks](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md)).[49][50] The fix: a handle about 8 in long, with short pieces of 1/2 in dowel rod glued in at the appropriate spacing, snugs the tank's deck plate down tight enough to stop the leak while still opening it easily — a "brute force approach" preferable to relying on Vaseline alone, which helps the seal but still lets the area get damp under the mat.[50][51]

The **transom port** itself is not a leak risk: it sits **outside the baffle** (see [transom deck plate and vent baffle](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md)), so any water getting in there simply drains overboard rather than reaching the cabin; the only real trouble on record is losing its cover if it isn't refitted before towing.[52][53]

On **inspection-plate leaks and build time**: better-quality covers (not tape) are the real fix, and build-hours experience puts an 18-month build at roughly a year for a repeat, or as little as a couple of months for an experienced, full-time professional builder.[54] See [transom deck plate — leaking inspection plates](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md) and [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md#time) for the fuller answers.

## A control problem: losing the rudder's effect mid-tack (16 June 2003)

Sailing close-hauled in a disturbed F3-4 sea with 2-3 kn of tide pushing with him, Alastair tried to tack away from some moored yachts. The boat came head to wind and stuck there — normal enough — but bearing off back onto the starboard tack, the rudder had **no effect at all**, "as if the blade was raised." Drifting down onto the moorings, he lowered the sail and sculled clear; once re-hoisted, everything was back to normal. He also noted two other established quirks: "little Jim" cannot be sailed at all with the rudder fully raised (she simply rounds up into the wind when the sheet is pulled in, fine again once the blade drops to horizontal), and tacking to starboard had always come more easily than tacking to port.[55] See [rudder and chine experiments](/components/rudder.md) for the detailed technique on tacking, sail cut, and sailing with the rudder up that this episode drew out.

## Sailing two-up, and a new UK recruit (14 June 2003)

The same weekend, Alastair took "little Jim" out on Poole Harbour with his son for a father-son day sail, sailing **two-up** and reporting she "handled fine," though the single seat is not comfortable for two sitting side by side — one has to sit inside the cabin instead.[56] See [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md) for this data point alongside the other two-up findings.

While out, they met a new UK-based recruit to the list, who liked what he saw of "little Jim" enough to state an intention to build a Paradox of his own "(or even 2!)" — a plan that within days turned into an actual post asking to buy a second, ready-built Paradox (see [secondhand Paradox market — "Paradox for sale??"](/operations/secondhand-market.md)). Watching from the water, the recruit suggested that a sailor cruising two-up regularly could gain a second usable outside seat by extending the opening hatch forward, concertina-style. Alastair still did not think two could sleep comfortably aboard "unless they were very friendly," though he allowed a seriously cruising couple could work watches, with only one needing to lie down at a time.[56]

This sail also drew the first substantive design-verdict question on record from a newcomer, asking whether Alastair was satisfied with the design, whether the rig worked well, whether the lack of a keel hurt performance, and how the boat would handle heavy winds.[57] Alastair, answering as a self-described **non-sailor** who had originally planned to spend all of 2003 just learning to sail on the sheltered waters of Poole Harbour before finding himself confident enough for the 27-mile Portland cruise (above), gave his verdict in one line: **"In general the answer to all the questions is 'Yes, it works.'"**[58]

## A lake outing, and a Minisail speed comparison (September 2003)

Alastair took "little Jim" to a lake to show her off to his brother, posting photographs illustrating the boat's sensitivity (or lack of it) to weight distribution at [www.asjg.clara.co.uk/little_jim/little_jim.htm](http://www.asjg.clara.co.uk/little_jim/little_jim.htm), under a "stability & balance" section. In light winds (under Force 2) he was surprised to find he could keep pace with a two-up Minisail dinghy. For the fuller answer he gave on how "little Jim" behaves loaded versus light, and his verdict that a Paradox at design weight has to be sailed "as a yacht, not a dinghy," see [weight, ballast, and displacement — how a Paradox feels underloaded](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#how-a-paradox-feels-underloaded). The same message set out his next plan: launching at Poole and sailing to the far end of the Solent and back, roughly an 80-mile round trip, weather permitting.[59]

## A first crossing of the Solent: Poole to Cowes (September 2003)

Alastair's Solent plan became a genuine passage a week later: **"the fastest ever crossing from Poole to Cowes in a Paradox"** — and, since no Paradox had made the crossing before, also the only one, completed over 22–26 September 2003. The goal was to sail the roughly 15 nautical miles of open water from Poole Harbour to the Solent, visit harbours and anchorages on the Isle of Wight side, and make a leisurely return — including a stop at Ryde to let [Jeff](/people/jeff-doryman.md) see "little Jim" in person before starting his own stretched build.[60]

- **Launch and an early lesson in condensation.** Alastair launched into Poole Harbour on Sunday and anchored overnight near the entrance, shut up tight against the onset of autumn weather — and discovered, dripping on his head as he fell asleep, that a hatch window invites condensation in cold weather. The fix was to leave the hatch cracked and wear a wool hat; no rain fell during the whole trip regardless.[60]
- **The crossing, and a knockdown from a rogue wave.** The morning forecast called for SW F4–5 locally F6, veering NW–N F6 locally F7 — more wind than Alastair would have chosen, but with any northerly component meaning the open-water leg would be in the lee of the land, he went for it, reefed from the start. Once clear of the harbour the seas built to a genuine 5–6 ft, ridden easily at first, though Alastair himself was seasick by 09:10 — "the price that Neptune extracts from me for passage over his domain." Heel under the building wind ran to a normal 20 degrees with gusts to 40, and the rail went under on at least two occasions (an estimated 50 degrees). Approaching Hurst Narrows, one wave grew into **a vertical wall of water about 6 ft high**, picked the boat up, and knocked her down until water rose past the hatch window — the harness-strap gap that keeps the hatch from closing fully let a surprising amount of water below — before "little Jim" came up **from an estimated 80–90 degrees of knockdown** and sailed on as if nothing had happened.[60] Recalling the moment years later in an unrelated hatch-design discussion, Alastair added that the water coming through the harness-strap gap was enough to soak his sleeve as he braced a hand under the flow, but not enough reached the bilge to be worth pumping — a sponge sufficed — and the whole ingress lasted only the few seconds of the knockdown itself, leaving him satisfied that even a fully open hatch would have shipped only a "nuisance," not a "danger," amount of water in a knockdown that brief (see [Enuf — hatch mod discussion](/boats/enuf.md#a-fabric-roll-top-and-the-wider-hard-top-vs-soft-top-question) for the fuller exchange this recollection came from).[61] Alastair judged in hindsight that he had erred in setting out at all that morning, and that no conventional boat of similar size would have completed the crossing — but that having done it, and provided he was not so foolish again, "LJ will take me wherever I want to go." The only casualties below were a further-dented kettle and the contents of an unsecured shelf, which emptied through the side-bin access holes into the bilge along with 2–3 litres of water through the hatch — a practical lesson to stow loose gear in waterproof bags. A third recollection, given in 2008 while answering a general question about how Paradox fares knocked down or inverted, adds that as much water came in under the hatch's front edge as through the rear harness-strap gap — the plans' own external front seal not yet being fitted at the time, a lapse resolved afterward — and credits the knockdown directly with prompting a harness hard point fitted belowdecks, on top of the general re-stowage described below.[62] **Total run, Poole anchorage to Cowes pontoon: 28.4 NM in 7:20, averaging 3.9 kn** — arriving just ahead of a squall that swung the wind 90 degrees and had larger yachts on the outside berths rearing at their lines.[60]
- **Chafe found at anchor.** A lay day in Cowes turned up chafe on both the tack tie-down and, more worryingly, the furling line where it passed through the deck — the first time Alastair had needed to reef from genuine necessity rather than as a precaution, confirming the line sees much higher load reefed than not, and that a better fairlead is needed there. Jeff came over from Ryde to look the boat over and take photographs while Alastair replaced the worn line.[60]
- **A gentle return via Newtown Creek and Keyhaven.** Light easterly wind and a tide the wrong way for Ryde sent Alastair west instead, sculling clear of Cowes harbour before an easy sail with wind and tide together along the island shore to Newtown Creek — drawing curiosity from two passing dinghies and a note of praise left by a fellow Dinghy Cruising Association member who'd missed him ashore. From Newtown he yulohed out against a headwind to Keyhaven at the Solent's western end, riding the last of the tide past the paddle steamer Waverley under sail before the wind died and he finished by motor-sculling; low water at the Keyhaven approach (Hawkers Lake) left every moored yacht aground but just enough depth to scull "little Jim" in.[60]
- **The return crossing, and a seismic survey vessel.** Rather than wait for an evening tide change at the pub, Alastair sculled down-channel in fading light to a mooring near Hurst Castle, ready for the morning ebb. The tide stalled him dead level with the castle's round tower for the better part of an hour before releasing him to a fast, following passage back across in flat-calm conditions and full sun. Nearing Poole, an unfamiliar slapping under the hull turned out to be the flat bottom's first real encounter with confused seas; mid-slap, a survey vessel towing two 1.5-mile cables hailed him to alter course around them — as the ship passed the slapping briefly became a hammering "as if someone were hitting us with rubber sledge hammers," fading once two large torpedo-shaped tow-fish had gone by.[60]
- **Verdict.** After a calm final day pottering in the harbour (including disturbing a herd of deer ashore at Gold Point) and a rising-tide push up the river Frome to the boatyard, Alastair closed the report **"Hoping for calm nights"** — the trip having been his first without a tight schedule, more relaxed than his prior passages but no more careful in its weather judgment, and a further, concrete demonstration that "little Jim" could be trusted to bring him home even from a genuine knockdown.[60] Just how stormy that crossing had been was independently confirmed years later, recalled from a photograph of "little Jim" moored at Cowes: three 40 ft yachts had set out the same day from Lymington, one turning back in the conditions, and the two that made it in tied up on the pontoon's far side about a quarter of an hour ahead of her — their crews taken aback to learn "little Jim" had beaten them in from Poole. Alastair judged it one case where he would not have sailed at all had he had a second person aboard.[346]

The account raised a further question about the [Paradox righting-moment graph](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) — how much a heavier sailor's own weight, rolling around the interior during a knockdown, would add to or subtract from the boat's tested numbers, with the expectation of ending up "sprawled on the leeward bins" and the reasoning that this is likely just when water ballast earns its keep.[63]

## An autumn refit: sand and damp stowage, and chafe fixes

Once the Solent crossing was behind him, Alastair spent a couple of weeks on housekeeping fixes drawn directly from that trip's lessons. Frequent beach landings tracked sand and water aboard through his feet on reboarding, so he stripped the interior, vacuumed out the accumulated sand, and refitted everything with shock cord or velcro assigned to a fixed "home" for each item. The aft side storage bins, open at the bottom, had been letting anything cardboard or cloth sit damp; he added a shelf a couple of inches up from each bin's bottom — nearly identical to the shelf already at the top of the bin on the plans — keeping stowed items dry short of the worst flooding, while the space below the shelf stayed reachable through the floor hatches. Inspecting the rig after the first extended sail with the sail reefed down halfway, he found severe chafe where the furling line passed through the deck, traced to insufficient radius on the end of the copper pipe fairlead there; he fitted a small brass insert to fix it, noting it would have been easier to get the radius right the first time. A smaller chafe point on the tack line, where it also passes through the deck, he fixed by sleeving the line in plastic tubing.[64]

This experience bears on a further rig question: whether, sailed reefed for real, a **parrel** is needed to contain the yard once the sail is reefed down, since photographs of a reefed Paradox show the yard standing well out from the mast — a parrel would come at the cost of being able to stow the furled sail directly on the side deck, the same tradeoff a topping lift already involves.[65] On Alastair's own extended reefed sail, which had been on a single, starboard tack throughout, the yard riding well clear of the mast on that tack seemed, if anything, desirable, since it de-powers the sail more quickly in gusts, and any felt shortage of power is easily cured by hauling up more sail — the reefing system's infinite variation, as against fixed reef points, being a real advantage here. A topping lift adds only about 5 seconds to furling time.[66]

On the **endless halyard/reefing-line arrangement**: it works well and stayed free of serious line-twisting problems. When the chafe described above turned up, the furling end was disconnected and a new furling line fitted while keeping the rest as the halyard — meaning loose rope underfoot on the cabin floor for a week until the new brass fairlead allowed the full endless line to be reinstated. The line does show some tendency to kink, cleared out of the run whenever the rig comes down, but the kinking itself has never caused a problem. On balance there are two real risks worth flagging with this system: the sail cannot be dropped instantly by releasing the halyard, since only about six feet of slack is available, and any chafe damage to the line means replacing the whole endless run rather than a single piece — but the advantages outweigh both drawbacks.[66]

## Beale Park Thames Boat Show, and a righting-moment measurement (June 2009)

"Little Jim" was part of the Dinghy Cruising Association's display at the **Beale Park Thames Boat Show** the previous weekend. With plenty of spare hands on hand, Alastair took the opportunity to pull the boat over and measure her righting moment directly: set up in cruising trim with the sail hoisted and a body at the helm position, the mast was hauled down until the water stood a couple of inches below the hatch, with a 25 lb spring balance rigged at the masthead to read the load. The balance ran straight off its own scale — "well over 25 pounds" — so no exact figure was obtained, but the exercise left no doubt that **a correctly ballasted Paradox definitely self-rights from 90 degrees**, with a good deal of rolling before she finally settled upright. A video of the test was posted to YouTube.[86] See [self-righting and capsize recovery — firsthand knockdown accounts](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#firsthand-knockdown-accounts) for how this measurement fits the wider stability record, and [a 2020 retrospective: the AYRS talk](#a-2020-retrospective-the-ayrs-talk) below for how Alastair recalled the same test eleven years later.

The report drew an immediate personal reaction: having been shown round "Little Jim" in person only days before, one fellow builder called it "an interesting experiment" and credited the visit with giving him fresh incentive to get on with his own build — see [Pete Martin: Building JoHanna](/people/pete-martin.md#building-johanna) for the fuller account.[87] Two weeks later a further owner, watching the video, singled out how quickly the rolling settled down once the boat was released as the most striking part of the demonstration.[88]

Retelling the test again a year later, for a newcomer's benefit in an unrelated 2010 thread, drew out detail not captured at the time: the rudder had been left up by oversight, reasoned to be no real gap since genuine knockdown-risk weather would already have him retreated below with the hatch shut; rather than sitting on the bottom, he was sprawled against the side bins and deliberately shifted position to test the effect, which his helpers found made no noticeable difference to the load on the spring balance.[196][197] Separately, nothing aboard had shifted from its stowed position by the test's end — the as-designed bins and their shock-cord retainers held everything in place, down to an unlit kettle still standing on the stove.[197]

## Photographed and filmed at Beale Park, from outside the community (2008-2012)

"Little Jim" was a fixture of the **Dinghy Cruising Association's** presence at the Beale Park Thames Boat Show across at least four years, and the UK weblog [intheboatshed.net](/references/intheboatshed.md) — written by boat-design writer Gavin Atkin, who had no connection to the design or its builders — documented her there in a way the community's own record does not.

Atkin met **Alastair** and saw the boat close up at the **2008** show, calling it a highlight of his year, and published **five photographs** the following May.[228] They are the archive's best close-up documentation of a UK-built Paradox in cruising trim: the DCA fleet moored at Beale with Little Jim in the foreground; her cabin looking forward, showing the hinged table to port, the GPS and compass mounted on the forward bulkhead, the sole boards and the side bins in use; her sliding hatch and deck detail from aft, with the spars stowed on deck and the wire mainsheet bridle rigged; her foredeck and cabin from forward, the sail furled on the boom under its cover; and Alastair himself standing in her hatch in a DCA sweatshirt. The paint is visibly worn and chipped at the coamings and sole — a working, cruised boat rather than a show finish. A sailing video Atkin shot at the same show remains online.[228]

Atkin's framing of the boat is worth recording as an outside reading of the design. He set out the sharpie argument without settling it — some hold that the heavy-displacement Paradox is a small sharpie, others that it cannot be one because it is under 19 ft, both correct "in the mad logic of boat nomenclature" — and offered his own image instead, that in some ways she is **more like a model of a small modern ship** than like a dinghy. He judged the type of particular interest to a sailor who has come to accept that their family is unlikely to sail with them, and who finds a small, easily managed boat sailed alone an agreeable alternative.[228] See also the published DCA appraisals at [references/dca-sailing-the-paradox.md](/references/dca-sailing-the-paradox.md) and [references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md), the latter's author having first met the boat on the DCA pontoon at the **2006** show.

Previewing the **2010** show, Atkin reported that a key aim of the DCA's attendance was to demonstrate that a wide range of small craft — "(including, hopefully, Alistair Law's *Paradox*)" — can be used for cruising in coastal waters, the association putting the design forward as an exemplar coastal cruiser; the wording makes it an expectation rather than a confirmed attendance.[229] By **June 2012** there were **two** Paradoxes on the DCA jetty: Atkin's gallery from that year's rain-shortened show photographs a green hull matching Little Jim's colouring alongside a blue-hulled boat carrying the name "[Johanna](/boats/johanna.md)," neither named in his text.[230] His parenthetical remark that he knew "of a couple in the UK" is also an outside estimate of the UK fleet's size at that date.[230] By January 2020 the show run had lapsed entirely: no boat shows were planned for "Little Jim" that year at all, Beale Park having been the only one a Paradox ever fit into "seamlessly" — sailing that summer was planned instead for the south coast, including the Dinghy Cruising Association's Cobnor fortnight on Chichester Harbour toward the end of August.[237]

## Attested as the first Paradox built in the UK (September 2008)

The claim that "little Jim" was the **first Paradox built in the United Kingdom** is made outright, and by someone in a position to know, in a comment [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md) left on the American small-craft weblog [70.8%](/references/70point8-blog.md): "**Al Law was the first in the UK to build a Paradox sailboat. Derek Clark built the next which is now in Norway and others are being built here.**"[231] It is a plain statement of what the rest of the archive establishes only by assembling launch dates — this boat at Poole over Easter 2003, [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" in August 2006 — and it comes from the man who ran the UK Paradox website and built the third boat himself. The post the comment sits under names the boat and its builder in the same breath — "Alastair Law, also in the UK, built and sails 'Little Jim', the interior of which we see at the top" — and heads itself with a photograph of her cabin, shot from aft through the open hatch: the forward bulkhead with its round access port and oval opening, instruments on the bulkhead face, the side bins, tiller and mainsheet across the frame, and the dark green painted sole.[232] A 2015 mailing-list recollection extended the same claim to Europe as a whole — "the very first one anywhere in Europe as far as" its own writer knew — a hedged, secondhand impression rather than independent verification beyond the UK-specific claim above.[233]

## Seafair Haven, Milford Haven: filmed under way (2012)

"Little Jim" appeared at **Seafair Haven** — the biennial maritime festival on the Milford Haven Waterway in Pembrokeshire, Wales — in the summer of 2012. Fellow Paradox owner [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md) ("Paradox Pete") was there with his own boat and filmed "little Jim" tacking down the upper estuary against a **force 5**, posting the clip to YouTube.[89] Watching it back, Alastair was surprised how much the boat appeared to pitch as she punched through the waves, when from aboard the motion had felt nothing like that lively — "a feature of sitting near the centre of rotation," the same effect he would set out at length years later at the [2020 AYRS talk](#a-2020-retrospective-the-ayrs-talk) (see also [sailing characteristics — why the seat's position damps the felt motion](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#why-the-seats-position-damps-the-felt-motion)).[89] Pete Martin's own contemporaneous account, posted to the mailing list on returning from that week, corroborates the trip firsthand — three unedited clips, with more promised once he found time to "edit out the rubbish" — and drew a viewer's compliment that the boat "sails real well."[205][206]

The clip itself drew a contemporaneous reply judging it showed the boat making a good turn of speed — some **6 knots** — for a displacement hull under 14 ft LOA, and asking why the yuloh sat mounted so far aft in the shot.[207] Alastair put the yuloh's position down to plain laziness: having used it a few minutes earlier and expecting to need it again coming ashore in the sheltered water, he had simply left it on its pivot rather than stowing it properly; replying the same day, he also confirmed, in his own contemporaneous words, that the pitching had genuinely surprised him on watching the footage back, since it hadn't felt anything like that lively from aboard.[208] Pete Martin, replying in turn, put the pitching down to wind-over-tide chop — milder, he judged, than what the other boats motoring through the same water (uncaptured, camera left off) had shown — and was struck by how much more benign the water had looked from the shore than it had felt from out on it.[209] Alastair agreed, guessing the discrepancy owed something to sitting near the centre of rotation and to being relaxed and comfortable at the helm; on the same theme, though the boat sometimes heels quite a lot, knowing she won't go over lets him simply ignore it.[210]

## A sailmaker that skipped the yard-flex question, and a light-wind comparison against a Mk2 sail

Little Jim's own sail carries a lasting, self-diagnosed flaw: the loft that made it, Quay Sails of Poole, never asked how much the yard flexes under load, leaving a nearly vertical crease in the upper part of the sail — visible in Alastair's own early photographs — that had to be worked around afterward rather than avoided at the outset. Asking about yard deflection up front is the standing lesson for any future sailmaker briefing (see [rig and sail — a named UK loft, real deflection figures, and a sailmaker to avoid](/components/rig/sail.md#a-2012-recurrence-a-named-uk-loft-real-deflection-figures-and-a-sailmaker-to-avoid)).[211] Tacking in light winds during the same Seafair Haven week (above), [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md)'s taller [Mk2 sail](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md) seemed to be gaining on Little Jim's own original [Mk1](/components/rig/sail.md) — inconclusive on its own, but enough to make the newer sail worth considering.[211]

## A 2020 retrospective: the AYRS talk

Some seventeen years after her launch, Alastair presented "little Jim" to the **Amateur Yacht Research Society** at an online meeting in May 2020 (see [AYRS meeting talk](/references/ayrs-meeting-video.md)), giving a mature firsthand summary of the design and his long experience of sailing it. He called it **"an old man's boat"**: the sailing is so relaxed that the most strenuous act of a whole cruise is winding the trailer winch to haul out — he expected to fit an electric motor to the winch once even that became too much. The everyday sailing needs so little attention that he "get[s] through a lot of paperbacks," tweaking the course only every few minutes, the boat otherwise running "like it's on rails." He reaffirmed the settled picture from his cruising years: she sails best heeled about **20 degrees** (the weather chine just clearing the water); her flat 18 mm bottom sheathed in epoxy glass means "I don't bother to clear the stones off the beach before I land"; and she asks to be sailed like a small yacht rather than a dinghy. He also reported the boat's **ballast had evolved**: launched with roughly **70 kg of water plus 180 kg of lead**, by 2020 the lead was down to about **50–70 kg**, progressively swapped for weight that earns its keep — batteries, anchors, chain, tinned food, even a bicycle — all doubling as ballast (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — the designer's layered ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#the-designers-layered-ballast-water-tanks-stores-and-lead-together)).[90]

**A masthead righting-moment test, recalled.** Retelling the [June 2009 Beale Park test](#beale-park-thames-boat-show-and-a-righting-moment-measurement-june-2009) above, put a number on the pegged-out spring balance reading: "well over 25 pounds" implies a righting moment of **well over 350 ft-lb**, "more than required to undo the wheel nuts on my car." It remains the archive's only quantified field measurement of a Paradox's righting moment (see [self-righting and capsize recovery — firsthand knockdown accounts](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#firsthand-knockdown-accounts)).[91]

**Two self-recovered knockdowns.** Alastair recounted having twice been laid flat with the sail in the water, both times recovering unaided. The first was on only his second trip outside a sheltered harbour, in a force 5: the topping lift had fouled the sail so he could not spill it, a wave pushed her over, and she had righted and was sailing again before he had registered going down. The second time he had let himself get **broadside onto broken surf** and was knocked to **90 degrees**, coming upright the moment the surf had passed. He had also watched a big man deliberately pull a Paradox down by the masthead and fail to capsize her — "very, very stable."[91]

**Why the pitching goes unfelt.** A film taken of "little Jim" beating to windward in a force 5 — the [2012 Seafair Haven video](#seafair-haven-milford-haven-filmed-under-way-2012) above — astonished Alastair when he saw it: the boat was pitching quite violently, yet he had felt none of it aboard, because the helm seat sits close to the boat's **centre of pitch rotation**. The same effect explained a two-up passage across the Helford to Falmouth, where whoever sat further forward in the cabin kept getting seasick, so he and his companion repeatedly swapped places (see [sailing characteristics — why the seat's position damps the felt motion](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#why-the-seats-position-damps-the-felt-motion)).[92]

**Yuloh cruising, and the Thames.** He will push "little Jim" at a comfortable **2 knots all day** under the yuloh, and on the Dinghy Cruising Association's annual Thames raid he sculled roughly **50 of the ~55 miles** — arriving without the blisters that afflicted the crews of the conventional rowing boats, because sculling lets the body keep shifting position rather than repeating one fixed stroke (see [sculling and yuloh technique — performance](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#performance)).[92]

## An outside eyewitness account: crossing Bideford Bar in a gale (Roger Barnes)

A rare **third-party account of "Little Jim" in extreme conditions** comes from **Roger Barnes**, president of the Dinghy Cruising Association, who in an undated "Fireside Yarns" video recounted a late-September DCA passage in **Bideford (Barnstaple) Bay**, north Devon, sailed in his own Ilur in company with **Alastair's "little green Paradox"** (see [Fireside Yarns "MAYDAY" video](/references/roger-barnes-mayday-video.md) for the source, the boat's identification, and the machine-transcript caveats). The two boats set out from **Appledore**, out over **Bideford Bar** in a strong Force 5–6 southerly, and beat a long way to windward to the drying harbour at **Clovelly** — the same ground Alastair had cruised her over years earlier (see [Appledore–Clovelly passage](#a-load-tested-anchor-rode-measurement-and-a-passage-to-clovelly-july-2008)). A planned crossing to **Lundy** the next day was abandoned for worsening weather, and both boats instead ran back in over the bar, by then standing up into breakers on the sandbank one side and the beach the other.

Barnes's eyewitness detail is of the bar crossing itself: "Little Jim," a little ahead of him, **"vanished completely between the waves"** — seas he put at "at least 6 m … maybe 8 m" against the boat's roughly 5 m mast — and got through. Barnes's own Ilur, following, was rolled over by a breaking crest, flooded, bailed, and flooded again; fearing being driven onto the beach, he **put out a Mayday call**, and both Appledore lifeboats launched (the inshore Atlantic-class boat reaching him within ten minutes), though by then he was through the worst and needed no help.

Crucially, the capsize and the Mayday were **Barnes's own boat, not the Paradox** — "Little Jim" crossed the bar ahead of him and unaided. The passage therefore stands as an outside observer's testimony to how the design carries through a genuinely dangerous breaking sea, consistent with Alastair's own heavy-weather and knockdown reports (see [the Solent crossing and knockdown](#a-first-crossing-of-the-solent-poole-to-cowes-september-2003)).

## No rubbing strake, and how fenders are secured instead

The stock plans call for **no rubbing strake**; asked in jest whether "Little Jim" carried one, Alastair answered "a green rubbing strake about 0.5 m deep down each side" — a dry aside at the chine runner rather than an actual added fitting. For fenders, he settled on a pair of eye bolts through the hatch rail as tie-off points, having first tried a less satisfactory string of four fenders tied bow to stern. Coming alongside another Paradox in particular calls for larger fenders than usual, since the two boats' chine runners can otherwise strike each other before the fenders themselves take up the gap — a problem Alastair rarely meets in practice, since he mostly avoids coming alongside anything at all.[93][94]

## Twenty years on: trip reports, why they've stopped, and plywood costs for a UK build

Reread more than four years on, Alastair's own earlier trip write-ups drew a request for any recent updates, since he remained the only UK owner with a completely finished boat.[100] His answer: he only ever intended to write up genuinely significant outings, and family commitments since the Portland/Cowes years had kept his sailing to short local trips around the Solent and nearby estuaries rather than anything warranting a full report; a crossing to the Isles of Scilly, were it to happen the following year, would be the next trip worth writing up. Dictating a report came easily enough, but typing one up did not — "what I really need is a secretary" — and the real fix for the shortage of adventurous UK Paradox reports, in his view, was for other UK builders to finish their own boats.[101]

That reply reached a UK builder working through the archive who was, at the same time, pricing UK plywood suppliers for his own build: **Robins of Bristol's "Elite" marine ply** would cost about **£548** for the basic sheets, against **£252** for **"Far Eastern Marine Ply"** from **Clarkswood** (`www.clarkswood.com`) — a **£300** saving, judged worth taking given most of the hull would be layered in glass and epoxy regardless. **Robbins** remained the timber source. Having priced the ply, he sent off for the plans the same day.[102] A further supplier recommendation followed directly: for a UK build, save further by using **3/8 in "Far Eastern Marine Ply" for the sides and 3/4 in for the bottom**, with **1/4 in for the deck** — lighter than the stock 1/2 in sides Matt Layden's plans call for, which specify that thickness because they assume **exterior-grade plywood** rather than true marine ply.[103]

## Reworking the cabin for headroom: a lowered sole and an outside-ballast idea, discussed and dropped

A visit aboard "little Jim" left a prospective UK builder finding the cabin headroom uncomfortable, and thinking about how to build a variant with more of it before starting his own boat the following year. His idea: lower the cabin sole, using thin camping-bedding foam mats in place of the specified insulation to claw back some of the lost space, and compensate for the resulting loss of ballast volume by screwing **sheet steel to the outside of the plywood bottom** — adding damage resistance and putting ballast weight low and outside rather than inside.[104]

The sole-lowering half of the idea got a detailed, supportive answer: dropping the sole by **25 mm** and switching to thin camping-pad foam in place of the drawn insulation would free a useful few inches, and trimming the sole locally to leave just over **100 mm** of clearance under it at bulkhead 3 would still leave room to store 2-litre water bottles there — enough for a week or two. The lower side bins could also be omitted and bulkhead 3 cut back locally to **85 mm**, taking the sole right out to the hull sides for extra sprawling room. The outside-steel half was rejected as too much hassle, with a plain restatement that a stock Paradox already has more than enough ballast room without it; the broader design point offered alongside was that Paradox as drawn is built for **long-distance cruising, away from support for a month or more**, whereas UK "dinghy" cruising of the kind practiced on the list stays close to civilization — "and a Tesco" — and so does not need anywhere near that much stores capacity.[105] Separately, the boat's owner agreed there was no shortage of storage space even with a lowered sole, declined the steel-bottom idea as unnecessary hassle, but floated **copper sheathing** as a different proposition from plain steel.[106]

The idea's originator, still put off by the headroom question, restated his reasoning: even a modest increase in seated headroom would be welcome, since sitting on the bare sole for any length of time is uncomfortable for him; the outside-steel proposal had been meant to preserve the design's ballast ratio while moving weight to the most effective place, galvanised for corrosion resistance; and copper sheathing's antifouling benefit seemed unnecessary given a flat-bottomed boat can simply be beached to rub off any growth. He also asked directly whether "little Jim" takes on much bilge water.[107] A follow-up asked how the boat's bottom had held up after three to four seasons of sailing, and whether any repairs had been needed, noting that copper sheet of a useful thickness would be expensive.[108]

Both questions were answered from experience. On bilge water: none at all beyond what comes aboard on the owner's own shoes when launching off a beach, or occasionally through a leaking ballast-tank inspection hatch; a sponge kept in the locker behind bulkhead 3 catches it, wrung out every two or three days as needed.[109] On bottom wear: after a beach launch into Poole Harbour where the trailer could not be fully submerged, the boat had to be heaved some distance over a sharp, flinty bottom, expected to need winter repair — but on inspection no damage worth repairing was found, not even enough to justify touching up the paint, credited mainly to the hull being too heavy to drag or scrape along the bottom in normal use. The only planned winter maintenance was stripping and vacuuming the interior and a fresh coat of paint on deck and sides, plus renewing the non-skid strips (a 5 m roll of 25 mm non-skid tape from Ikea, at £2.50).[110]

A brief exchange closed out the bilge-water thread the same week: thanking Alastair for the answer, one reply called it "encouraging."[111]

## A published appraisal in the DCA Bulletin

The same Dinghy Cruising Association connection produced the boat's one **published owner's appraisal**: a signed two-page article, "Sailing The Paradox," subtitled "The owner's experiences of sailing Little Jim," in *DCA Bulletin* 193.[123] Written after **three seasons** — so most likely 2005 or 2006, the issue itself being undated on the captured pages — it sets the design out as **fifteen advantages against eleven disadvantages**, offered "in no particular order" and with a stated aim of being "completely impartial" for the benefit of "anyone considering building one of these remarkable boats."[123]

Its framing is a motor-boater's: the search was for "a sailing boat with the comfort of my motor boat," a standard the boat is judged to come "pretty close" to.[123] Because the piece is an edited summary rather than correspondence, it is the one place the design's drawbacks appear as a deliberate itemized list from someone who had built and cruised one; the individual findings are folded into the topical pages, and the source itself is described at ["Sailing The Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-sailing-the-paradox.md).

## An independent sail trial at Chew Valley Lake (October 2006)

The same issue of the *Bulletin* carries a second, separate article about the boat — an **evaluation by a non-owner**, running two pages ahead of the owner's own appraisal, and written from the opposite standpoint.[124] Its author approached the design from **declared scepticism** that a hull with no keel, centreboard or leeboard could work to windward on a bare flat bottom, and arranged a day and a half aboard "Little Jim" specifically to settle the question, having first met the boat on the DCA pontoon at the **Beale Park Show 2006**.[124]

That makes this the archive's **only published independent sail trial** of a Paradox: not owner testimony, not a builder's log, but an outside assessment by someone who set out to disprove the claim. The scepticism was not his alone — the sailing club's president had a **rescue boat stand by** for the launch.[124]

The trial found in the design's favour on the central question, and against it on a secondary one. She made good about **45° to the wind** at close to her **4.5-knot** hull speed, tacked through about **90°**, and never failed to complete a tack that day.[124] But she goes into a tack quickly and then **drops level onto her flat bottom** as the pressure comes off the sail, her weight only just carrying her round — leaving the boat, in the author's judgement, unpromising for beating up narrow rivers.[124] That is the same limitation the owner had listed as a disadvantage in the companion article, now independently corroborated by a different observer on a different day.

The verdict carries one stated caveat: the wind was **0–4 mph for most of the day**, with a single useful spell of about **8 mph** after lunch, so the windward figures rest on a short period of usable breeze.[124]

Two photographs of "Little Jim" accompany the piece — afloat at the water's edge and bow-on on her road trailer — credited by an editorial note to the author and dated to Chew Valley Lake, October 2006.[125] The source is described in full at ["Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md).

## Abandoning water ballast for lead, after a leaking tank inspection cover (May 2007)

A rough weekend out in Force 5-6 (mid-May 2007) turned up a bit of damp in the bottom of "Little Jim"'s main locker, traced to a failing screw-in inspection cover on one of the water-ballast tanks. The leak was not a new problem — the covers had given trouble before, and replacing one only a month earlier had brought only limited success — and it prompted a change of ballast strategy rather than another repair: abandoning water ballast in favor of **permanent lead**, keeping only **10 kg** as water ballast, carried in 2-litre bottles for fine-tuning trim rather than relying on the built-in tanks.[126]

The fix offered for the underlying leak was the same **dowel-rod tool** already on record for water-tank deck plates generally (see [transom deck plate — water-tank deck plates: a more serious leak, and Glen Maxwell's dowel-rod fix](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#water-tank-deck-plates-a-more-serious-leak-and-glen-maxwells-dowel-rod-fix)), refined into a purpose-built handle: a **10 in length of 1x2**, with **5/8 in dowel rods about 2 in long** set into it at the correct spacing to fit the finger holes in the tank's inspection plate, giving enough leverage to tighten or loosen the plate without the earlier trouble getting it tight enough to seal or, once tight, getting it off again.[127]

Even that dowel-rod tool did not fully solve the underlying leak: the cylindrical threaded lid fitting had twice parted from its own disc, forcing the same repair again, and the seal held reliably only with a liberal coating of Vaseline. A kayak-building technique was offered as an alternative to the screw-in inspection covers altogether: **press-in hatches**, found by kayak builders to seal and open more easily than screw-ins, and to stay leak-free even after considerable rolling — a much harder test of a hatch seal than a Paradox ever sees. The screw-in covers' underlying design mismatch was diagnosed in reply: they are built to keep water *out* of a compartment, the opposite of the job asked of them when used to keep ballast water *in* — a distinction that likely explains their persistent leaking on "Little Jim" better than any workmanship problem. Having dithered over the water-to-lead ballast switch since the boat was first built, the change was reported as an unambiguous improvement once made.[128][129][130]

Replying to a South African builder's own search for a lead alternative in September 2019, Alastair traced his own ballast lead back to scrap wheel-balancing weights, melted down after a local tyre shop gave them to him for free — the same low-cost route recorded generally at [weight and ballast — sourcing lead as wheel-balancing weights](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives) — and noted such shops otherwise have to pay to have the scrap taken away.[236]

## Chichester Harbour, a shallow-water lifeboat callout, and choosing a UK cruising ground

The same rough force 5-6 weekend that turned up the leaking inspection cover kept "Little Jim" inside Chichester Harbour rather than out in open water: with the wind on the nose for a passage toward Bembridge, discretion prevailed and the boat stayed in harbour. Chichester Harbour itself carries plenty of water in its channels at low tide despite extensive mud elsewhere — distinct from the more extensively drying Christchurch Harbour, which the account was initially taken for. The shallow draught proved its worth that weekend: the lifeboat was called out three times on the Saturday to boats aground *inside* the harbour, none of them "Little Jim."[131][132]

Weighing where to hold a first shakedown cruise, **Poole Harbour** was recommended over the alternatives on offer: despite plenty of quiet corners, it also has large enough open areas of water to try sailing on all points without being confined to narrow channels, and its tides are unusually accommodating for a first-timer feeling out a new boat.[132]

## A wire-nail tooling-pin technique for hull assembly

Little Jim's own hull-gluing method, recalled years after her 2003 launch in answer to a newcomer's question, turned on a technique discovered partway through the original trial assembly: the pilot holes drilled for the ring nails proved a nice push fit for ordinary wire nails, so once a bulkhead sat correctly, a couple of extra holes each side took a wire nail as a temporary locating pin. This let the hull's components be taken apart and put back together in exactly the same position, and let every other ring-nail hole be pre-drilled before any glue was mixed. At final assembly, ring nails were started into all the side-panel holes except the wire-nail-pin holes; bulkhead 3 was glued first and positioned using the pins, with ring nails driven home (finishing with the pin holes last), then the sequence worked outward to bulkhead 2 and on to the stem — not needing to wait for earlier joints to cure, since a nailed joint cannot move regardless. Fitting the stem, a line run from the centre of the transom across the centre of each bulkhead to the centre of the stem kept the hull from ending up banana-shaped. The same wire-nail tooling-pin trick was used at every later stage of Little Jim's construction, not just this one. Alongside the wire-nail pins, separate location blocks were glued on with a hot-melt gun at each trial-assembly joint and knocked off again once the ring nails had gone in. The bows proved the one genuinely difficult part of the whole trial assembly — "nothing to get hold of" to pull them together — a problem solved on later reflection by leaving the side panels long there and drawing them in with studding run through oversized holes in the overhang. The sheer strakes were fitted entirely single-handed, unlike the trial-fit-and-refit approach used on the other panels, terminating at the transom with a wedge that forms a socket for the strake's own end. Floor panels and diagonal bracing went in at this same trial-assembly stage, chosen deliberately because they make the whole hull easy to take apart and put back together again for the glue-up.[242][243][244][245] See [hull assembly sequence — gluing up the hull: one sitting or several](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#gluing-up-the-hull-one-sitting-or-several-and-locating-the-bulkheads-with-temporary-pins) for the full technique alongside a complementary one-session glue-up method, and a cautionary account of what goes wrong locating panels with screws instead of nails.[133]

## Confirming the bulkhead side curve's purpose, and the philosophy that mistakes end up epoxied stronger

Answering a fellow builder who had already glued a bulkhead panel without the plans' small stiffening curve along its edge, Alastair confirmed the curve's real job — stiffening the side panels against oil-canning — and gave the general fix: extend the bulkhead's own cleats out over the missing edge and fill the resulting gap with epoxy at assembly, since cured epoxy is stronger than the plywood it replaces. He credited that same philosophy for "Little Jim" herself, judging her, only half in jest, **stronger than the as-designed boat** thanks to the accumulated epoxy-backed fixes to her own early mistakes. Full exchange: [construction/panel-layout.md — bulkhead side curves](/construction/panel-layout.md#bulkhead-side-curves) and [Wizard Spawn — a missed bulkhead curve, and a cleat-extension fix](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#a-missed-bulkhead-curve-and-a-cleat-extension-fix).[134]

## A boom-trim question, a proposed Poole meet, and 2008 cruise plans with Bill Serjeant

Sailing through a UK winter comes down mostly to wind rather than cold: based two hours from the coast, going out at all needs a firm forecast first, a caution learned after pushing the season further than was comfortable a couple of winters earlier and ending up, more than once, somewhere not at all relaxing. The settled rhythm since has been to spend the winter working and save the whole summer, starting in April, for sailing.[138]

By January 2008, a fellow UK builder then cutting plywood for his own car-topping, Enigma-style Paradox proposed a group meet at Poole Harbour and asked whether a shorter boom, or a light spinnaker for calm days, would suit a Paradox-family boat better against increasingly strong summer winds.[139] The reply held Paradox's own sail at a confirmed 100 sq ft, against a much smaller sail loosely associated with Enigma (a rough, unconfirmed 65 sq ft); explained that shortening the boom by a foot would need the mast stepped roughly 6 in further aft to keep the sail's centre of effort in the same place; and met the spinnaker idea with caution rather than encouragement, unsure how a Paradox-style hull would cope if it went wrong. On the meet itself, Yeovil, Somerset put Poole within easy reach, though the Solent might suit an Isle-of-Wight-based builder better; [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md) and Alastair had, in fact, already settled on firmer plans of their own for the year — most of May 2008 cruising the West Country together, calling at Plymouth for the start of the Transat on 11 May and the Jester Challenge on 31 May, with the Isles of Scilly hoped for somewhere between the two.[140]

## Retrofit philosophy, and a planned floor-level mod for a folding bike

Weighing in on a newcomer's aft-deck fuel-compartment proposal elsewhere on the list (see [auxiliary outboard power — petrol safety practice, and two aft-deck fuel-compartment proposals](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#petrol-safety-practice-and-two-aft-deck-fuel-compartment-proposals)), Alastair set out his own settled approach to modifying "Little Jim": build to the stock plans first, then retrofit an idea later only if it still seemed worth the trouble once the boat was finished. In practice, none of his own pre-build ideas were ever actually implemented, usually because each one turned out to conflict with some other aspect not appreciated at the time, and his real mods have stayed minor — cutting the hatch covers in two, for easier access. One larger change remained under consideration: cutting the access to the forward stateroom down to floor level, which would lose the storage space currently under the forward floor but should just allow a folding bike to be stowed there.[141]

## Continuing the "Hello :o) In new here" outboard-and-fuel debate

The tiller-clearance problem sets the real limit on aft-deck fuel stowage: the tiller sweeps under most of the aft deck, leaving room for a fuel compartment only where already proposed on the list, and a long-abandoned attempt to fit an anchor well accessed through the deck confirmed the same space just isn't there — "a boat is a compromise," and not worth losing the things one likes about a design chasing some "improvement."[142] A suggested aft-facing tiller, which would free up some of that space, was weighed and declined: it would be far more complicated to repair in a remote anchorage, would need extra length to clear the mainsheet, and would leave too shallow an angle on the steering rope — the stock arrangement stayed.[143] Weighing in once more on outboard mounting generally, a side-mounted electric motor kept strictly for emergencies and up-river use at a trip's end remained the preferred answer, alongside a wariness — after repeated green water aboard in conditions later judged unwise to have sailed into — of both deck-stowed fuel cans and transom-mounted motors. Full debate at [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#aft-deck-tiller-clearance-an-abandoned-anchor-well-and-yoke-or-crank-steering-declined).[144]

## The 2008 Scilly Isles cruise: Little Jim's side of the log

The West Country cruise planned with [Faith](/boats/faith.md) that spring went ahead in May: waiting at Plymouth for Alastair to arrive, Faith's owner anchored off the large public slipway at Mount Batten on 12 May 2008, then anchored in shallow water alongside it and waded ashore to help — "the launching was a painless affair," and by 1315 the two boats were anchored together back at their Plymouth anchorage.[145] A photograph of Alastair standing inside "little Jim," taken once the pair had joined up at Plymouth, was captioned as proof that "Paradoxes have full standing headroom."[146]

Crossing to the Isles of Scilly on 18 May, both boats scraped their rudders on the sand crossing the shallows between Samson and Crow Point on a falling tide, and anchored together at Tresco where "apart from our Paradoxes there was only one yacht at a mooring" — the two boats moored there with lines run to the crown of each anchor, so they could be retrieved from the beach rather than left to strand as the tide rose. Full joint account of the crossing and the cruise's course at [Faith — to the Scilly Isles and back, with Little Jim](/boats/faith.md#to-the-scilly-isles-and-back-with-little-jim).[147]

Returning to New Grimsby a week later on a falling tide with under an hour to low water, Alastair took "little Jim" east of Puffin Island where the rocks lay exposed, shortened sail, and was forced to wade her through for a short stretch, while Faith took a deeper route north by Yellow Rock to rejoin him; both boats then crossed between Bryher and Tresco in no more than 3 ft of water, trailing weed brushing their bottoms.[148] At St Agnes, Alastair devised the pair's landing technique for a rocky lee shore: bringing "little Jim" in close, dropping one anchor, sculling to shallow water at the rocks, and laying a second anchor in a crevice above the waterline; Faith's owner then sculled his own boat alongside, climbed aboard "little Jim," and waded ashore to push her clear once she had landed.[149]

As a severe gale warning for force 9 reached the anchorage at New Grimsby during the same cruise (see [Faith — the same gale warning](/boats/faith.md#to-the-scilly-isles-and-back-with-little-jim)), Alastair took "little Jim's" mast down as a precaution ahead of the blow — recalled years later, in an aside about rigging an umbrella over a hatchway, as the reason an umbrella "wouldn't have stood a chance" that day.[150]

At Porthloo Beach, St Mary's, the two boats were beached and anchored together once more, "seldom... one faster than the other" under sail — "If one got ahead an appreciable distance, her skipper would spill wind until the other boat drew near" — and kept in touch by VHF on passage throughout the cruise.[151] A fuller day-by-day account of the whole voyage is kept in [Bill's Log](/references/bills-log.md).

A postscript reported a few weeks after the boats were both back on their trailers: sailing hard on the cruise had been enough to impress on Alastair just how much the boom and yard actually flex under load.[152] A secondhand account of the same cruise adds one further detail: even sculling that devotedly, a tow under Faith's outboard was accepted when the chance arose.[153]

## Glassing the deck before the cabin, recalled (June 2008)

Confirming a fellow builder's tire-cushioned technique for rolling a hull over (see [Ironsides — fiberglassing before the cabin](/boats/ironsides.md#fiberglassing-before-the-cabin-a-tire-cushioned-roll-over-and-the-rudder-blades-lead-cast)), Alastair recalled his own build sequence for "Little Jim": glassing the deck before fitting the cabin, so the glass's edge sits tucked under the cabin structure rather than butting up against it — judged the neater way to do it, and easier besides, since a single sheet of glass cloth was stretched over the whole deck at once, with the openings cut out only once the epoxy had started to cure.[154]

## A deliberate no-motor choice, and the case for leaving it at home

Alastair carries no petrol engine as a fallback aboard "Little Jim" at all, keeping only the side-mounted electric motor already described above for genuine emergencies, and defended the choice as deliberate: going without a motor to fall back on is what actually forces sail- and yuloh-handling skill to develop. Recalling Faith's owner's own experience of Paradox, he noted that despite years of general sailing experience beforehand, adapting to the boat took a steep learning curve — and that a habit of starting the engine whenever conditions turned hard on the wind, tacking into a bay or similar, meant fewer chances to build those skills under sail alone. Nothing wrong with using an engine, in his view, but a sailor who actually wants to learn to sail and scull well is better off leaving it at home: there is nothing like being a couple of miles from home on a falling tide and a fading wind to teach a sailor to get the best out of the boat, and getting home under sail and yuloh alone in those conditions is "incredibly satisfying."[165]

Pressed in jest on what happens when that satisfying return does not come off, Alastair took the joke in stride: not getting home simply means spending another night aboard instead of getting home to mow the lawn — and once the sailing and yuloh are properly mastered, "you will always have a neat lawn" regardless.[166][167]

## A load-tested anchor-rode measurement, and a passage to Clovelly (July 2008)

Taking "Little Jim" out for a weekend, Alastair launched at **Appledore**, on the north Devon coast, on a Friday evening and sailed to **Clovelly** on the Saturday, returning Sunday. At anchor off Appledore that first night, a spring balance carried aboard let him put an actual figure on anchor-rode load across a range of conditions: with about 1 knot of tide and a force 3 wind blowing the same direction, the load read **1.5 kg** head to tide, rising to **5 kg** lying broadside to the tide with the hull upright, **8.5 kg** broadside and heeled about 10 degrees into the tide, and back down to **5 kg** heeled the same amount away from the tide. The figures do not reproduce actual sailing loads, and heeling was not enough to lift the chine runners clear of the water, but stand as the archive's only quantified anchor-rode load series measured across a range of heel and current combinations — see also [ground tackle and anchoring](/operations/ground-tackle.md) for the wider anchoring record this adds to. The passage itself, in two legs, covered about 10 miles: beating out of the estuary against both wind and tide (2.25 nm as the crow flies, 6.75 nm actually sailed) then close-hauled on to Clovelly took 6 hours, while the broad-reaching return took 2 hours 45 minutes.[168]

The load data drew a request from a fellow builder for a similar test of towing drag at different speeds, with and without the rudder down.[169] Alastair confirmed the load figures above had all been taken with the rudder raised.[170]

## A topping-lift question from abroad, answered with photographs (August 2008)

A fellow owner's private query about "little Jim"'s topping lift — the direct reply having bounced back — was relayed to the whole list instead, together with the answer itself (see [furling system — what the topping lift does, rope choice, and use](/components/rig/furling.md#what-the-topping-lift-does-rope-choice-and-use)). ## A partial-reef account in a Force 5-7, and the hull's way after masting down (September 2008)

A few days later, Alastair gave a further firsthand account of "little Jim" sailing well shorthanded: out in a forecast **Force 5-7**, with only about **2 ft of luff unrolled**, she was still making over **5 knots**. Lowering the mast entirely to pass under a bridge, she kept moving at **2 knots** with no sail set at all.[175] The observation drew an admiring reply crediting the hull's continued way, once masting down, to the same **"lifting body"** hull behaviour claimed for this type by Sven Yrvind, working in combination with the boat's own relatively heavy displacement (see [how chine runners work — the designer's own account: a lifting-body system](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#the-designers-own-account-2007-a-lifting-body-system) for the fuller theory, and [Sven Yrvind — voyages and boats](/people/yrvind/voyages-and-boats.md#a-2008-sighting-yrvind-½-under-construction) for Yrvind's own adoption of the Paradox rig around the same date) — offered as confirmation of just how much a low-key design can achieve.[176]

Downplaying the drama afterward, Alastair explained the moment felt less exciting than it sounded: he had started out with more sail up than needed, the boat felt over-pressed, and reducing sail settled everything back to normal — it would have been a different story going the other way. The passage itself ran from Calshot east to Langstone Harbour, in a wind pretty well due west, passing under the bridge at the top of Hayling Island into Chichester Harbour; timed close to low water, with more mud and less water than usual, conditions there were calmer than the forecast alone would suggest. Dropping the mast meant stepping onto the foredeck to lay it down on top of the furled sail, then running off almost dead downwind.[177]

## The forward-stateroom access, cut down for bike stowage

Alastair carried through on the floor-level modification he had been weighing since January 2008 (see [above](#retrofit-philosophy-and-a-planned-floor-level-mod-for-a-folding-bike)): the access opening into the forward stateroom was cut down flush with the cabin floor, giving up the storage space that had sat beneath it so a folding bike could be stowed there instead — being limited to walking once ashore can sharply limit a cruise's range. The change served stowage rather than headroom: at 5 ft 6 in, the stock cabin already suited him well for sleeping. He judged the result a general improvement on first inspection, with a fuller verdict to follow once back afloat the next weekend.[179] The same flattened, undivided floor running forward to bulkhead 1 also proved easier to load with clothing, bedding, and other gear on the trailer than the stepped arrangement it replaced.[180] The modification drew interest from a fellow builder then finishing his own boat, who asked to see it in more detail and any photographs of the bike stowed in place, going in and out of the boat.[181]

## Cruise provisioning, and a folding bike stowed aboard

The bicycle stowed in the enlarged forward stateroom (see [above](#the-forward-stateroom-access-cut-down-for-bike-stowage)) is a **Brompton**, a British folding design with 16 in wheels — the smallest wheel size Alastair judged safe, having also looked at, and rejected, an even more compact 6 in-wheeled folding "A-bike" over doubts about hitting a rut safely at that size. It takes up a good deal of room in the newly-opened space, but since even before the modification he rarely used even half of it, the loss cost him little; a fuller verdict on the installation would wait for an actual sail with the bike aboard.[182]

Answering a question about how much storage margin an extended cruise actually leaves, Alastair reported using only about a third of "Little Jim"'s available storage on a three-week cruise — packed neatly, he reckoned he could have fitted in at least three times as much as he took, much of it carried only because it might prove useful and largely going unused. Paradox's stowage costs nothing in trim, since any gear weight simply displaces an equal weight of lead ballast. For an ordinary weekend trip his own routine load runs to a week's tinned food, several sets of dry clothes, a large battery, three anchors with chain, four fenders, a full toolbox, a similar box of spares, and, now, the bicycle — all readily absorbed into the boat's various storage areas.[183] Once the bike was actually aboard, the fixed lead came down further still — to about **60 kg**, counting the bike's own battery as part of the ballast load, a further real-world instance of gear weight displacing an equal weight of lead.[184]

## Opening up the forward access further, and a folding-bike seat-post swap

Answering a fellow UK builder's own account of designing a lowered stateroom floor into a Paradox derivative from the outset (see [Noli — a lowered floor built in from the start](/boats/noli.md#a-lowered-floor-built-in-from-the-start-and-a-bike-still-too-tall-to-fit-through)), Alastair confirmed the same trade-off from his own retrofit: the step as originally drawn used to dig into his side reaching forward, and the dividers made the space feel restricted — opening the access up should ease reaching in, though with the dividers gone more can probably be packed in, at the cost of making any one thing harder to find again, no real problem given his own surplus of otherwise-unused storage. His secondhand folding bike came with an already-fitted extended seat post that did not pass through the new opening, until a chance swap that summer with another cyclist wanting the longer post, in exchange for a standard-length one, brought the fit to "almost perfect."[185]

## A full accounting of departures from the stock plans

A beach conversation with a passerby who took "Little Jim" for a stock, as-designed Paradox prompted Alastair to write out every way his own boat actually differs from the plans — organized as changes made during construction, changes made from further experience afloat, and ideas he would try building again — the fullest single-boat accounting of its kind on record, and one that corrects a couple of details this page had previously carried only vaguely.[187]

Of the construction-time changes, most stand on their own: the mast carries no lightning-protection rod, reasoning that kind of storm is not much of a problem in the UK; and the sail's grommets, for fastening it to the yard, are moved onto the side of the sleeve rather than sitting at the plans' own corners — which, restated here the day after Alastair had offered exactly this as his own fix for another builder's yard/grommet mismatch (see [above](#continuing-the-fitting-the-sail-thread-a-sheet-9-furl-direction-question)), turns out to have been a standing feature of "Little Jim"'s own sail all along rather than a fix improvised for the occasion. The **window let into the hatch**, already known from the 2003 Solent crossing as the one place condensation collects and drips overnight (see [above](#a-first-crossing-of-the-solent-poole-to-cowes-september-2003)), is explained here for the first time as a deliberate fitting, essential for watching the sail with the hatch shut — the same requirement independently reached from the helm on "Zoë"'s own first extended cruise and answered there with a skylight instead (see [Hatch — steering from below, and the case for a port in the hatch](/components/hatch.md#steering-from-below-and-the-case-for-a-port-in-the-hatch)).

The changes made from experience add real detail the page did not have before. The **floor hatch** — previously recorded on this page only as "cutting the hatch covers in two" — turns out to mean four covers rather than the stock two, letting the forward sections open without moving the seat. A shelf fitted to the bottom of the aft bins, keeping their contents clear of the bilges, and the folding-bike stowage cut into the forward stateroom (see [above](#the-forward-stateroom-access-cut-down-for-bike-stowage)) are both already told in full elsewhere on this page and are not repeated here. Three items are genuinely new: a small drain hole at the rear lower corner of each side window, letting water standing on the window ledge run out (though Alastair himself doubts it was worth the effort, having since concluded the hatch is better closed before water starts coming in at all); the yuloh pivot, moved about 4 in nearer the handle — restating, in rounder terms, the 150 mm relocation already on record from June 2008 (see [Yuloh — building the yuloh itself](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket) for the two figures, left unreconciled); and a shackle hung on the rudder's up-haul line between the last fairlead and the cleat, stopping it dropping off the cleat's groove as the rudder kicks up (see [Rudder and chine experiments](/components/rudder.md#a-lost-rudder-up-haul-and-sailing-on-with-reduced-rudder-area)).

Of the ideas considered for a future build rather than actually tried, none has been built on "Little Jim": toe rails fitted outside the hull rather than on the deck, to double as a rubbing strip (see [Enuf — toe rail: is it needed, and where to source one in the UK](/boats/enuf.md#toe-rail-is-it-needed-and-where-to-source-one-in-the-uk)); plumbing and a wiring duct run in during construction, with standard electric bilge pumps fitted permanently inside the ballast tanks (see [Water tank construction and lining](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#a-permanently-plumbed-electric-bilge-pump-considered-but-not-built)); yuloh and sail storage hoops built without any vertical section, so lines slip over them instead of snagging (see [Deck — stowage loops for the yuloh and lowered rig](/components/deck.md#stowage-loops-for-the-yuloh-and-lowered-rig)); and, for the floor-hatch hinges, which foul with grit and need regular cleaning and oiling, either fabric hinges or lifting the boards out entirely, "as Bill has done on Faith" (see [Faith — a removable floorboard technique for full storage and ballast access](/boats/faith.md#a-removable-floorboard-technique-for-full-storage-and-ballast-access)). On fitting an outboard for regular use, the list restates a preference already on record (see [above](#a-deliberate-no-motor-choice-and-the-case-for-leaving-it-at-home)) with one new data point: his early fears about a transom mount have turned out to be mostly unfounded in practice, even though the transom remains, in his own words, only "the best of a bad lot."

## A transom-slap noise at anchor, explained by trim

A "plunging" noise sometimes heard aboard while sleeping at anchor traces to trim rather than any defect: with the bottom of the transom sitting proud of the water, ripples slap directly against the exposed hull bottom. Loading the boat more heavily, so she sits deeper, stopped the noise recurring — the rudder's boarding step, in that same loaded trim, staying fully submerged throughout.[188]

## A rudder-stock pintle repair, and the folding bike's first cruising season

A pleasant weekend sail at the start of March 2009 turned up, during a routine check of the trailer afterward, scratching on the rudder stock and its step far worse than the paint-only wear on the hull's own bottom. Pulling the rudder to add a fresh layer of glass — the first time it had been off since the 2003 launch — Alastair found a real surprise: the lower pintle, a 12 mm stainless rod, was floating free inside the stock, though undamaged. The original fit had only roughened the rod with emery paper before bedding it in epoxy in an oversized hole — evidently not enough of a mechanical key on its own. After draining and drying the stock out, the fix was a few saw cuts into the rod's sides for a proper key before re-bonding it the same way; a check of the pintle at the end of every season is now the standing precaution. See [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#fitting-the-lower-pintle-without-drilling-the-rudder-stock) for the general technique this adds to.[192]

Separately, Alastair reported the Brompton folding bicycle already stowed forward (see [above](#cruise-provisioning-and-a-folding-bike-stowed-aboard)) had by then proved itself over its first full cruising season: kept out of the way forward while under sail, it markedly extends the boat's range once ashore, and makes it far more practical to leave the boat at anchor and go back for the car and trailer if the weather turns — being a folder, it travels just as easily by bus, train, or in a car's boot.[192][193]

## A taller sailor's comfort verdict, and dropping the cabin sole considered (October 2010)

Replying to a Taiwan-based sailor's account of finding a stock Paradox's interior tight for his own 6'3" frame (see [operations/crew-size-and-fit.md](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#the-thread-continues-a-taiwan-based-sailors-own-fit-account-october-2010)), Alastair reported that the same forward-stateroom lip cut down for bike stowage (see [above](#the-forward-stateroom-access-cut-down-for-bike-stowage)) let him sleep further forward with his feet in the forepeak rather than the main cabin, keeping only emergency dry clothing, a towel, and a blanket stowed forward of bulkhead one and crawling past bulkhead two only at anchor. ## A return to water ballast, for easier handling out of the water (by 2010)

A further reversal followed the 2007 switch to lead (see [above](#abandoning-water-ballast-for-lead-after-a-leaking-tank-inspection-cover-may-2007)): having sailed a season on lead alone, Alastair went back to water ballast, judging it easier to manage out of the water despite the leaking inspection covers that had originally prompted the move to lead.[195]

Alastair filled in the fuller story in January 2014, replying to a direct question about the switch: the tanks were never really used for storage even during the lead interlude — with all his cruising gear, including the bicycle, aboard he already has plenty of empty stowage elsewhere. Leaking inspection hatches (see above) were the whole reason for the lead, installed at the same 70 kg weight the water ballast it replaced would have carried and, unlike water, staying neutrally buoyant should the boat ever swamp; it was only ever meant as an interim measure while he sourced reliable, genuinely watertight hatches. Once found, water ballast returned for good, but run actively rather than passively: electric pumps fitted inside the tanks empty them, and a loose pump on a wire fills them again, so a long row can be lightened by pumping the tanks dry, refilled once sailing becomes possible, and the boat trailered 70 kg lighter by leaving them empty.[219][220] A follow-up question the same week settled the pump count precisely: three electric pumps in total, plus a manual pump kept as a backup.[221][222] Asked separately whether the missing 70 kg makes a difference launching or retrieving from the trailer, Alastair reported no problem either way, with no sign the trailer was suffering — though he still felt it was better to lose the extra weight, and simple to do once the pumps were sorted.[223][224]

Restating the same forward-access modification once more in 2021 for a Facebook question about fitting very tall sailors, Alastair described it unchanged: the hatch from the cabin through to forward storage, cut down flush with the top of the water tanks, still gains the extra sleeping length a taller frame needs at the feet.[246]

## A lee-shore self-rescue under sail alone (December 2013)

On one occasion Alastair had grounded "little Jim" on a lee shore in about 20 knots of wind — in an estuary, so the waves were not significant. To get off, he hoisted sail, stood behind the boat to point her into the wind, dropped the rudder blade onto the mud, and pushed her out into deeper water; putting one foot on the boarding step let him scoot the boat clear before climbing over the aft deck to take the sheet and tiller. The manoeuvre was "surprisingly easy" and needed no outside help, in contrast to a group of dinghy-sailing friends caught on the same shore, none of whom could sail their own centreboard boats off it — he eventually set an anchor and floated a fender down to them so they could pull themselves off into water deep enough to lower their boards.[218]

## A further 2015 restatement: ballast that doubles as gear, and the bike's stowage explained again

Asked in passing what else can serve in place of lead, Alastair answered with the same principle behind his own long-declining ballast figure (above): spare anchors, a sizeable battery, emergency stores, and a full tool kit and spares all add weight low in the boat while earning their keep twice over, so a boat built deliberately heavy can simply carry whatever "might come in useful." Of his own roughly 75 lb of actual lead by that point, the bulk of his effective ballast was made up of exactly this kind of gear, the bicycle foremost among it.[225] Pressed for how the bike itself was stowed, he confirmed it is the Brompton already described above (see [Cruise provisioning, and a folding bike stowed aboard](#cruise-provisioning-and-a-folding-bike-stowed-aboard)), fitted through the opening cut through bulkhead 2 down to the cabin sole into the forward stateroom, and restated — six years after its first cruising season — the same range-extending case for carrying it: having the bike aboard makes him more adventurous with the weather when coasting, since failing to sail back is no longer a real problem when he can cycle, bus, or train back to collect the car and trailer instead.[226] Weeks later, comparing notes with another owner's own widened-boat Brompton stowage (see [Ironsides — a 2015 Florida cruise](/boats/ironsides.md#a-2015-florida-cruise-the-st-johns-river)), he restated the same through-bulkhead-2 fit once more.[227]

## The build

Before cutting any wood, the whole boat was assembled first as a 3D model in the CATIA software used professionally at work — time well spent, since it settled a number of questions about how the parts actually fit together before a single panel was cut.[247]

As built, the transom stands 516 mm overall beyond sheet 8's own figure; a builder following the plans is better off leaving the transom oversized until the deck beams are fitted, rather than cutting it to final height beforehand.[248] The transom's own missing radius dimension was worked back separately, from sheet 10's deck-beam information, and pencilled onto the plans as 57 mm; fitting the deck without any trouble confirmed the figure, which was independently cross-checked and matched against a digital model built from the plans by another builder.[342][343] The cabin-top and hatch-cover beams likewise depart from bare plan dimensions once fitted: the foremost cabin beam came out at 52 mm, the lower aft cabin beam at 75 mm, both bevelled freehand rather than to a jig, and laminated up rather than routed out for want of a router at the time.[249] Sitting with the hatch closed, headroom under it comes to 930 mm (about 36.5 in).[250] The stock sleeping length is 1.8 m (roughly 5 ft 11 in), extendable by a further 0.8 m (about 31 in) once the forward opening is cut down,[251] and the flat sleeping area itself measures 1.9 m by 0.9 m, extended a further 0.5 m of legroom by that same modification to the forward access.[252]

She is built in marine ply chosen deliberately over exterior grade, on the reasoning that the extra cost buys less ongoing maintenance and needs no more of a building floor than the cheaper alternative.[253] The grade used was the cheapest BS1088-certified marine ply available locally, fully skinned in epoxy and glass outside and epoxy and polystyrene inside, and never re-inspected for ageing since it was skinned; exterior-grade ply from a home centre would only be worth using where the specified marine grade genuinely could not be sourced, the design's own margin absorbing any slight void-related weakness.[254] Doubling up thinner sheets in place of a single specified thickness is judged worth the bother only where that thickness cannot be had locally at all, since gluing two layers void-free eats a lot of epoxy; the hull itself went together in the cheapest marine grade with no bending trouble — the sides came round with two fingers' pressure, and the bottom pulled down by nailing bow to stern with the stern propped up and a helper's weight added for the final stretch.[255] On epoxy quantity, the working principle was to buy more than seemed necessary rather than run short mid-job: two 25-litre lots went into the build, with some left over, against a material bill that ran to roughly ten sheets of plywood and 40 to 50 litres of epoxy in total.[256][257]

Deck panels are nailed the same way as the rest of the hull, heads hammered flush and left deliberately unfaired under the glass and paint — a choice made so the fastener positions stay identifiable under the finish and can be avoided later when screwing anything else down.[258] Bulkhead 3's edge joint departs from the plans' usual cleat-and-nail pattern, the one place stock construction nails directly into the bulkhead's own edge instead: two locating pins per side, no cleat at all.[259] Copper nails, riveted through, fasten the plywood doublers forming the bottom of the forward cabin and the front window's lower edge wherever no structure sits below the deck there.[260] The chine-runner blanks proved impossible to bend into position in one piece; the runners are cut into roughly foot-length sections instead, fitted before the bottom panel goes on.[261] Deck-beam cleats were left proud rather than trimmed flush before the deck went on, shaped only once assembly reached that stage; the interior is finished with a primer chosen specifically to stick to epoxy, and a foam mat softens the knees when kneeling forward on the sole.[262]

The finish has held up well. Four UK summers of sun on bare, exposed epoxy left no obvious damage — long enough to suspect the standard "sunlight degrades epoxy" warning was calibrated to Florida conditions rather than English ones — while the painted areas exposed to the same sun peeled steadily instead, a choice since regretted.[263] One outright paint failure traced to a plain building error rather than the product: an epoxy-compatible primer, applied in the depths of winter, turned out only afterward to carry a minimum-application-temperature warning on its own tin.[264] After following the plans' instruction to glass the hull "all over," only two small spots on the bottom showed any real wear after two full seasons.[265]

Building the deck and roof beams used a steam box built rather than bought: a tea urn boiled the water, a metal plate with a mesh-covered hole served as the lid, and a length of plastic drain pipe held the timber being steamed. Four beam lengths went in together, held under a fixed clamp and pulled down with a ratchet clamp once soft enough to bend.[266]

## The rig

The mast carries a waterproof electrical socket embedded just above its solid base, reached from inside the cabin by a plug fed through the vent box; in hindsight, the tricolour and all-round lights fitted this way overlap too much between their sectors, and only the all-round white light — legal under COLREGs for a boat this size — would go back in if the rig were built again.[267] The steering-line's forward block is screwed directly to the intermediate deck beam, simpler than the plans' own mid-air mounting position.[268] The masthead light itself is a compact LED unit, under 2 in across and 1.5 in tall, combining the tricolour and all-round functions with a two-wire reverse-polarity switch; over five years of use the tricolour has been switched on only once, the all-round light used occasionally at anchor, and in practice a hand-held torch inspires more confidence than either fitted light.[269]

The sail is UK-made, tan, in a 6 oz cloth — heavier than most — and bears against the mast on the starboard tack; the original single-part sheet became the plans' own two-part purchase once fitted. The continuous halyard and furling line trade an instant sail-drop for a cleaner cabin, and the heavier cloth can make the drop-onto-roof problem worse without a topping lift to check it; the lift is normally slackened once sail is set, but left tight when a reef is expected soon. Sheet 9 shows the sail, and its furling line, running to the port (outside) side of the boom, which has given no trouble here.[270][270b] The reefing triangle and boom taper were worked out over three seasons: an under-tapered clew was retrofitted with a rigid-foam wedge.[271] The Mk1 boom taper measured 50 by 38 mm at the clew and 35 by 38 mm at the tack;[271b] the Mk2 sail's forward creep runs to about 2.5 in, comfortably within the boom's own length, and furling itself proved as easy as the WaterTribe video promised, once the hands hardened to the rope.[271c]

The yard is Douglas fir and has needed no attention at all in thirteen years.[272] It works as a double cantilever; a mast-chafe problem was fixed by lengthening the sleeve gap and adding a leather mast sleeve, and a mast-mounted yard traveller — borrowed from a Welsford Houdini design — was floated but never built.[273]

The yuloh is laminated from two planks over a 30 mm block, its pivot a 10 mm stainless-studding bolt through an epoxied ball seated in a Tufnol socket, with a bungee-cord fix, credited to Dave Bolduc, to stop the ball popping off in use.[274] The blade measures 3.5 in (90 mm) at its widest point.[274b] It was shaped entirely by hand — bulk removal with a plane or a coarse sanding disc in an angle grinder, then a strop of sandpaper worked alternately over the corner for a smooth curve, checked against cardboard templates throughout.[275] Whether the blade's curve should run its full length or only near the tip probably matters less than it seems; a full-length curve simply stows more neatly.[276] The 30 mm curve, however produced, shifts the handle forward and down by about 30 mm, not a large change; part of the reason for moving the pivot hole nearer the handle in the first place was to stop the handle striking the sculler's face on the pull stroke.[277] That pivot hole was later moved a further 150 mm toward the handle, since the lower gearing at the original position gave too little purchase; a worn socket was bored oversize, filled with epoxy and fibres, and re-drilled to fit, and a smear of candle wax cures a squeak plain water won't.[278] The pivot itself is seated directly in thickened epoxy and simply re-drilled to size as it wears, needing refurbishment roughly every four or five years.[279]

The boom's tack-end swivel-rod bore was drilled oversize and filled with epoxy to give a copper-pipe bearing surface for the stainless rod; the same fix — drilled oversize, filled, re-tapped — was used again at the sheet and topping-lift tang bolt.[280] The topping lift itself runs as an eye, not a block, on the mast's aft side, a two-part span with the port side of the mast opposite the sail's starboard side, and doubles as a tie-off point on deck at night.[281] Deck loops are made of copper pipe rather than brass tube, judged the weakest of the boat's exterior fittings and kept low to guard the starboard one in particular.[282] The mast-foot electrical connector is waterproof, fed through from inside; a side, below-deck wire exit is worth avoiding, since it makes stepping and unstepping the mast harder than an above-deck exit does.[283]

A tapped-epoxy fix at the boom tang, matched by a rounded boom end and a 10 mm spacer, followed a passage sailed hard enough — on the joint cruise with "Faith" through the Scilly Isles — to make plain just how much the boom and yard actually flex under real load.[284] The furling axle's own long-term bearing fix has proved trouble-free for years: its end domed, a cap brazed over a length of water pipe and bonded into the boom's cap end first, so the axle rides a rattling-loose fit against the cap, kept turning with nothing more than a smear of grease. The tack-arrangement spacer that failed once, before this fix, had earlier been bent and rebuilt in the same repair session.[285] Restated a year later for a new correspondent, both fixes remained unchanged and still working.[286] The topping lift itself is a simple two-line design: a rope from each end of the boom joins and runs through an eye at the masthead, the only real care needed being to keep the yard on the correct side of it when starting to hoist.[287]

One change was tried and reversed: a self-simplification of the forward reefing fitting failed its own test hoist, and the fitting was rebuilt back to the stock design — which has worked perfectly ever since, since it carries tension when the sail is fully hoisted and shifts into compression once reefed.[288] The tack strop's own forked spacer top traces to a plans revision from May 1994; working from an older, unforked plan set explains why the joint collapsed sideways under a reefed load exactly the way the original, unrebuilt strut here had failed before.[289] A worked example of the continuous halyard-and-furling-line trick: 12 m of rope leaves about 2 m of slack, doubled to 1 m, coiled in the cabin once the sail is set.[290]

## The rudder

A friction lock on the tiller rope — two roughly 3 in discs, one on a bolt and one on a nut fastened under the side deck, clamping the rope between them — is rarely needed in ordinary sailing, where friction alone usually suffices, but becomes necessary in shallow water with the rudder blade partly raised, where the load on the tiller line can run high.[291] The pintles themselves sit on the centerline, which puts the rudder blade slightly to starboard and the tiller slightly to port — a minor cosmetic asymmetry with no effect on handling.[340]

An early repair, over the winter of 2004, reinforced the rudder's worn leading edge with split, annealed 15 mm copper water pipe, epoxy-riveted on through countersunk holes. The same 15 mm split copper pipe, used between the furling spindle and the deck, had given no trouble there either, sitting mostly in tension — until an unexpected gybe sailing home the next day (a Force 5 following wind at 4-5 kn, then a broad reach at 3-4 kn with the sail half reefed) badly twisted that fitting's split end, discovered only once trailered. The fix was to hammer the bends back out and solder on doublers cut from spare rudder-repair tubing — stiffer afterward, though untested at the time.[341]

A routine trailer check in early 2009 turned up scratching on the rudder stock and its step far worse than the wear on the hull's own bottom paint. Pulling the rudder — the first time it had been off since the 2003 launch — showed the lower pintle, a 12 mm stainless rod, floating free inside the stock though otherwise undamaged: the original fit had only roughened the rod with emery paper before bedding it in epoxy in an oversized hole, not enough of a mechanical key on its own. After drying the stock out, a few saw cuts into the rod's sides gave it a proper key before re-bonding it the same way, and a check of the pintle has been a standing end-of-season precaution ever since. The lower pintle is suspected to take the heaviest load of any rudder fitting, especially grounding, and a side push is thought to load the joints between the rudder stock's own plywood laminates — a risk a ready-made rudder, rather than one built in step with the hull, might not survive as well.[292]

The rudder's leading edge and bottom took an early repair — split copper water pipe hammered around the wear points and bonded on with epoxy — that has stayed sound for three seasons since, aside from the paint wearing off it.[293] Confirming how the beaching mechanism actually behaves: an upward force on the rudder, as when the boat grounds or dries out, pushes the blade up against the underside of the hull rather than loading the pintles, so bouncing on a beach — though it sounds and feels alarming — does no real harm to the structure.[294] Left to swing freely down, the blade clonks the transom corner but then moves clear enough to allow the tiller's full travel; the haul-up rope can be used to hold it at any shallower angle instead.[295] On a broad reach the mainsheet has a tendency to drop into the gap between the rudder stock and the transom, fixed with a small wooden fairlead insert.[296]

The rudder's one real failure came off Lepe Spit in the Solent in August 2020, on the last day of a week's cruise: running downwind for eight miles in a Force 5 with a foul tide, the blade sheared cleanly through its pivot bolt in the disturbed water — "Well, oh dear, I thought, or words to that effect." No other damage resulted, and the boat came through the rest of the passage without incident.[297]

## Ballast and trim

Filling and draining the ballast tanks by tap takes 5 to 10 minutes in practice, longer by pump; forcing water in too fast once blew an inspection hatch clean off. Sailing light, with the tanks empty, is noticeably less stable even with the full lead ballast still aboard.[298] A bottled reserve of drinking water rides inside the ballast tank itself — ordinary 2-litre soft-drink bottles, fished out as needed and topped up from whatever the boat happens to be floating in via the bilge pump.[299]

The ballast itself is fastened removably: lead pigs drilled and tapped on top, bolted to a plate anchored into the chine log — not especially rigid, but showing no sign of movement over the years. The general recommendation to a first-time builder is to leave the ballast unfastened at first, since pigs often need shifting around on early sails to find the right trim.[300] The water ballast tank tops were bedded down the same way as any other join: a generous coat of thickened glue on the underside, then a hand reached in through the access hatches to work the squeezed-out excess into a fillet by thumb, gloved against the epoxy — a method that has kept that joint entirely leak-free, unlike the inspection hatches themselves.[347] The lead itself came originally as seventeen bricks of scrap wheel-balancing weights, handed over by a local tyre shop for the price of two pints of beer — tyre shops otherwise having to pay to have the scrap hauled away — and melted down and recast before going aboard as pigs; only four of those original bricks remain aboard today, the rest displaced over the years by gear that earns its keep twice over. "Ballast" itself is a slippery term on a boat like this: honestly counted, the figure could run anywhere from 100 to 350 lb depending on what is allowed to qualify.[307]

Being underballasted has proved forgiving rather than dangerous in practice: "Enuf" launched significantly underballasted with nothing worse to show for it than increased tenderness, and the water ballast here has twice been forgotten entirely before a sail, noticed only once back on deck.[301] The tank water itself is not for drinking — an "interesting slime" turned up in it after the first season — and is topped up instead from whatever the boat is floating in, via the bilge pump.[302] There is no fixed pipework to the tanks at all beyond the vents and deck-mounted fillers shown on the plans; submersible electric bilge pumps handle both filling and draining.[303]

An early, careful tally settled the lead ballast at a precise figure: weighing the boat, her gear, and her sailor together, then adding pigs until the total reached 640 kg overall, gave 120 kg of lead — 96 kg (48 kg per side) carried just forward of bulkhead 3, and a further 24 kg behind bulkhead 4 — a figure rechecked, and found unchanged, each spring as accumulated gear crept the boat's overall weight upward.[344]

That fore-and-aft balance was tested to destruction once, deliberately. A winter of adding gear — nothing individually heavy bar a second anchor — raised a worry that the boat was carrying too much weight altogether, and a trip sailed with the water ballast tanks left empty showed no ill effect, so 100 lb of lead was pulled for the next outing: 50 lb from the stern locker, where the new anchor had just gone, and 50 lb from by bulkhead 3. The result was immediate and unwelcome — with the centre of gravity shifted forward, the boat picked up considerable weather helm and lost her previously excellent tracking outright. The fix followed at once: the fifty pounds went straight back into the stern locker, with more lead again pulled from bulkhead 3 to correct the balance the other way.[345]

Ballast has come down steadily as gear that pulls double duty has taken its place. By one point the lead alone had dropped to about 36 kg (75 lb), down from roughly 60 kg only a month before — the bicycle, brought aboard around the same time, having been the last big non-ballast addition; the reasoning offered for spending on gear instead of dead lead is that a storm anchor or a good battery does the same ballasting job while actually being useful.[304] An 80Ah AGM battery, weighing about 12 kg, was fitted forward of bulkhead 3 to replace one of the lead bricks almost pound for pound, and runs a small trolling motor for about 8 miles on a charge besides.[305] In full sailing trim the boat displaces 640 kg (1,410 lb): 180 kg of that is lead, 70 kg is water ballast, and roughly 180 kg remains for stores, anchors, batteries, and the rest. On at least one occasion she carried two extra people aboard without any noticeable change in sailing performance, though the cabin grew crowded and the outing stayed short.[306]

## Systems and fit-out

A full account of living aboard turns on a handful of settled routines. Three anchors — a 5 kg Bruce, a 10 lb Fisherman, and a folding grapnel — all launch from the cabin onto a permanent bow line without anyone needing to leave it. Cooking is done seated, twisting round for simple jobs and kneeling on the floor for anything more ambitious; the heads means standing and leaning over the side; and a rotation of different cushions through the day shifts the seat's pressure points before any one of them starts to ache. Storage runs to a fore-peak, a forward stateroom, port and starboard floor lockers, a stern locker, and side shelves — a detailed account of exactly where everything on this boat lives: emergency clothing, a wellington-boot change, and sleeping gear forward; a week's food and water, tools, a spare anchor with chain and rope in the floor lockers; wet-weather gear, spare gas, and nibbles to hand nearby; a cooker, a second anchor, mooring lines, and fenders aft; and binoculars, papers, a camera, a radio, and a torch on the side shelves. None of it runs full to capacity, and none of it costs anything in trim, since any gear's weight simply displaces an equal weight of lead ballast.[308][308b]

A flip-up backrest at bulkhead 4, cut into the seat support forward of it, lets the seat fold up into a raked rest — a fix that has stayed satisfying years on. Sitting bolt upright, headroom comes to about 35.5 in; a couple of armrests on the backrest's base and an inflatable pool chair have both been considered, neither built.[309] The everyday seating habit is to lounge into the leeward corner, standing and leaning on the cabin top on a long passage to relieve the ache, with a selection of cushions swapped around roughly every hour; asked again some years later, the same answer came back in the form of a beanbag-or-footwell suggestion for anyone wanting to sit with the hatch closed.[310]

Cold has never been the problem it might look like from outside. Two inches of foam, fitted as designed along the sides and under the deck, does triple duty as flotation, sound-deadening, and insulation; a heater has never once felt necessary, even through snow and a night that reached about -2°C comfortably slept through in a bag, with body heat and a bedtime coffee doing the rest of the work — the coffee, in particular, warming the cabin enough to dress in before the heat dissipates. Ventilation still supplies a single lit burner even with the hatch shut, and the only real caution is not to slice the stove's chimney off when opening the hatch.[311] Airflow through the vent box is controlled simply, by stuffing a towel into it; window condensation, on the nights it does appear, only starts to drip toward morning and clears up entirely by sleeping head-to-bow instead of head-to-stern.[348] A permanent line runs each side from bow to transom, doing three separate jobs: an instant bow rope for a pontoon, a line to clip the anchor rope to so anchoring never means leaving the cabin, and a harness clip-on point for the rare occasion of going forward at sea — not once needed for that third purpose in twenty years of sailing. Going forward at all is avoided whenever the sail is up, since the boom would do its best to throw a sailor overboard; the mast itself is the main handhold if the foredeck is ever reached.[312]

A watertight tub kept in the fore-peak all season holds a complete change of emergency clothing, a towel, and a space blanket; the cabin floor is lined in rubber matting sold for garage and workshop use; and a bungee-cord fence replaced the forward storage area's original partition.[313] The transom ventilation port stays covered except when loading or unloading spars through it; a bottom-hinged alternative cover was floated but never built, and it remains an open question whether Matt Layden — whose own transom port opens from inside — sails with his own cover off.[314] The tiller-port baffle is a loose rubber disc that presses shut under a wave slap but otherwise lets air pass freely through — "a bit like the mushroom valve in your inflatable air tubes, but a much looser fit."[315]

Electric propulsion settled, after some trial and error, on a 28 lb-thrust Minn Kota chosen over the 54 lb thrust more often recommended for a boat this heavy; the same 80Ah AGM battery that partly substitutes for ballast (above) also runs the echosounder, GPS, VHF, and phone charging, still showing about 80% charge after a three-week cruise. The motor itself is used rarely — the yuloh does the job at every launch site but one narrow river.[316] It mounts to the boat's side rather than the transom, on brass hinges with a removable pin and a forward strut fitted after an earlier bracket distorted at full throttle; it cannot stay mounted while under sail.[317] Photographs of the bracket, published for other builders' reference, prompted a change-of-address notice when the site later moved.[318]

## How she sails

A quantified tacking trial came from a single hard beat up Poole Harbour in a full Force 5: a GPS track analysed afterward, over 19 tacks sailed reefed to about half, gave an average tacking angle of 130 degrees, ranging from a best of 98 degrees in flatter water to a worst of 150 degrees in a choppier stretch, with heel holding steady at 20 degrees throughout. Ducking into the cabin just before putting the helm over reliably completed a tack that would otherwise stall head to wind; sculling up a narrow river in the same wind with no sail up could still leave her briefly unable to make any headway at all.[319]

An early knockdown, on one of the boat's first outings, came when the topping lift fouled the sail with the hatch not quite shut: a wave laid her over and water got in, but re-spacing the boom tang and raising the topping lift's join afterward ended the problem for good — and the episode left more confidence in the boat than it shook.[320] Sculling turns out to be genuinely low-effort even for a self-described non-athlete: a 3-mile session averaged just under 2 knots, and the longest continuous stint ran to 4 hours with room to keep going.[321] Riding through breaking waves bow-on, keeping the bow square to them, brings no water at all over the deck, "considerably higher than the sheer" — letting the bow fall off is what actually causes trouble.[322]

A stability caution drawn from theory alone met a direct answer from experience: a photograph exists of a deliberate attempt to capsize the boat by hand, which failed; Matt Layden's own two-up sailing and Hugo Vihlen's 105-day crossing in the 5 ft 4 in "Father's Day" were offered as further counter-evidence against the same worry.[323] A rear-deck-entry sheet arrangement was considered and set aside early on, along with a claw-fitting idea, after an unplanned gybe wrapped the working sheet round a cleat and, on a separate occasion, the yuloh's own pivot pin — freed by hand each time without real trouble.[324] The mainsheet lands at the helmsman's own back in ordinary sailing, which turns out not to be awkward in practice: a forward thrust of the hand takes up the slack, which then simply falls into a bin rather than needing to be coiled.[325]

Across six years of sailing, only about two litres of real water has ever come aboard beyond ordinary spray, and that from a single incident when the masthead dipped into the water.[326] The circumstances and lessons of that incident are documented in full on the "Trip 4" page and video on the boat's own website:[327] the lessons were the ordinary ones — reef early, watch the weather — but the experience itself left more confidence that the boat will look after a sailor even when he does something silly.[327b] An occasional tacking quirk showed up soon after launch, sticking head to wind rather than completing the turn; trim mostly resolved it, though how much was the boat and how much simply developing technique is hard to separate. Every other point of sail has proved benign by comparison, and gybing consistently so — always giving another way round rather than catching anyone out.[328] On a long passage, standing up and leaning on the hatch coaming, or hanging over the side and watching the water go by, works as a real alternative to hours of sitting.[329]

## Trailering

The trailer was built as close as practically possible to the boat's own footprint, with the drawbar sticking out at the front and the wheels at the sides rather than underneath — narrower in principle, at the cost of raising the boat higher to get the wheels under it.[330] At the last weighing, the boat came to 526 kg on the trailer with stores aboard; the trailer itself, built purposely sturdy enough that its own wheels never need to enter the water, weighs just over 400 kg, putting the all-up total comfortably past 900 kg. Keeping under the UK's 750 kg unbraked limit might have been legal, but manhandling the rig on a sloped drive — as this one sits on — argued for fitting brakes regardless of the legal threshold.[331]

No commercial trailer on the market met the brief, so the trailer here was designed from scratch: it allows a genuinely single-handed launch and recovery with little physical effort, at the cost of needing a proper slipway rather than any random beach, and rides docile on the road behind an ordinary 2-litre diesel car except on the steepest slipways. The core design requirement was keeping the trailer's own wheels dry, which means the trailer itself acts as the launching ramp, putting the boat into water deep enough to float — and puts a fair amount of upward force on the towball in the process.[332] After a later suspension swap left the old bearing-buddy adaptors no longer fitting the new hubs, the fix was to saw the ends off the new hubs' bearing caps and braze the buddies straight into them instead.[333]

Launching deliberately onto a slipway left dry by the tide is a favourite habit, not a mistake — it usually means having the whole slipway to himself, with no rush: drop the boat off the trailer, park the car, board, make coffee, read a book, and wait for the water to return.[334] Four transverse rollers — two supporting the boat in transit, two further aft for winching — mean the wheels never need to enter the water at all, on a slipway or a dried-out beach alike; after thirteen years the brakes still hadn't got wet.[335] The design was later restated as a horizontal-and-vertical roller layout, simplified from the original, and had by then gone fifteen years without a significant problem; the standing advice drawn from it is to size a trailer winch several sizes beyond what seems necessary — this one rated at 950 kg — and to choose a braked winch over the standard ratchet type, since a braked winch and trailer together make manhandling on a slope much easier.[336]

The boat can also come off the trailer entirely, without a ramp: tipped until the rudder touches the ground, then the trailer driven forward out from underneath her, or — recovering — a couple of boards laid down on soft or rough ground for the trailer's own rollers to run on as it backs in under the bow.[337] Hauling a boat up a beach is easier done stern-first, since the rudder becomes a problem going forward instead; a well-matched, purpose-built trailer of this kind "pays for itself" in a very direct way — launching and recovering single-handed now takes about fifteen minutes, meaning a short evening sail is worth going out for at all, and so the boat gets sailed more often as a direct result.[338] Inflatable rollers, tried separately, can lift a whole hull clear of the ground rather than merely sliding it; bearing buddies of the ordinary kind were never fitted to this trailer in the first place, since its wheels never go into the water to need them.[339]
