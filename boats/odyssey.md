# Odyssey

**"Odyssey"** is Alexander Fadeev's [Paradox](/paradox.md), launched in May 2011 with a distinctive teak deck and home-based at Barnegat Bay, New Jersey.[1]

## Launch and a first coastal passage

Soon after launching, "Odyssey" sailed north from Barnegat Bay to Staten Island, New York, where a weekend at a fellow Watertribe member's mooring field was spent "fixing and trying to break" the boat before she continued exploring the Long Island coast; video from the weekend, including a capsize test (below), was posted to YouTube.[1]

## A capsize test, and a stability comparison against "Little Jim"

Hanging a 240-260 lb, drysuit-clad tester from the masthead heeled the loaded hull to about 45 degrees before she began going over quickly enough that the mast was released to avoid shipping water; with her owner aboard in a separate test, she was flipped to 90-100 degrees and could not be forced down any further.[2] Watching the footage, [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) — unable to get his own "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" anywhere near capsizing by the same mast-hanging method — wondered whether "Odyssey"'s owner was simply heavier, or the boat needed more ballast; she was already carrying ballast at the plans' upper design level at launch, with more added since.[3] See [self-righting and capsize recovery — a 2011 comparison](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#a-2011-comparison-a-heavier-hanging-load-needed-to-heel-odyssey-than-little-jim) for how the test compares against the design's documented self-righting figures.

## A tricky inlet, under sail

Continuing up the coast, "Odyssey" navigated a difficult inlet from the open ocean into Great South Bay with the tide running hard against the wind, surfing between breakers the whole way through — handling that left her owner deeply impressed, and growing more so with every mile sailed.[4]

## Windward pointing, and a comparison against other boats sailed

By eye rather than by instrument, she points markedly better on a starboard tack — roughly 60 degrees off the wind — than to port, where about 45-50 degrees is more typical, a tack-to-tack asymmetry left unexplained. Sailed against a personal range running from a Sunfish up to a 36 ft keel yacht, she handles unlike any of them, and took some time to get used to.[12]

## The completed cruise, construction timeline, and cost

The passage continued past Long Island to Point Judith, Rhode Island, completing roughly 250 nautical miles in 8 days: Barnegat Bay to Staten Island, through the Intracoastal Waterway along Long Island to the Peconic estuary and Gardiners Bay, around Montauk Point, and on to Point Judith.[5] Construction began on 3 December 2009 and "Odyssey" launched on 12 May 2011. Raw materials — ACX plywood, dimensional Hem-Fir 2x4 and 2x6 lumber from home centers, epoxy (Polymer Products and Progressive Epoxy brands both tried and liked), fiberglass, acrylic, fasteners, lead, varnish, and paint — came to roughly $2,500; rigging hardware (blocks, lines, stainless rails, hatch, anchors) to roughly $1,500; and electronics and trailer gear (GPS, radio, solar, lights, battery) to a further $1,500. Ballast comprises 350 lb of lead plus 55 liters of water carried in the tanks, bringing the boat to her waterline together with roughly 100 lb of food and gear aboard; the sail is a new pattern ordered from Sailrite. At 6 ft 3 in and 225 lb, her owner found plenty of room aboard, sleeping with 10 days of food and water stowed for the passage.[5]

## Outboard power

Congratulations on the finished cruise arrived from [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md), then nearing his own Paradox "[JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md)"'s launch, along with a question about the outboard fitted to "Odyssey" given its external fuel tank.[6] "Odyssey" runs a Tohatsu 4 hp short-shaft outboard — at 57 lb heavier than ideal, but effective, keeping its propeller submerged even in choppy seas. Its 1.3-liter internal tank runs for over 1.5 hours at roughly 5.5 knots in calm conditions; a 3-gallon external tank fitted for the first cruise proved excessive, with under a gallon used for the whole 250 nm passage, and one or two sealed 1-liter metal fuel cans are planned as a replacement.[7]

## Reception

Video of the finished boat continued to draw admiration after the cruise, with one group member singling out the teak decking as looking "amazing."[8] A further admirer, particularly struck by the electrical panel and the teak decking, added "Odyssey" to the boats he was using as his own example while starting his own Paradox, "[Endeavour](/boats/endeavour.md)," the same month.[9]

## A detailed bill of materials

A fuller, itemized accounting followed the rough cost estimate above, refining the roughly $4,000 combined materials-and-hardware figure to **$3,915** across specific categories: ACX plywood and doorskin ply $400; fir studs (2x4, 2x6, 2x8) $55; teak planks and boards $200; foam insulation panels $100; roughly 7-8 gallons of epoxy $800; fiberglass cloth and tape $350; bronze nails, stainless screws and bolts $250; deck hardware (blocks, cleats, plates, round rail, fittings, rudder pintle and gudgeon) $550; hatch $250; grab rails $175; 350 lb of lead ballast (cast from wheel weights) $150; 1/4 in Lexan windows $185; paint $150; and miscellaneous items (copper tubing, PVC plumbing and water pump, pouring foam, varnish) $300.[10] The plywood used came close to the plans' own call-out: 2.5 sheets (4x8 ft) at 3/4 in, 4 sheets at 1/2 in, 2 sheets at 3/8 in, 1 sheet at 1/4 in, and 2 sheets at 1/8 in.[11]
