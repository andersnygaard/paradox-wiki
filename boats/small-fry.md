# Small Fry

**"Small Fry"** is a Paradox built to [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s design by **Geoff Davis** and sailed on the coast of **Western Australia**, her transom lettered **"SMALL FRY / FREMANTLE"** for her home port.[1][2] She is the archive's only documented Western Australian Paradox — the previously recorded Australian builds are both Queensland boats, [Walden](/boats/walden.md) and [Aussie Paradox](/boats/aussie-paradox.md), on the opposite side of the continent.

Small Fry was a **completed and cruised boat before her builder moved on to a larger project**, a William Atkin *Perigee*; the Paradox is described as the previous boat-building venture of the two.[1] The photographic record of her is dated by a **10 April 2014** capture in Shark Bay, published on a personal site assembled across **2017-2018**.[1][3]

Small Fry has also rounded **Cape Leeuwin** — the exposed southwestern tip of the Australian continent, where the Indian and Southern Oceans meet — a sustained open-water passage well outside the sheltered, ultra-shoal cruising grounds documented below, told in Geoff Davis's own words at the time in [the firsthand account below](#a-firsthand-account-rounding-cape-leeuwin-march-2011).[4]

## Shark Bay, and a design suited to the ground it sits on

Shark Bay drew the strongest verdict in the record: it **"must have been created with Matt's design in mind,"** and is recommended without qualification to **"anyone looking for an exciting wilderness in which to go exploring with an ultra shoal draft sailing boat."**[1] The photograph carrying that judgement shows Small Fry lying to her own reflection on dead-flat water at dusk, **waiting on the morning tide to float her after drying out overnight** when the tide went out — the boat treated not as stranded but as simply parked until the water came back.[1]

That is the practical case for the design's **9 in draft** stated from the cruising end rather than the drawing-board end, and it is echoed at the other extreme of the same coast: the anchorage at **Jurien Bay** is **"open only to very shallow draft boats,"** making it **"so good to cruise in a paradox."**[5] The photograph there shows her afloat in water clear enough to read the sand ripples beneath her, well inshore of where a keelboat could lie.[5]

## A distance and duration recorded for the Perth-to-Shark-Bay passage, and an Abrolhos round trip dated to 2013

A cruising blog post, spotted and relayed to the mailing list in August 2014, put a figure on the Perth-to-Shark-Bay passage for the first time — a distance of roughly 800 km, covered in about five days, or around 160 km (100 miles) a day — with a photograph of the anchorage credited to Geoff Davis.[26] The same post's own stated hull length, 14 ft 6 in, does not match the design's stock 13 ft 10 in (see [hull scantlings](/design/hull-scantlings.md#citations)).

A fellow Western Australian builder, then still working through his own Paradox build (see [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)), confirmed the voyages described above were genuine and dated one of them for the first time: the previous year, Small Fry had made a further round trip to the **Abrolhos Islands and back to Perth** — the same Abrolhos visit already recorded above (see [stiffness at rest, measured by a bird](#stiffness-at-rest-measured-by-a-bird)), now placed in **2013**.[27]

## Drying out as ordinary practice, not incident

Ultra-shoal draft is recorded here as an **anchoring advantage first and foremost** — it "opens up all kinds of opportunities for anchoring" — with the consequence stated dryly: she "has **dried out in some slightly odd places at times**."[2][6] The archive's other drying-out material treats taking the ground as an event to be managed (see [Faith — drying out on a sunken mooring block](/boats/faith.md#drying-out-on-a-sunken-mooring-block-during-the-west-country-cruise) and [beaching stern-first for a comfortable sleeping angle](/components/rudder.md#beaching-stern-first-a-sleeping-comfort-technique-and-why-it-doesnt-damage-the-rudder)); this record treats it as the normal daily rhythm of the cruise, the tide dictating when the boat is a boat.

The stern photograph taken aground on a sand spit shows how little the practice costs the boat: she sits **upright and level on her flat bottom** in a drained tidal channel, the **transom-hung rudder blade still fully lowered** below the level of the hull bottom, with no legs, props, or beaching gear of any kind in use.[2]

## Stiffness at rest, measured by a bird

While cruising the **Pelsaert Group in the Abrolhos Islands**, Small Fry was visited by a **Brahminy Kite**, and the encounter produced an unusually concrete measure of the boat's behaviour at anchor: **the force of the bird taking off was enough to rock her as much as a sailor moving about on deck**.[7] For a boat of roughly 1,400 lb design displacement (see [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)), that is a real datum on how a lightly-loaded Paradox responds to a small impulse at rest, and sits alongside the archive's other anchor-motion observations — see [pounding: rare at anchor, occasional underway](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#pounding-rare-at-anchor-occasional-underway) and [why the seat's position damps the felt motion](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#why-the-seats-position-damps-the-felt-motion).

## The galley bench under the hatch

The **removable seat doubles as a galley bench**, carrying a **small butane-cartridge camp stove** judged **"adequate for simple meals of a hot beverage."**[8] The siting is the point: because the bench sits **directly under the hatch**, **"excess heat and steam does not build up in the cabin"** — the open hatch acting as the galley's flue.[8] See [interior — the removable seat as a galley bench](/components/interior.md) for the same arrangement stated generally, and [cabin heating — window condensation](/operations/cabin-heating.md#window-condensation-causes-and-mitigation) for why keeping cooking moisture out of the cabin matters in a boat this size.

## Trailering on an oversized utility trailer

Small Fry travels on an **oversized utility trailer** that **"works as a boat trailer, and can be used for many other things when not launching the boat"** — a galvanised, mesh-sided box trailer carrying the hull on its bed, with a spare wheel stowed beneath the bow.[9] That dual-purpose choice is the practical answer to how a Fremantle-based boat reaches cruising grounds several hundred kilometres up the coast, and it inverts the archive's usual trailer problem: rather than adapting a boat trailer to a Paradox (see [trailering](/operations/trailering.md)), a general-purpose trailer is bought large enough to do both jobs.

## What the photographs show

The published photographs are the fullest colour record of a finished Paradox in the archive outside the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) material, and several details are legible in them:[1][9][2][5]

- **Finish**: high-gloss **black topsides** over a **white bottom and boot-top** — an unusual choice for a boat kept in the Western Australian sun, and the reverse of the light topsides most builders on record chose.
- **Rig**: the standing [lug sail](/components/rig/index.md) set in the driveway is a **tan/rust-coloured** cloth on wooden spars, with the unstayed, varnished wooden mast carrying only running lines; the sail is shown **rolled on the boom** at rest, the design's [roller furling](/components/rig/furling.md) in its stowed state.
- **Cabin**: large, dark-tinted cabin windows carried well around the forward corners of the cabin. Whether this represents a departure from the stock window arrangement is not established here — no dimensions are on record for either, and the record carries no builder's comment on the choice.
- **Fit-out**: a **solar panel** mounted flat on the cabin top abaft the hatch, and spars stowed along the side decks.

## Ballast, and a heavy-weather account in Geoff Davis's own words

Geoff Davis gave his own cruising ballast load-out directly on the `paradoxbuilders` mailing list: roughly **100 kg of lead** plus a battery of about **30 kg**, an anchor, food and water, enough to put Small Fry down on her lines by the time he is aboard, with no adverse effect noticed on sailing beyond a slower response to gusts and an anchorage made extremely comfortable even where otherwise unremarkable.[10] A later firsthand account from the same mailing list, riding out 2-3 m seas and about 25 kt of wind under reduced sail by lashing the steering line and letting the boat look after herself, is told in full at [steering — locking the helm off](/components/steering.md#locking-the-helm-off).

## Departures from the plans: a hinged hatch and flush-framed windows

Building Small Fry, Geoff Davis made two deliberate departures from the stock plans: the sliding hatch was replaced with a hinged hatch modelled on Sven Yrvind's boats, built so it could still be converted back to a sliding hatch later if wanted, and the windows were set flush in frames rather than left as a simpler stock panel — more work to build than the plans called for, and a small sacrifice of window area, but preferred for its more conventional look, and still leaving full 360-degree visibility.[11] The sail itself carries no battens, despite one photograph appearing to show them; Small Fry was still under sail, not at rest, when that photograph was taken from ashore, which accounts for the misleading impression.[11]

## A full painting account: interior, exterior, bottom, and mast

Small Fry's finish uses different paint systems by area. Her high-gloss black topsides are a two-pack gloss product, International Perfecton — expensive, but yielding two full coats from a single litre; rolled and brushed on, the brush marks level out and disappear shortly afterward, giving a finish judged better than any spray job seen and durable enough to have withstood serious abuse.[12] The same high-gloss two-pack was also used below the waterline, on the bottom, while the interior was finished instead in an easier-to-apply single-pack enamel.[13] Surface preparation, rather than the paint itself, is treated as the step that actually decides the outside finish's quality: the plywood is sanded and filled, then sanded and filled again, repeatedly, before paint goes on.[13] A single-pack, purpose-made non-skid deck paint was tried and found wanting — fine dry, but markedly slippery as soon as it gets wet — with a coarser product recommended instead (see [non-skid deck finish](/construction/non-skid-deck.md) for alternatives that avoid this failure mode).[13] The mast is left bright, clear-finished with an inexpensive varnish rather than painted, and has held up well since the boat is kept under cover between outings.[13] Judged overall, every finish held up to satisfaction except the deck paint, though the two-pack gloss topcoats used outside are conceded not to be a budget-minded choice.[13]

## A 2010 resorcinol question, answered with his own epoxy experience

Asked whether resorcinol might replace epoxy, Geoff Davis's own answer drew on Small Fry's construction, which used epoxy exclusively: easy to work with and reliable for gluing, though less convincing for glassing, where the result has sometimes seemed to peel too easily, and carrying a real drawback in its high price.[14] In a follow-up reply he traced that peeling impression to a specific incident — removing a piece of fiberglass from Small Fry's own bottom, after some earlier breakages, found the sheet came away more readily than expected once started — while still judging epoxy the strongest resin available for glassing, and putting the impression down to his own expectations rather than any fault in the epoxy itself; he also suggested temporary bolts, with the holes filled afterward, as a way to reach a resorcinol joint's demanding clamping-pressure spec, noting that Small Fry's own weekend-only build pace already left natural multi-day gaps between glue sessions.[15] See [fastening and gluing — a resorcinol alternative to epoxy](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#a-resorcinol-alternative-to-epoxy) for the fuller resorcinol-versus-epoxy comparison this question prompted.

## A 2010 testimonial: trusting the design

Answering a newcomer's question about how Paradox's history bears on real-world performance, Geoff Davis gave his own verdict. He had been skeptical about the design's likely performance while he was still building Small Fry; since her completion he has sailed some pretty wild conditions and found her performing very well every time. Years of experience have deepened his appreciation for how much thought Matt Layden put into the design, discovering fresh detail even now, however simple a given feature looks. His advice to anyone unsure: trust Matt as a designer, whose plans carry more detail and instruction than other well-known designers' he has had the chance to examine; stick closely to the design, resist the temptation to deviate, and the result will be good.[16]

## A firsthand account: rounding Cape Leeuwin, March 2011

The rounding took place on **22 February 2011**, sailed out and back from the **Swan River at Perth**: a night passage under sail through the bridges at Fremantle and the harbour past Garden Island, timed to favourable wind and tide, made good conditions as far as **Bunker Bay**, near Cape Naturaliste, where the boat anchored for a night.[17][18] Bad weather held her at Bunker Bay a further night and allowed only slow progress south after that; conditions turned genuinely difficult nearing the cape, ridden out some distance offshore, before she made **Hamlin Island** — about 10 miles north of Cape Leeuwin — the next day. Anchored there, she dragged in the night and went aground on the beach; freed the following morning, she continued south.[18]

The rounding itself took several hours of repeated attempts against a SE wind gusting to about 20 kt and a building sea, broken by a lull that let more sail be carried and better windward progress made — the lull that finally carried her round.[17] Despite Small Fry having sailed in stronger winds and bigger seas before, always in the relative safety of open ocean well offshore, the exposed, reef-strewn water right at the cape — waves breaking and throwing spray with no reef visible, conditions changing rapidly, and an unforgiving lee shore close by — stood as the single most menacing stretch of water she had sailed.[17] Once round, she anchored at **Seal Island** on a rock bottom for two nights, riding out a 20 kt gust in the island's lee on the second and wishing for more chain and a spare anchor, before more settled weather let the passage continue the next day.[17] *(A later, secondhand account of the voyage instead describes Small Fry "continuing for several days without putting in" after the rounding; the contemporaneous firsthand record above has her anchored at Seal Island within a day of rounding the cape, and is treated here as the more reliable account of the two.)*[4]

Reef country along this stretch of coast is safe to explore only in light, offshore conditions with little swell — water a keelboat could never enter, where the worst a flat-bottomed, keelless Paradox risks is a few scratches to her bottom paint.[18] An earlier folkboat, sailed to Albany a couple of times, had already given a rough idea what conditions to expect further south; whatever limit was found at Cape Leeuwin was judged more his own than the boat's.[20] Even larger boats based at Fremantle rarely, if ever, venture south of Bunbury on this "surf coast without harbours."[19]

Provisioning for the roughly **two-week** round trip used only about a quarter of the food and water carried, suggesting a stock Paradox could readily supply a solo sailor for a month, and perhaps up to two months, with careful planning and packing.[18]

## Future plans, and heavy-weather watertightness in Geoff Davis's own words

A visit to **Esperance**, further east along the same coast, showed an ocean that "did look rather enticing," though Geoff Davis doubts he would sail Small Fry that far — more likely he would trailer her there instead; the **Abrolhos Islands** are a further destination he has his eye on, tempered by wariness of the downwind leg out, since there is no guarantee of being able to get back against the wind, and any such trip is not planned for the near future.[21]

Answering questions about the boat's watertightness in the conditions described above, Geoff Davis gave a full account of where water does and doesn't get in. No water comes through the hatch at all — a deliberate departure from the plans, a **hinged hatch** rather than a sliding one, dogged down against a layer of high-density foam gasket tape that seals very effectively. Water does find its way in through the **rudder hole in the transom**, but only in genuinely rough conditions running with the occasional wave breaking over the stern: on one occasion in 2008, a big wave broke over Small Fry from astern with enough force to use the baffle "as a launch ramp," and about 10 litres were bailed out after a dawn-to-dusk sail in those conditions — still no more than a discomfort, never a dangerous amount, and going to windward, even in very rough seas, no water gets aboard at all. The vent is adequate to windward; downwind it can get a little steamy, since steering with the hatch closed takes more effort. He would not suggest altering the design over it — "Matt has things just about right."[22] Other builders have since fitted DIY fixes directly to the tiller/rudder-hole opening for exactly this kind of water ingress; see [vent baffle — two 2011 DIY tiller-port baffle designs](/components/vent-baffle.md#two-2011-diy-tiller-port-baffle-designs).

## The biggest seas encountered: a broken reefing gear and a double-digit-knot reach

The roughest conditions Small Fry has been sailed in on record were around **4 to 5 metres** of sea. Running under bare poles after the reefing gear broke through a series of accidental gybes — the result of concentrating on a phone call rather than the steering — the boat still made **3 to 4 knots** under no sail at all. Once far enough downwind, raising full sail again for a reach toward shelter, easing the sail well to spill wind, saw the GPS repeatedly register **double-digit boat speeds in knots**; control was only partial, in "moments of semi control," enough to reach a sheltered bay. Small Fry survived those conditions "quite happily enough," with some acknowledged luck alongside the boat's own capability, and it was not an experience to be deliberately repeated. Whether the boat points better on the port tack was also wondered aloud from this same passage, a question taken up more fully at [sailing characteristics — a one-tack-fluttering puzzle, and a contrary experience](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-one-tack-fluttering-puzzle-and-a-contrary-experience).[23]

## A six-day passage without anchoring, and self-steering on any point of sail

The longest stretch Small Fry has sailed without dropping anchor is **six days**. That endurance rests on a self-steering technique worked out through experimentation: with a length of elastic, "occy strap"-type cord added to the tiller line, the boat can be made to steer herself on almost any point of sail — except running downwind, where she holds a course unattended only if kept to **4 knots or less**.[28]

## Antifouling during a month at anchor: an open question

A question about how the hull is kept clear of marine growth during a month spent largely at anchor without a haul-out — whether simply scrubbing the sides regularly, and letting the flat bottom's periodic drying-out on sand abrade off any growth on its own, would be enough, asked specifically as a way of avoiding copper-based antifouling — remains unanswered on record.[29]

## Staying sane alone: routine, mood, and staying in touch

A full month aboard, largely without setting foot ashore beyond the odd walk on a drying mudflat, produced a firsthand account of the discipline solo cruising demands. The routine is a running self-check — tired, so sleep; hungry, so eat; chilly once the sun goes down, so put more clothes on — obvious in principle but requiring conscious, repeated review, since the most trivial shore-side task becomes harder at sea. Mood is treated the same way: ordinary ups and downs, happiness, boredom, and the odd bout of wondering what one is doing alone in a small boat in the middle of the ocean, are expected and actively managed rather than dwelled on — upbeat music, singing (with no one aboard to judge the result), or simply cataloguing the good things of the moment (the sparkle of the water, a storm petrel or albatross overhead, the boat itself, self-built) reliably breaks a low patch and restores the pleasure of sailing.[28]

The passage was not spent in true isolation. Other boaters investigated and checked in when Small Fry lay at anchor in Shark Bay; well out to sea, a large fishing boat once altered course to come alongside, her skipper's first guess being that she was an Indonesian fishing vessel strayed from home waters. VHF and broadcast radio supplied a human voice at need, and a mobile phone kept contact with the world within range of a signal — a double-edged convenience: the ability to communicate breeds an obligation to do so, and worry about others worrying in turn, a dynamic compared to Bernard Moitessier's own account of taking on risk to relay a message home for exactly that reason. In an age of pervasive connectivity, the observation drawn from the passage is that being truly alone has become surprisingly difficult to achieve.[28]

## Completion time compared with another owner's account, May 2011

Small Fry's own build took about the same schedule as another owner's account of building "Little Jim" — roughly **eighteen months** of active work, at an hour or so most evenings after work plus some time most weekends — though the whole project, from start to finish, spanned closer to **three years**, stretched out by travel and moving house several times along the way.[24]

## A full build-cost account, and a buy-vs-build comparison (2013)

Every receipt from Small Fry's construction was kept, filed away and forgotten for some years until a clear-out turned the pile up and it was finally added: a grand total of about **$12,000 AUD**, including roughly **$2,000** for the trailer. Not every Paradox would cost as much — the build used top-shelf materials throughout, West System epoxy and marine plywood, with international-brand epoxy primers, undercoats, and top coats — but even a cheaper build was judged likely to cost more than expected. Once shipping a well-built Paradox halfway around the world is weighed against building from scratch, buying may still work out the better option financially. Even so, the building process itself was judged worth it regardless of the sums — a source of real satisfaction on every cruise since, knowing the boat carrying him safely through all kinds of weather and seas was built with his own hands.[25]

## An unconfirmed 2009 report: a rolling off Fremantle, and a presumed news source

A newcomer to the mailing list relayed, secondhand and six years after the fact, an account of a Paradox — matching "Small Fry" by name and Fremantle home port — reported rolled and dismantled off Fremantle in 2009 in near-hurricane-force winds, citing a news article on the rescue in which Geoff Davis credited the design with keeping him from being washed overboard, and a separate reference to Davis five years later still sailing her, having taken her 400 miles up the coast and around Cape Leeuwin.[30] No such incident appears anywhere else in the archive — the earliest firsthand account on record here dates from April 2010, and the roughest conditions Geoff Davis describes in his own words (see [the biggest seas encountered](#the-biggest-seas-encountered-a-broken-reefing-gear-and-a-double-digit-knot-reach) above) are a broken reefing gear and a bare-poles run, not a capsize or dismantling.

The same exchange, two days later, turned up what is presumed to be the underlying source: a Western Australian news item headlined "Sailor rescued after boat capsizes in heavy seas."[31] Its own text goes unquoted in the record and does not by itself establish whether "capsized" meant the boat stayed inverted or was briefly knocked down and righted; read alongside the word "rescued," the boat is presumed to have remained afloat and been salvaged after her skipper was lifted off by helicopter, though this reading is inference rather than something the item states outright.[32] A separate secondhand recollection placed Small Fry meeting a Benford Badger sailboat in a remote area further south along the same coast, believed by its source to be the same boat, though it adds no further detail of the incident itself.[33] No reply from Geoff Davis himself appears anywhere in the archive to confirm or deny any of this; the report is no longer wholly untraceable, but it remains unconfirmed at first hand — recorded here still as an unconfirmed, secondhand account, to be rewritten around a firsthand source if one surfaces.

A further account, from a fellow Western Australian builder who has known Geoff Davis personally for some years (see [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)), adds detail while remaining one step short of Davis's own words: Davis told him about the incident directly but left the telling of the full story to Davis himself; what was understood from that conversation is that Davis recovered the boat, repaired a hole in the side of the hull, and built a new mast, before going on to the further long voyages already recorded above — the round trip to the Abrolhos Islands in 2013 and the passage to Shark Bay in 2014.[34] Still no account in Geoff Davis's own words appears anywhere in the archive.

Asked directly what caused the capsize, Geoff Davis's own belief — relayed the same day by the same correspondent — was that the **rudder had given way first**, prompting advice in turn to build any Paradox's rudder strong, given how much force passes through it.[35]
