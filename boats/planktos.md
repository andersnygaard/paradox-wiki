# Planktos (Roselt Croeser)

**Planktos** is [Roselt Croeser](/people/roselt-croeser.md)'s Paradox, built in **Western Australia** between **early 2013 and the end of 2016** and cruised out of Perth, most notably on a shakedown passage north to **Shark Bay**.[1][2] She is the archive's only documented Paradox **converted from the stock lug to a junk rig and sailed** — one of only two junk-rigged Paradoxes built and sailed on record, the other being [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s [THU](/boats/thu.md), which carries a purpose-designed junk rig rather than a conversion (see [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md)) — and one of the few builds on record from outside the mailing-list era, worked up from the plans alone.

She is also the fullest single statement in the archive of what a **deliberately frugal** Paradox costs and what it is like to live with: the build ran to roughly **$2500** in materials, and the boat carries **no electrical system at all**.[3]

## The build: four years, four locations

Four months before ground broke, Croeser told the Paradox mailing list — replying to a fellow owner's question about junk-rigging a Paradox — that he had already converted an eighteen-foot fibreglass boat to junk rig and planned to give his own, still-unbuilt Paradox the same treatment; see [people/roselt-croeser.md](/people/roselt-croeser.md#the-junk-rig-conversion) for the message in full.[4] The build itself took **from early 2013 to the end of 2016**, run alongside owner-building a house — the two projects competing for the same time — and moved through **four different locations** over its span.[2] The result was deliberately a **working boat finish**: "very strong, very simple," built using what was already to hand wherever possible.[2] The **mast was repurposed from a sunken boat** and the **sail came from an SS18**, so the two most expensive items in a small boat's rig cost nothing.[2][3] Her bottom panel is **12 mm plywood** rather than a heavier option, chosen because 18 mm "was too heavy to carry," and judged, once glassed to the plans, "bullet proof."[5]

The finished assessment of the design as a building proposition was unreserved: Paradox "must be the easiest type of boat to build," on the strength of the **quality of the plans** and of the **builders' group** behind them.[3]

## Departures from the plans: the cabin structure, and flotation without built-in tanks

Planktos's cabin was not built strictly to plan. Answering a fellow correspondent's question about whether the frame was built to the plans and then covered in plywood, Croeser explained that he framed the edges around the cockpit independently and then built the whole cabin structure by stitch-and-glue methods. The central laminated beam came out about 75 mm wide, wider than the plan calls for, with a second, similar beam laminated onto its outer face; the front of the cabin roof is two layers of 4 mm plywood laminated together with a heavy application of epoxy, and every edge and corner is reinforced with extra timber. The sliding hatch at the back sits inside structure built extra strong for it — 9 mm plywood backed by 19 mm pine, epoxied on the inside. The plan's central knees were left out entirely. None of this shows up as extra weight; the only give anywhere in the structure is a slight flex in the front of the cabin roof under a point load, which still feels solid underfoot.[6]

Flotation and water storage depart from the plans in the same direction. Rather than following the plans' flotation-foam installation strictly, Planktos carries a number of sealed containers secured in place instead — a choice shaped by an earlier dinghy, owned before the build, whose flotation tank held polystyrene foam beads that were judged to have helped along a serious rot the tank later developed. The sealed containers had not been put to a real test as of the 2019 Force 6 rescue (see [below](#a-force-6-rescue-2019)). Built-in water tanks were left out for the same rot concern; drinking water is instead carried in ordinary 2-litre soft drink bottles, which stow conveniently in various spaces about the boat.[7]

## Launch (November 2016)

Planktos was launched in **Western Australia in November 2016**, reported to the mailing list the same day: winds of **15 knots with stronger gusts**.[8] At launch her ballast ran to about **90 kg of lead plus roughly 30 kg of chain** — "definitely light on," by her builder's own contemporary assessment, with another **100 kg** reckoned still needed — and she carried **no windows yet**, which gave a few nervous moments when gusts laid her over to the gunwales.[8] For a first launch the verdict was still a pleased one: she **sailed well, pointed well, and survived the gusts**, junk-rigged from the outset with **four panels**, the sail itself modified from a previous junk-rigged boat.[8] The **lazyjacks arrangement** was flagged as needing rework — probably separate lines for the sail's front and back — and remained the main outstanding issue a little later that same day, once the first photographs had gone up.[8][9] Signing off the announcement, the builder noted it had been only **six years since the plans were ordered**.[8]

This contemporary record — filed in real time, more than four years before the ABBA newsletter account above — corroborates the retrospective's build-end date and adds detail (the exact wind conditions, the ballast still being tuned, the windows not yet fitted) that the later write-up does not carry.

## Costs

The **overall build cost was approximately $2500**, "mostly wood, epoxy, glass and paint," with the mast and sail repurposed rather than bought.[3] The **trailer cost more per dollar of the project than most of the boat**: about **$2300**, plus roughly **$200** of modifications.[3] Itemized, the sail runs to about **$1000**, paint to about **$600**, epoxy to a similar amount and plywood is the least expensive item of the whole build, though on Planktos herself, whose mast and sail were both repurposed, the sail component cost nothing.[10] Set against the build figures recorded elsewhere in the archive, this is a low-end result, achieved by salvaging the rig and accepting a working finish.

## The junk rig

Planktos was built to the stock **lug rig with roller furling** and then **changed to a junk rig**.[1] The verdict after cruising her is favourable and specific: the junk rig "works very well" — **easy and very quick to reef and unreef**, with **very good sail twist control**, and **cheap, light-weight fittings**.[11]

That twist verdict has independent corroboration predating the 2021 newsletter by five years: replying on the mailing list in April 2016, while still building, Croeser gave the same underlying reason in his own words — some lug sails twist so much that only part of the sail actually works, a fault his own earlier conversion of an unrelated eighteen-foot keelboat had already convinced him a junk rig cures. He signed off that message "not finished after 6 years."[12]

This is one of only two places the archive's long theoretical argument about junk-rigging a Paradox meets a real built boat, and the only one where the stock lug was **converted**. The objections raised in that early debate — spar weight aloft, the extra line to manage, sheeting a sail that already overhangs the transom, and the risk of moving the centre of effort forward of the hull's centre of lateral resistance — went unaddressed point by point at the time, before Planktos existed to test them; how the sheeting and the mast position were actually resolved is recorded below.[1][11] For the same questions worked through on a purpose-designed junk rig rather than a conversion, see [THU](/boats/thu.md).

Restating the conversion's rationale directly in a July 2017 exchange, Croeser named two firsthand frustrations sailing a friend's stock-lug Paradox as his real motivation — the sail was hard to raise, and the friend had himself been caught out unable to reef fast enough — problems the junk rig, in his experience, simply removes; he counted himself already "a junk rig fan" beforehand. Matt Layden's own roller-reefing system he judged "ingenious" but never attempted to build, choosing lazyjacks plus extra luff-haul-parrel line instead as a solution he could actually implement himself. The one drawback he conceded honestly: a fully lowered junk sail bundle is bulkier than a rolled-up lug sail and does not stow as neatly to the side of the cabin, a real concern in extreme conditions.[13] A further technique refined through his own early experiments: too much balance in the sail — the portion forward of the mast — acts as a brake, so the **luff parrel** is wound from the yard's forward end around the mast, then through a block at the front of each batten and on down, and kept pulled tight, especially to windward, to hold the balance portion in check; the halyard and the luff parrel are the only two lines led back into the cabin at the mast end.[14]

**Three and a half years** after fitting it, the verdict held unchanged: a very sensible rig that works very well on a Paradox.[15]

## Systems: none

Planktos carries **no electrical system**.[3] Navigation and power are handled entirely by portable equipment: a **hand-held GPS**, **Navionics on a mobile phone**, **power-bank batteries**, and a **10 watt solar panel** to keep them charged.[3] For a boat sailed to Shark Bay and back, this is the archive's most minimal outfitting on record.

## How she sails

The flat bottom and shallow draft are judged "excellent for carefree sailing and anchoring": lying **overnight in knee-deep water**, stepping off into **ankle-deep water**, **drying out level**, no anxiety about running aground, and an easy shove off the beach.[3] She is **easy to launch and retrieve**, and **very forgiving** under way, with an easy motion.[3] The flat bottom also means **very little hobby-horsing** and an easy motion at anchor.[11]

Sailing from inside the cabin is recorded with a dry qualification — there is "no frying in the cockpit on hot days, instead you bake" — a Western Australian gloss on the enclosed-cockpit trade-off.[11] Going to windward in rough water is "challenging," as with all small boats.[11] And she is "definitely a 1 person boat except for very short outings."[11]

The stated ceiling is **around 20 knots of wind**; "beyond that it is survival."[3]

A July 2017 mailing-list account, still mid-cruise, adds a quantified windward comparison against two other boats Croeser had sailed in the same bay under the same conditions: a **Vertue** owned for seven years and an **eighteen-foot fibreglass fin-keeler**, both tacking through **110 degrees** under Bermudan rig, with the fin-keeler tacking through the same **110 degrees** once he had converted it to junk rig — a figure Planktos herself matches, tacking through **110 degrees** as a matter of course with no trouble in light air.[16] The junk rig proved just as easy to sail as the lug it replaced: an early attempt at one continuous sheet, rigged per *Practical Junk Rig*, gave too much friction for a quick release; four separate sheets (one to the boom, one to each batten) gave good control but needed too many single-purchase ropes; the settled arrangement is **two sheets** — one for the boom and first batten, another for the second and third battens — giving full control of sail shape.[16] Performance improved further with **more ballast, especially further forward**; he moves his own weight forward too and steers **from the front of the cabin** for better visibility, and uses the boat's considerable weight to pinch through a crowded mooring field on the way to the launch ramp.[16]

A wintery shakedown a few weeks later put the junk rig through rougher handling than the July account above: an onshore wind made for a difficult launch, needing a push off the beach since a small oar proved useless, and building dark clouds brought waves up to half a metre in around 15 knots of wind. Tacking through moored boats into increasingly exposed water, one tack stuck fast in irons; getting under way again with a jibe instead proved rough, knocking the boat a long way over. Tacking more carefully after that, releasing the sail halfway through each tack and moving weight well forward got her through cleanly on most attempts. Running off downwind, she took off and surfed the small waves at up to 5 knots — fast enough that holding the sheets by hand and steering by feel on the tiller, rather than working the sheets as rope led to a cleat, proved the better technique. The photographs taken that day, of necessity rough since there was no time to look away from the boat for long, show the junk-rig sheeting arrangement: two sheets, cleated around a custom-made piece of wood, and the yard's own parrel and luff-haul parrel. She continues to be sailed with very little balance — not much sail extending forward of the mast.[17]

A further report that October, still confined to bay sailing close to home while finishing odd jobs left over from the build, gave an early, favourable verdict on the junk rig: more lines to handle than a lug sail, but never any trouble furling — simply letting the halyard go. Sailing in 10-15 knots, she covered 14 nautical miles in 4.5 hours; pointing lower than the jib-and-main crowd, she nonetheless kept pace with a McGregor 26 to windward even as it pointed higher, helped by a shallow draft that let her carry on into water right up to where the sea grass touched bottom, "no one else goes there." Other sailors, watching, always assumed she was having more fun than they were — and she agreed.[18]

## A Force 6 rescue (2019)

In early May 2019, Croeser uploaded a written account of being towed in from about 12 nautical miles offshore in Force 6 conditions — the account itself is not preserved in the archive, only the covering message and the general lesson he drew from it about the design's true limits. Full exchange, including a corroborating account of a fellow owner's own tow, at [design/blue-water-suitability.md — a 2019 test case](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-2019-test-case-a-force-6-rescue-and-skipper-endurance-as-the-real-limit).[19]

Answering a further correspondent's question of whether a drogue would have helped, Croeser judged it would only have bought a couple of hours before he was very close to a lee shore fully exposed to the open ocean regardless. He rated the chine runners "as good as it gets on a small boat," judging the real difficulty to be sailing into a substantial sea with a small boat at all, not any shortcoming in the design — a verdict weighed against his own prior ownership of a 25 ft Vertue (four tonnes), which he judged would have coped better with the conditions but had still been hard work in her own right. Heaving-to through the worst of it felt comfortable and safe; his real concern was the rudder, which took loud, audible strain as the bigger waves pushed the boat backward — the standing lesson he draws from it is that a Paradox's rudder simply has to be built as strong as it can be made.[20]

Asked further whether an outboard would have changed the outcome had it stayed running through the ordeal — which lasted something like eight hours in rough conditions — Croeser judged it would have been a major advantage: easier to turn back to safety, to motor-sail for quicker headway, and to get further out to sea for room to manoeuvre.[21] He weighed that answer against his own reasons for staying engine-free: fitting an outboard is a big decision, since it commits a boat to carrying fuel and creates a snag risk with the mainsheet, and for him Paradox is specifically about a simple, uncluttered boat with no electrical system at all, tolerant of a bit of water coming aboard — a philosophy he remains happy with, even when it costs him a long final tack home to windward through a crowded mooring field, taken as practice in patience and sailing skill rather than a shortcoming in the design.[22] Thanked for the encouragement, Croeser summed up his own outlook in one line: he does not see himself as brave, only doing what he wants right now, and has often had to laugh at his own impatience with a lack of wind — congratulating himself on sailing well only to muff the next tack and run into a moored boat. Sailing a Paradox, in his words, "is a very good way to be kept honest and humble."[23]

## The group record: the junk rig proven, speeds, and the sale

Croeser (posting as "Waldo Cruiser") added a fuller first-hand account of Planktos on the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md). On the junk rig he is specific and favourable: the sail was a **hand-me-down adapted from another project** — so its bottom panel never set perfectly, though "the back 80% of it worked fine" — and the **mast was left in its original Paradox position** rather than moved.[24] In Western Australia's wind he was "often reefed down to three of the four panels," and rated the rig, on balance, **less twist-prone than the lug, more reliable and quicker to reef, and more efficient** thanks to better control of sail shape; sheeting to the end of each batten gives "excellent control."[24][25] Planktos was featured as the **Junk Rig Association's "boat of the month"** in December 2023.[25]

His performance figures fill out the design's real numbers: sustained **5.5 knots** on a broad reach in about 15 knots of wind, a general cruising average of **2.5 knots**, an **80-nautical-mile overnight run averaging 3.3 knots**, and the ability to hold 2–2.5 knots even in light air — "as Yrvind says, it's all relative."[26] Planktos is, in Croeser's own account, "a great light wind boat" — very little wind is needed to get her to 2 knots — but she can just as suddenly lose that momentum regardless of helm input, a stall traced to over-sheeting a flat-cut junk sail in a light breeze; a fuller-cut replacement sail remains under consideration, while keeping the junk rig.[27] For steering he simply **ties the tiller rope in place** rather than fitting any self-steering gear — a preference reached only after building, and then setting aside, a homemade wind vane for the boat three years earlier (see [self-steering — a 2017 build from the Wharram sketch, tried and set aside](/operations/self-steering.md#a-2017-build-from-the-wharram-sketch-tried-and-set-aside)) — keeping the boat "good at being simple," and sculls in calms with a **6-foot oar paddled over the side, SUP-style**, rather than a yuloh; he steers from **inside the cabin**, wedged into the leeward corner on a beanbag cushion with a clear view through the windows, the boat sailing better heeled.[28][29] He fitted a **removable panel at the back of the doghouse** to cut the claustrophobia of the enclosed cockpit.[28] Planktos was offered for sale in Western Australia and **sold in July 2023 to [Kevin Williamson](/people/kevin-williamson.md)**, who kept her junk rig, fitted a **folding mast** to ease unstepping it for bridges, then **extended the mast** for more sail area — Croeser measured the enlarged sail at about **8.3 m²** — and renamed her **"Skye."** She remains a junk-rigged Paradox in Western Australia under her new name (see [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md)).[30][31]

In May 2024 Williamson cruised her overnight from Guildford, the furthest upstream ramp on the Swan River, down to Fremantle and back, about 19 miles each way, timed to ride the easterlies downstream and the afternoon sea breeze back up. He used the trolling motor to clear a road bridge, a railway bridge and a low power line, then moored for the night between two fallen tree limbs on a 60 m-wide reach of the river, cooking on a metho burner with collapsible cookware and the fold-out table in use for the first time, and lowered the mast twice more before reaching the city centre. Calm air and then persistent westerlies turned the return short of Fremantle, but he judged the time well spent for what it taught him about camping aboard.[32][33]

## Shark Bay, and what was contemplated after

The **shakedown cruise was to Shark Bay**, roughly 800 km north of Perth up the Western Australian coast, and the photographs record her under junk sail on open water, at anchor off the dune coastline, and beached and dried out on the sand at low tide.[1] A closing list of what might be attempted "if you are really brave" names three longer passages: **Perth to Shark Bay**, the **Abrolhos and back**, and **Cape Leeuwin and back**.[3] These are stated as prospects at the April 2021 presentation, not as voyages made.
