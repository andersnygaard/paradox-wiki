# Cachivache

**"Cachivache"** — Spanish slang for a piece of junk or a knick-knack — is [Alberto Callejon](/people/alberto-callejon.md)'s [Paradox](/paradox.md), under construction from May 2008 at **Rawson, Chubut**, on Argentina's Patagonian coast. Alberto styled her on the list as **"el Paradox mas austral,"** the most southerly Paradox on record.

## Starting construction on the Patagonian coast

Before starting, Alberto weighed whether the design could handle his home coast's strong wind and sea, a stretch of open South Atlantic at 43°21′S, 65°03′W with almost no local sailing tradition beyond rowing and occasional windsurfing; see [blue-water suitability — a high-latitude query from Argentine Patagonia](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-high-latitude-query-from-argentine-patagonia) for the reassurance this drew. Construction began in May 2008, with early progress photos posted to a "Cachivache" folder in the group's photo section.[1] Alongside starting the hull, he raised the recurring question of building the bottom and sides in sheet steel rather than plywood — see [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — a 2008 recurrence, from a builder in Patagonia](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#a-2008-recurrence-from-a-builder-in-patagonia) for the fuller exchange this drew, and Alberto's own non-technical contributions (an offered instant-messenger contact for Spanish-speaking builders, and a search for Don Elliott's out-of-print construction manual) on [people/alberto-callejon.md](/people/alberto-callejon.md). Plywood itself was the underlying problem: it had to be hauled some 2,000 km to Rawson at considerable freight cost, leaving only pine and the local beech, lenga, readily available in the region — the practical reasoning behind his interest in steel as an alternative.[2]

## Enlarging the cabin to the hull's edge, considered and answered

While laying out Cachivache's bulkheads, before cutting, Alberto considered enlarging the cabin out to the hull's edge and asked whether doing so would affect any of the boat's other design parameters, echoing an idea raised once before and left untested at the time. A reply the next day answered directly against the idea: the stepped-in side decks stow the lowered sail and the yuloh, a wider cabin top makes it harder to reach the anchor or fend off from forward, it adds windage especially to windward, and the cabin's usable interior space would not even increase unless the internal side bins were removed too — so, on balance, little was seen to recommend it. See [flush cabin sides for Paradox (speculative)](/design/ideas/flush-cabin-sides-idea.md#a-2008-recurrence-answered) for the fuller record of both messages.[3][4]

## A drain-pipe material question, where PVC is hard to find

Asking about the PVC pipe shown on drawing 9 of the plans, Alberto drew two different answers. One read the question as a matter of materials sourcing: the pipe carries waste water, and where PVC itself proves hard to obtain locally, copper pipe — or the lead pipe once standard before PVC became common — would work as a substitute.[5][6] A second, posted two days later on the sister overflow group, answered the routing itself: the pipe passes through the bottom of the hull, carrying away any water running down the mast or through the air duct and draining it straight into the sea.[7]

## Reinforcing the hull for Patagonian seas

Construction advanced further that July: panels were reinforced with angle iron and stainless steel bolts and laminated with fiberglass on both sides — extra strengthening measures taken specifically against the rough conditions of the Patagonian sea (see [blue-water suitability — a high-latitude query from Argentine Patagonia](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-high-latitude-query-from-argentine-patagonia) for the conditions themselves) — with new build photographs due in the group's "CACHIVACHE" folder within a few days.[8] New photographs duly went up in the album later that month, drawing further encouragement from the list, with more images following within days.[9][10][11]

## A sail-geometry question for the newer rig

Framing Cachivache's rig, Alberto asked for the distance between the mast and the forward end of the yard on Paradox's newer sail — almost certainly the [Mk2 sail](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md) current at that date, since the design's plans were not revised again until the 2023 Mk3. The question drew no dimension in immediate reply, only a pointer to the group's own Files section, where a "New Sail Plan" folder already held the drawing itself.[12][13] A day later, a fellow builder measured the drawing directly and supplied the missing figure: about 800 mm from the mast centerline to the sail's forward top corner along the top edge, or 900 mm to the halyard attachment point along the same edge — a dimension not otherwise given on the plan (see [Mk2 sail — locating the sail's forward top corner](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#locating-the-sails-forward-top-corner-and-tuning-balance-through-the-sleeve-gap) for the full figure and the sail-balance-tuning technique it enabled).[14]

By mid-August 2008, a fuller set of building photographs was posted to a personal website rather than the group's own photo folder.[15] The link proved unreachable for at least one overseas list member, whose browser simply timed out trying to reach it; contacted directly, Alberto reported the site working fine from his own end, leaving the access problem unresolved on record.[16] Two further list members confirmed the site loaded without any trouble from their own connections — one had checked it several times with no issue, the other praised the photographs directly — narrowing the access problem to that one report rather than any fault with the site itself.[17][18] Revisiting the question weeks later, the browser-timeout report was updated rather than resolved: the site was eventually reached, though it still usually times out, tentatively put down to the long internet path between the UK and Argentina rather than to any fault with the site itself.[13]

## A polyester-and-epoxy question

Weighing how best to impregnate Cachivache's wood, Alberto asked whether a diluted coat of polyester resin applied first would still let epoxy resin be laminated over it afterward. The answer: not reliably, since polyester resin typically leaves a wax on the surface that must be removed before epoxy will adhere at all, and diluting the polyester does not impregnate the wood in any case — only the solvent penetrates, not the resin. A professional's caution, from prior experience at a marine-resin manufacturer, put it more bluntly still: never put epoxy over polyester; seal the wood with an epoxy sealer first, then laminate with epoxy resin. Full exchange and the fallback advice for a tight budget: [epoxy brands, chemistry, and cure behavior — epoxy over polyester](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#epoxy-over-polyester-wax-impregnation-and-a-professionals-caution).[19][20][21] Alberto settled on laminating with epoxy resin just above the waterline, painting everywhere else.[22] A further reply arrived a day later, after the thread had already effectively closed, offering yet another combination — epoxy resin itself diluted with acetone in place of a polyester pre-coat, sidestepping the adhesion problem altogether.[23]

## Continued progress into the Patagonian winter

By late August 2008, the hull's sides were finished, with the remaining work hoped to be done by the end of the Patagonian winter so assembly could begin.[24] New photographs of the finished sides went up in the "Cachivache" folder the following day.[25] The build photos drew a further, unprompted compliment: another builder credited the site directly with the design of his own beam-laminating jigs, judging the method shown there considerably easier than the one given in the construction manual — see [Wizard Spawn — starting on the beams](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#starting-on-the-beams-jigs-sizing-confusion-and-a-lesson-in-plywood) for the fuller account of that build.[26] A further photo went up the following week, along with a wry aside that the Patagonian cold had returned just as it seemed to be easing, with a hope that shifting weather patterns would not draw the low temperatures out any further.[27]

## A cheap imported tape measure, and a full bulkhead redo

Posting in machine-translated Spanish under a subject line that only makes sense once decoded, Alberto described a construction mishap: bulkhead dimensions carefully marked out with a "Made in China" tape measure turned out not to agree with a second measuring tool once cross-checked against it — a seamstress's own tape among them — revealing the cheap import's centimetre scale ran short of true, reading only "99" where it should have read 100. The mismatch went uncaught until after the bulkheads were already cut, forcing a full redo of the lot.[28] Some of the machine-translated phrasing resists confident decoding — a "hammer of 20 kgs" in the subject line, apparently some idiom for the shock of the discovery, and an aside about "the subway seamstress" (Spanish "metro" serves for both "tape measure" and "subway," a likely source of the translator's confusion) — but the underlying gist survived plainly enough for [Nels](/people/nels-arvidson.md) to restate it back in plain English without correction: a scale error on the imported measuring device had thrown off the bulkhead dimensions.[29] Replying the same day, in equally garbled machine translation, Alberto confirmed the gist: he was able to recover the material and get back on track, though it cost him the lost time, with the mate (the drink) close at hand throughout the ordeal — a further phrase, translated as having "gone over gander" to recover the pieces, resists confident decoding, consistent with the same translation difficulty already flagged above rather than a new fact.[30]

A message the following week settles what the "hammer of 20 kgs" subject line actually meant: after "many hours of meditation," the offending tape measure itself met a hammer, rather than merely another cross-check — a literal detail confirming the earlier garbled phrase referred to smashing the faulty tool, not to a literal 20 kg tool of any kind. With a corrected measure in hand, construction of Cachivache continued, every measurement now checked consistent with the plans; new photographs went up at the same personal-website gallery used before, drawing further encouragement from the list.[31][32]

## Considering an aluminum mast

Weighing a wood-to-aluminum substitution for Cachivache's own mast, Alberto had a **63.5 mm** diameter aluminum tube on hand and asked whether it would serve. The general engineering answer he drew — conditional on the tube's wall thickness, with a specific technique offered for reinforcing its lower end — is set out in full at [components/rig/mast.md — aluminum as a mast material](/components/rig/mast.md#aluminum-as-a-mast-material-engineering-fit-not-just-substitution); his own tube's wall proved to be **3 mm**, and no further reply settling whether that was thick enough is on record.[33][34][35]

Raising the same substitution again independently a few days later — restating the tube's diameter as **63 mm** rather than 63.5 mm — Alberto drew two further opinions, both leaning toward wood. One judged the tube better suited to a boom than a mast, recommending a larger diameter (roughly 73 or 88 mm, with a 5 mm wall) if aluminum were used at all, and said he would use wood himself since it floats in the event of real disaster. The other questioned substituting aluminum for wood at all, seeing no advantage besides ease of construction, judged the tube undersized, and suggested testing it by laying the tube across two sawhorses with a central weight and comparing the deflection against a wood box-section built to the mast's own dimensions — predicting wood would win clearly. Full exchange: [components/rig/mast.md — aluminum as a mast material](/components/rig/mast.md#aluminum-as-a-mast-material-engineering-fit-not-just-substitution).[36][37][38]

## Building the sail: a broadseaming question answered

Turning to the sail, Alberto asked whether the cloth should be cut and sewn perfectly flat or given some bag or curve — Spanish "vela" (sail) coming through machine translation as "the candle." The answer: a Paradox sail is genuinely three-dimensional, its shape built in by **broadseaming** — varying the width of overlap along the seams joining each panel, with more overlap at each end than at the centre — following [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own sewing instructions in the group's "Files" section under "New Sail Plan," whose measurements differ from the sail shown on drawing 1 of the plans. The fuller technique, including a caution about working from a translated copy of the instructions, is at [Sail — broadseaming](/components/rig/sail.md#broadseaming-how-the-sails-three-dimensional-shape-is-sewn-in).[43][44]

Nearing launch, Alberto turned to the ballast question, reporting he had obtained sections of railroad track weighing **20 kg per metre** and asking how best to install it.[67] The answer: start with the track pieces close to bulkhead 3 without fixing them down permanently, since some will likely need repositioning to get the trim right once test-sailing begins — better to have too much ballast aboard than too little.[68]

## Completion, and the finished boat (Facebook record)

The mailing-list record above stops in January 2010 with the launch reported just days away; the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) supplies the completion the list never recorded. Cachivache was **finished and sailing** — Alberto dates the build to about 2011 and credits the work of a **master shipwright, Néstor Siri** — and by 2019 he confirmed her first-hand as **"the southernmost Paradox in the world,"** sailed on the **Río Chubut** and the open **Atlantic** at Rawson, and that he was "really happy with it."[74][75] One departure from the plans, made deliberately for his home waters: the **through-transom rudder slot is closed**, not left open, "the waters of the South Atlantic [being] rough enough to leave it open."[76]

## A fellow Spanish-language builder's congratulations, and a shared joke about the boat names

Congratulating Alberto on Cachivache's success, a fellow Spanish-speaking correspondent — building his own Paradox, to be named **"Melandro"** — noted the two builders had, without coordinating, chosen similarly self-deprecating names: in Asturian dialect "melandro" means something old and worth little, fit only to be discarded, much as "cachivache" means junk or a knick-knack in general Spanish. Both, he judged, do Matt Layden a mild injustice, since Paradox is neither a "cachivache" nor a "melandro" — the names meant ironically, not as any real slight on the design. Cachivache's own launch had already proved the point; whether "Melandro" would live down to her own name was left for her launch to decide.[86]

## First sailing tests

Cachivache's first sea trials followed within days of the launch: weighted with 200 kg and sailed in a 9-knot wind, she showed a rapid response to the rudder and handled well under way, deriving a little to leeward on the beat but staying easy to control and hold on course; she heeled low and behaved well tracing the ebb current on the way back. Still practicing furling the sail, Alberto expected to take her out to open water within a few more days.[87]

## A 2011 postscript: the cabin widened after all

Replying on a fellow builder's own thread months after Cachivache's launch, Alberto shared a photograph of her vent trunk and mentioned, in passing, that he had also modified her cabin to make it wider — see [flush cabin sides for Paradox (speculative) — a 2008 recurrence, answered](/design/ideas/flush-cabin-sides-idea.md#a-2008-recurrence-answered) for his own 2008 question about doing exactly this, and the practical objections it drew (lost side-deck stowage, harder forward access, added windage, no net interior gain) before he apparently went ahead regardless. ## Confirmed built stitch-and-glue (2011)

Replying to a newcomer's question about stitch-and-glue construction, Alberto confirmed Cachivache was built that way: plastic cable ties, of the kind used for electrical installations, served as the "stitches" through putty-filled holes to join the panels, followed by strips of fiberglass laid inside and out. See [chine-log vs. taped-seam construction — a further 2011 exchange](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md#a-further-2011-exchange-whether-stitch-and-glue-changes-paradoxs-sailing-characteristics-and-two-further-builders-methods) for the fuller stitch-and-glue technique discussion this answer joined.[89]

## Vandalism damage (December 2011)

In December 2011, vandals broke Cachivache's windows and stole what reads, from Alberto's own English rendering, as a boat fender — documented in a photograph — prompting sympathetic replies from across the list.[92][93][94][95][96] Thanking one correspondent for "the accompaniment," Alberto judged the vandals fortunate not to have caught his eye — in his own words, "today would form part of the seabed."[97]

## An accidental jibe, survived (December 2011)

Sailing with the wind on the port quarter and a ten-degree heel, steered from the aft deck with his wife resting in the cabin on the weather side, an unexpected wind shift jibed Cachivache without warning; she luffed up quickly and righted herself "like a buoy." Alberto took the moment as proof of just how reliable a sailor the boat is, closing the account happier every day to own her.[98] A further video, posted without accompanying description, followed the same week.[99]

## The first outing to open salt water (January 2012)

On 2 January 2012, taking advantage of a public holiday, Cachivache made her first outing beyond the river onto open salt water. Provisioned and crewed by Alberto, his wife, and a young crew member, she left the dock under a clear sky in a 15-knot northeasterly at about 20°C, riding the outgoing current down the Río Chubut some 12 miles toward the sea — the first two hours covered by drift alone under a following breeze, "hard to describe in words." Nearing a stretch overhung by trees, an unnoticed branch snapped the cable to the VHF antenna before the mast was lowered, as planned, to clear two low bridges further on. The outboard, a 3.3 hp Evinrude, ran dry when a split fuel-line hose between the shutoff valve and the filter let the fuel leak away — found and fixed within minutes with a pocket knife and fresh hose clamps. Nearing the fishing port of Rawson amid the wake of other boats and personal watercraft running recklessly close by, Cachivache moored off the coast for lunch before pressing on, drawing greetings from fishermen as she passed the day's catch being unloaded. Rawson's harbor infrastructure proved unsuited to a boat her size: unable to find a proper mooring, the crew tied up to the shore and waded ashore through knee-deep mud rather than risk a rusted, dangerously exposed pier ladder. The outing ran over seven hours in total — tired, but proud of a boat built through years of hard work; in Alberto's own words, only this voyage finally made "Cachivache" real.[100]

## Official registration, the southernmost authorized Paradox (January 2012)

After passing all of the Argentine maritime authority's inspections and tests, Cachivache received her official registration in January 2012 — becoming, in Alberto's words, the most southern Paradox and the first in the country authorized to navigate — drawing congratulations from the list.[101][102]

## A scrap-fabric staysail, tried on the river (March 2012)

A leftover scrap of sailcloth — from Cachivache's own sail, "candle" again standing in for the Spanish "vela" as in the boat's earlier broadseaming exchange — became a small hand-sewn staysail. Tried out the same day on the Chubut river and carried about a mile by the current, she was hoisted to starboard on a halyard and traced the wind's flow nicely in roughly 10 knots, with the new sail judged a complete success.[103]

Alberto's own question on the list, whether anyone had sailed a Paradox under a [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md) (see [people/alberto-callejon.md](/people/alberto-callejon.md#community)), drew an encouraging answer from a fellow owner who had already converted a different boat to junk rig and planned to fit one to his own, still-unbuilt Paradox — full exchange at [Junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#planktos-the-stock-lug-converted-outcome-not-method) and [people/roselt-croeser.md](/people/roselt-croeser.md#the-junk-rig-conversion).[105] Alberto answered that he had begun sewing a junk sail of his own; no further report of it — whether finished, fitted, or sailed — is on record.[106]

## A 2013 chine-runner reshaping, and photographs shared

Answering a wider list question about whether later Paradox-family designs' shorter, straighter, wider runners actually improved on the original shape, Alberto reported having remade Cachivache's own chine runners shorter and wider, following the shape of the hull with a downward tilt — a change he found held direction much better and turned with ease, with a noticeably more sensitive rudder — and linked two videos of her under way.[107] A request for photographs of the modified runners themselves, since they were hard to make out in the video, drew a reply the next day sending them directly rather than posting to the group's photo folder or the personal-website gallery used throughout the earlier build; the attachment itself is not preserved in the archive scrape.[108][109] A separate correspondent's question that same week, prompted by an archived photograph showing Cachivache fitted with a jib, asked how the rig had worked out.[110] Alberto answered that an unusually poor summer had limited testing, but sailing on the jib alone worked well, with a bowsprit fitted to give it clearance forward of the mainsail.[111]

## A 2013 return to the aluminum mast

The aluminum-mast idea judged too costly and abandoned for wood back in 2009 (see above) did not stay abandoned. Answering a fresh builder's own aluminum-mast question on the wider list, Alberto reported having fitted the same tube kept on hand since 2008 — 63.5 mm in diameter with a 3 mm wall — much lighter than wood and roomy enough to step all the wiring, lights, and the radio antenna inside it. At the base, a piece of wood shaped to the support fitting covers the tube's stamped end under a hard rubber strip, and the main-halyard sheave is carried on a riveted omega clamp.[112]

A further video testing the jib, sailed on the open South Atlantic, was posted in December 2014.[113]

## The aluminum mast's weight confirmed (January 2015)

Replying to a newcomer's own mast-weight question on the wider list, Alberto gave the aluminum mast's actual weight for the first time: about **6 kg** for the 63.5 mm, 3 mm-wall tube fitted since 2013. Full text and citation also at [components/rig/mast.md — finished mast weights compared](/components/rig/mast.md#finished-mast-weights-compared).[114]

## A 2015 registry entry, and a build-year discrepancy

Replying to a group-maintained registry effort, Alberto gave Cachivache's own entry as "the world's southernmost Paradox," built in Rawson, Chubut, Patagonia Argentina, in **2010** — a year earlier than both the mailing list's own record of her January 2011 launch (above) and his 2019 recollection of completing her "about 2011." The discrepancy is left unreconciled rather than resolved.[115]

## A 2017 return: Cachivache's halyard fixing point

Nine years after the mailing list first tried and failed to pin a halyard fixing point to a plans dimension (see [components/rig/index.md — halyard fixing point on the spar](/components/rig/index.md#halyard-fixing-point-on-the-spar-an-open-question)), Alberto returned to ask exactly where along the yard Cachivache's own halyard should be fixed.[116] [OkieBob](/people/okiebob.md) answered from his own boat's experience: three feet (about 92 cm) along the yard as a starting point, then adjusted forward or aft depending on how much weather helm is wanted.[117]

## A 2018 thickening-agent note: talc

Answering a question addressed to Alastair Law and "others" about what to use thickening epoxy for fillets, Alberto gave his own practice for the first time on record: talc, used throughout in building Cachivache.[118]

## The 2012 clew-fouling fix, finally photographed (March 2021)

The mainsheet/outboard fix noted above only as a linked photograph (see [a recurring clew-fouling problem at the outboard mount, resolved](#a-recurring-clew-fouling-problem-at-the-outboard-mount-resolved-march-2012)) is preserved in full nine years later: a bent metal loop, mounted at the transom corner beside the outboard, lifts the mainsheet clear over the top of the motor rather than letting it drag across the mount — the same "horse" arrangement floated but never confirmed built on the mailing list back in 2005 (see [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling — a horse fitted to clear the outboard](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-horse-fitted-to-clear-the-outboard-and-the-mainsheet-angle-physics-behind-it)). Two photographs show the loop from both the outboard side and the stern, the hull's registration marking visible in the second.[119]

## A river-mouth passage, and the motor's real use case (March 2021)

Cachivache is kept moored in the **Río Chubut**, at **Rawson** (43.20°S, 65.03°W), and an **Evinrude 3.3 hp** outboard is what gets her from the mooring down the river and out to the open sea, and back in again — a concrete answer to why a motor earns its place aboard even a boat meant to sail: the river passage itself is the use case, not open-water cruising. An aerial photograph of Puerto Rawson's harbour, at the river's mouth, was attached to illustrate the passage.[120]
