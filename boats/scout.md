# Scout

**"Scout"** is Sean Mulligan's [Paradox](/paradox.md), sailed on **Lake Havasu, Arizona**; she was built to a high standard in Texas by **[Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md)** under the name **"Tardis,"** a build Henson documented step by step on her own blog.[1] Henson sold her **almost finished and never sailed** her; Mulligan then bought her, finished her, launched her on **12 September 2013**, and renamed her "Scout," documenting the completion and her first season on his own blog, [Full n By](/references/full-n-by-blog.md).[2][3] He founded the **Havasu Pocket Cruiser's Convention**, and, with his wife Jo, also owns and sails a trailer sailer named "Dauntless."[4][5]

## The build: a stock Paradox named "Tardis"

"Tardis" was **[Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md)'s** first from-scratch boat, built to the plans in the Houston, Texas area (Pasadena, then Kemah) across roughly two years — the bulkheads first dry-fitted in January 2011, the bottom glued on by March, and the boat all but complete by early 2013 — and documented step by step on her blog.[1] It is a **stock Paradox**, built carefully and a little beyond the minimum. Henson **insulated** the hull (not all Paradoxes are), covering the insulation with 5 mm ply screwed to epoxied dowel pegs, and built the plans' **400 lb of ballast** as lead "pigs" in bolt-down "pens" alongside water-tank ballast that her husband **Dale**, an electrical engineer, plumbed with 3-way valves for ballast use only.[6] She made her own **gimbaled stove** from aluminium angle to swivel both ways, modelled on **[Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md)'s** stove for "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" rather than pay for a bought one — the "single burner Coleman stove fitted to gimbals" a UK observer would later note at second hand.[7][8] A copper-flashing ground plate was bedded over woven roving on the bottom, the hull finished in Glidden Porch & Floor off-white with a navy stripe and bright-varnished spars and trim, and non-skid applied to the decks per Charlie Jones's method on "Traveler."[9] She built to the plans plus **[Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s** construction manual, with questions worked out on the **[Paradox mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md)**; the full first-hand record is preserved at [Lezlie's World](/references/lezlie-henson-blog.md).[1]

## An unfinished "Tardis," and a UK import cost estimate (January 2013)

While still owned by Lezlie Henson and offered for sale at Kemah, near Houston, Texas, for "in the region of 3,000 US dollars" (about £1,861), "Tardis" was unfinished: her window hardware and materials were not yet bought or fitted, her boom was unfinished, and she lacked a yuloh, anchors, fenders, and "the usual cruising gear," though she already carried a single-burner Coleman stove fitted to gimbals.[8]

Henson was selling because the project had lost its purpose. Begun as a dream shared with her husband, "Tardis" became "a reminder of a dead dream" after their separation; she lost her motivation to finish and launch, and sold her almost complete, in February 2013, **without ever having sailed her** — "I do wish I had been able to sail her... at least once."[2] She had already announced the coming sale on the mailing list itself two months earlier, in December 2012, framed instead around an opportunity to go sailing for an extended period of time; full text at [people/lezlie-henson.md](/people/lezlie-henson.md#sold-unlaunched-and-a-candid-record-of-why).[26] Her own blog recorded the January 2013 interest from the United Kingdom — a phone call and forum discussion of the sale — the seller's side of the same episode as the import estimate below.[10]

A UK enthusiast priced shipping her from Houston to Felixstowe, itemizing freight and the various port and handling charges along the way. Because an unfinished, amateur-built boat needed its own HM Revenue and Customs commodity code, and the boat and her secondhand trailer needed separate VAT invoices, importing her to Britain would have added substantial cost and complexity on top of the purchase price.[11]

"Tardis" was ultimately listed at **$3,000**, needing only to be rigged; unsure whether the figure was fair, Henson put the pricing question to the mailing list itself, drawing a fuller valuation framework — comparable sales, replacement cost, and supply and demand — at [secondhand Paradox market](/operations/secondhand-market.md#tardis-priced-at-3000-and-a-fuller-valuation-framework-january-2013).[27]

## Finishing "Tardis," and her first launch as "Scout" (2013)

He had introduced himself to the mailing list itself on 1 February 2013, ahead of the boat's own arrival in Arizona from Texas, asking for the yuloh's dimensions after a friend already checked the plans and build manual without finding them; [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md) pointed him to the plans' own sheet 12, "spar details," which gives the mast, boom, yard, and yuloh together.[28][29] [Dave Raftery](/people/dave-raftery.md) answered too, confirming the same sheet and pointing to a fellow Enigma-460 builder's own photographed yuloh-making, and mentioned his own Enigma build was then about two years behind Mulligan's Paradox.[30] Thanking both for the pointers, Mulligan reflected that buying a boat someone else's "blood sweat and tears" had gone into felt a little like "cheating the system" — a debt he meant to repay by sailing her hard, and a first departure from the trailer sailer he and Jo already owned and sailed.[31] Later the same month he asked the list how the tack strop's deck-entry tube and its line were meant to work, drawing a full answer covered at [tack strop — ten years' service confirms the deck-entry fix, and a chafe cure](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#a-2013-recurrence-ten-years-service-confirms-the-deck-entry-fix-and-a-chafe-cure).[32] The same week he also asked how to keep the rudder blade locked down under sail, drawing on the established gravity-and-weighted-uphaul answer at [rudder and chine experiments — keeping the blade down under way](/components/rudder.md#keeping-the-blade-down-under-way-gravity-a-weighted-uphaul-and-a-raised-height-stop).[33]

That April, still working toward completion, he wondered on the list how many other Paradoxes existed and were still sailing, and whether any registry existed to find out — a question the self-reported [2014 worldwide census](/references/builders-and-owners-census-2014.md) would only partly answer, itself a floor rather than a full count, more than a year later.[34] By late July, with new trailer plates fitted for both "Scout" and the Montgomery 23 "Dauntless," he judged himself so close to finished he could taste it, but expected the summer heat to push actual completion into September — a forecast the boat's own 12 September launch (below) bore out almost exactly.[35] Three weeks later he confirmed work had resumed after a pause, still aiming for a September launch, and pointed the list to his blog for the fuller day-by-day record.[36] A few days after that he pointed the list to the blog once more for a further progress update, and again two days later still, and once more a little over a week after that, with a final "ready to splash" teaser the day of the launch itself.[39][40][41][42]

Mulligan bought "Tardis" about **95 % complete** and finished her over the summer of 2013, documenting the work on his blog, [Full n By](/references/full-n-by-blog.md). He rated Henson's build "first class," and the completion was mostly small work, though it took him longer than he expected.[12] He found the mast built to a variation from the plans — a stainless eyebolt let into the side of the mast in place of the plans' internal halyard sheave, with a built-up platform around the masthead for an anchor light.[12] Her finished mast, complete with its masthead band and internal copper lightning-ground run, weighed **10.45 kg**.[37] Scout's mast wasn't built from scratch by Mulligan and its copper conductor was already installed when he acquired the boat, so the band and conductor's own separate weight couldn't be pinned down — the mast band alone was guessed, unconfirmed, at somewhere close to half a kilo; see [mast — finished mast weights compared](/components/rig/mast.md#finished-mast-weights-compared) for the fuller cross-owner comparison.[38] Inside, he left out the plans' removable bow partitions (which Henson had already built) and moved the battery **forward and on-centre**, to trim the boat against his own weight aft and open the whole forward compartment for a bedroll.[13] He judged Henson's sail — sewn from a **Sailrite** kit — to be the later **Mark-II Paradox mainsail**, extended its yard about eight inches, and re-drilled the outhaul to cure a wrinkle in the foot.[13] He made **aluminium brackets** for the mainsheet cam-cleat and a yuloh storage bracket, and later added transom **mainsheet fairleads**, a PVC cabin **air scoop**, masthead wiring run out above the deck rather than through it, and bow and stern **dock lines pre-rigged on both sides** — a running effort to cut rig time and make her easier to single-hand.[12][14]

She was rigged and **launched for the first time on 12 September 2013** — the hull's first time ever afloat, since Henson had sold her unsailed — and Mulligan rated her the fastest, easiest boat he had ever rigged.[3] The launch was announced to the mailing list itself the following day.[43]

## Renamed "Scout," and a fast trailer-to-sailing routine on Lake Havasu

Under Mulligan's ownership on Lake Havasu, "Scout" runs from trailer to sailing in under **20 minutes**, helped by a non-tidal slipway with an adjacent jetty where she is moored to add water ballast and finish preparations before setting off — the same long-tow-bar launch method used elsewhere on the Paradox fleet, demonstrated on video reaching "trailer to sailor in under 10 min," the flying, furling boom and sail needing no hanking on and the mast simply stepped into its hole before sailing off in 25 knots, unrushed and without strain.[15][16][78] That same January 2014 launch ran on for about six hours under sail until sundown as the wind built through the afternoon to around 20 knots, documented in a further video shot on a GoPro Hero 3+ Black Edition mounted on a monopod handle, with no separate stabilizer.[79][80] Like [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md), "Scout" is sailed largely by yuloh in calms rather than by her outboard.[17] The yuloh built for her by a friend had its non-symmetrical blade cut the wrong way round; Mulligan re-worked it — flipping the blade and moving a sharper curve into the loom above the pivot so it self-orients leading-edge-down on both strokes — to lift his sustained sculling speed from about **1.75 knots** toward 2.[18] His own blog records a first autumn-2013 season of day-sails and overnight rendezvous at **Steamboat Cove**, about 6.5 nm south of Lake Havasu City, in winds up to **15–25 knots**.[19]

## A second shakedown sail, and early reception

Six days after the launch, video of a second shakedown sail in **20+ knots of wind** went up on the mailing list itself — the primary-source posting of very likely the same clip Bill's Log relisted the following year among "Scout" videos.[20][44] Glen Maxwell, who by then had sold "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," congratulated the sail and said it made him wish he still owned her.[45] Gustavo Krause, a sailor and shipbuilder in Brazil, shared the same video with his own local sailing and shipbuilding community.[46] The video also drew a rueful reply from **[OkieBob](/people/okiebob.md)** — who had followed "Tardis" since 2012, hoping to see her at that year's Texas 200 — admitting he was "kicking myself for missing the brief window she was for sale"; Mulligan confirmed he had moved fast to secure her, certain someone else would otherwise have "scarfed her up."[47][48]

**[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)**, then still working out his own first Paradox, "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)," watched the video and called Mulligan "one hell of a sailor" for going out a second time in such wind — he had recognized the sailing spot himself from a Christmas visit to Lake Havasu the year before — and asked for a fuller account than "the boat sailed magnificently."[49]

## Handling under sail: stability, tacking, and a fast reefing system

Mulligan's reply gave the fullest first-person handling account on record for "Scout." Heeled to her greatest angle, she never felt "on the edge" — stability exceeded his expectations. Tacking felt entirely unfamiliar: rather than pivoting at the centre of the hull like a keel or centreboard boat, she pivots on the rudder, "like a forklift," pushing the whole hull against the wind and sea; he blew several tacks before being advised to shift weight forward to dig in the bow and add lift. The tiller felt backwards from what he was used to, and he found himself grabbing the wrong direction more than once; a heavier control-line diameter gave a better grip at the cost of more pulley drag and less rudder feedback. He judged the boat's slower response to the helm a consequence of having no lateral resistance amidships — no keel or centreboard — rather than a fault.[50]

The reefing/furling system rewarded correct halyard tension: hauled tight, the strop stands vertical and the furling line pulls easily; slack, the strop leans forward and the furling line becomes heavy and difficult. He ran a 2:1 purchase on the halyard — through a block on the yard and back to a dead-end at the masthead — halving the pull at the cost of twice the line to haul, and added a topping lift to support the boom and ease furling and reefing, at the cost of occasionally tangling in the yard. The mainsheet, rigged as per plans, worked but snagged cleats and rudder gudgeons, a problem he expected to worsen once he mounted a Honda 2 hp outboard.[50]

The stock plexiglass windows, cut but not installed when he bought the boat, gave early trouble: the windshield's compound curve fractured the plexiglass immediately, and he replaced it with 1/4 in lexan — tougher, but harder to heat-form into place. The plexiglass side windows, kept to save money, began cracking at the screw holes under the press of his arm when heeled; he judged 1/4 in lexan the better material throughout, especially with coastal ocean sailing in view.[50]

The yuloh felt awkward on only its second real use — he sailed off and back onto the dock rather than test it properly in wind. A short downwind leg under full sail at over 5 knots showed no unruliness despite all the sail set to one side. He rated the boat's reefing speed a particular strength: unlike most boats, which jam in the mast slot if the main is raised or lowered off the wind, "Scout" can be sheeted out to depower at almost any angle and then furled or reefed — "an ingenious contraption." For line management, small hanging bags — rock-climbers' chalk bags, with a rigid lip and hanging loops, tried one per sheet — and different line colors were both taken up after 3/16 in line proved to be "everywhere" underfoot while sailing. He fitted a masthead light and a lightweight anemometer during this stretch, and planned a VHF antenna, a GPS mount, and a compass next, budget allowing; he judged himself still needing a proper wind indicator, having found it hard to gauge wind direction from inside the sheltered cabin.[50]

## Seat height and a big sailor's fit

**[Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)**, sailing his own Paradox "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," asked whether Mulligan had deliberately mounted Scout's seat higher than the plans specify, or was simply tall — on his own boat the boom clears his head with room to spare and the sail does not block his view, and lounging heeled over, his shoulder tucks just under the top cabin member with only his head in the wind.[51] Mulligan confirmed the seat was built to plans: at **6 ft 4 in and 230 lb**, he is simply a big man for the boat — "my friends say I look like I am on a carnival kiddie ride when sailing Scout!"[52]

A further inquiry, prompted by a photograph that made Mulligan look like he was wearing "Scout" rather than sitting in her, asked how big he really was — the questioner, self-described as "a bit of a freak," gave his own frame as a six-footer's torso on legs with a 27-inch inseam and very wide (EEEEE) feet, hoping for a better sense of his own likely fit.[74] Mulligan put the camera's exaggeration down to focal length, restating his size at about **6 ft 4 in and 220 lb** — consistent with the figure already on record — and reported the interior proving far roomier than anyone would expect from outside, having by then spent a full night aboard "quite comfortably."[75]

## The Bosunator Challenge, a recurring yuloh-socket problem, and a fourth shakedown video

Mulligan announced "Scout"'s **Bosunator Challenge** — 40-50 miles, no motor, no excuses, no whining — pointing the list to his blog for details.[55] Preparing for it turned up a recurring problem: the yuloh kept popping out of its pivot socket. **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)**, who had fought the same problem on his own "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)," passed on the fix that had worked for him there — deepening the socket by a further half inch (to a full inch total, deeper than the ball itself) and gluing in a short length of hose standing proud of the yuloh by half an inch, giving the yuloh just enough flexible resistance that it no longer pops free even when let go entirely.[56] Mulligan planned to try seating the pin deeper and separately wondered about an off-centre pivot mount, similar to Enigma's, for a more comfortable standing position.[57] **[Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)**, sailing "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," answered with his own fix — a pivot socket seated directly in thickened epoxy, its depth adjustable at will simply by filling and re-drilling, needing refurbishment only every four or five years — and confirmed the design's own pivot pin is already meant to sit offset.[58] Bourbeau followed up a few days later to report the hose fix still doing its job, flexing slightly with the working yuloh rather than resisting it outright; Mulligan thanked him and considered trying something similar of his own.[59][60] Full technique record at [yuloh — ball-joint versus oarlock, and pivot-socket wear over time](/components/yuloh.md#ball-joint-versus-oarlock-and-pivot-socket-wear-over-time).

Video of "Scout"'s fourth shakedown sail went up the same week, in **10-17 knots** of wind: the shortened boom worked well, newly fitted LED running lights were in place and working, and the boat pointed well in company with keelboats — "very happy so far."[61]

## Sailing the Bosunator Challenge, and the lessons that followed

The Bosunator Challenge itself, sailed from a sunrise start on **Friday 18 October 2013** to a 3 a.m. finish the following morning, covered a roughly 50-mile course, engine-less, against eight other boats, about half downwind and half upwind, in conditions ranging from flat calm to 26 mph apparent wind:[92] extended downwind runs reached 5.3 kt with a 5.6 kt maximum, while a large wind chop upwind killed speed even though "Scout" still pointed well for progress to windward. Overnight, under a full moon with the wind eased to 10–13 kt and the water flattened out, the course ran back up the Colorado River past coyotes and burros heard calling from shore. Roughly 10 miles of the course, at the calm start and finish, were covered by paddle and yuloh: alternating a stand-up-paddleboard paddle (nearly 2 kt sustained) with the yuloh (slightly less) traded muscle groups and standing positions and made the effort more bearable. Total time on the course was about 20 hours.[62]

The 20-hour stretch surfaced problems a short day-sail never would: chafing mainsheet lines, comfort, night-time lighting, paddling gear, and ventilation were all flagged for modification once home, with the fixes already under way before the next test sail.[63]

## The same sail written up for a magazine readership

Six weeks after the challenge, Mulligan told the same day a third time — as "The Upside of Downsizing" in *[Small Craft Advisor](/references/small-craft-advisor.md)*, the only published magazine account of a Paradox written by the person sailing her. Pitched at readers who had never heard of the design, it drops the challenge's name, rules and date altogether and keeps the case for small boats instead, which is why several of its details appear nowhere in the two contemporaneous accounts.[93]

The boat's **designed displacement is given as 1,460 lb**, in the specific context of how hard she is to drive by hand — "not the lightest girl at the dance, nor the ultimate paddle/yuloh machine" — a figure some 50 lb above the design displacement the archive's other independent estimates settle on (see [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)). Her **9-inch draft and kick-up rudder** are described not as a specification but as what let him cut the corner of every twist of the lake to within feet of the shoreline, where the keelboats he was racing could not follow. Her anemometer, reading 26 mph apparent on the upwind leg, was made by **Inspeed** — the only Paradox wind instrument whose make is on record. She carried **no outboard and no bracket for one**, deliberately and by contrast with the Montgomery 23 "Dauntless," which he had sailed up to 100 nm motorless but always with the outboard aboard ready to go: "knowing that my fate is not tied to the outboard is a liberating feeling for sure." Setup and teardown at the ramp ran **around 20 minutes each way** — consistent with the under-20-minute trailer-to-sailing routine already recorded above — and at home she was "easily tucked away in the garage" with the trailer moved around by hand. He described the purchase as "over 90% complete," consistent with the 95 % figure already on record. The friend who stayed with him through the night was **Jan Maslikowski**, sailing a Balboa 20, who had dropped his headsail hours earlier to slow to the Paradox's pace rather than sail on past, and who took the photograph that closes the article.[93]

Its figures are rounder than his own contemporaneous account of the same sail — a "30-plus mile course," 5.5 kt rather than 5.6, 3 kt rather than 2 upwind in the chop — and where the two differ the earlier accounts should be preferred; the divergences are set out in full at [Small Craft Advisor](/references/small-craft-advisor.md).[93]

## The builder and the sailor, in public

The magazine article's comment thread is the only place on record where **the woman who built this hull and the man who sailed her speak to each other in public**. [Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md), who had sold "Tardis" almost finished and never sailed her, wrote that she was "so proud of Scout, and so glad you are taking her out as much as you do. She is a capable boat. I have enjoyed watching her adventures."[94] Mulligan answered by making the authorship plain to a readership that would not have known it: Henson was "Scout's true Mamma," who "built Scout from the ground up from nothing more than a set of plans…and did one heck of a job on her," and he counted himself the boat's **custodian** rather than her maker, directing readers to [Lezlie's World](/references/lezlie-henson-blog.md) for practically the whole build.[95]

The pairing was repeated in person the following year. In August 2014 the organisers of **Sail Oklahoma** announced that Mulligan and Henson would give a **joint program on "Scout"** — her build, her finishing, and her sailing — at the **October 2014 event**, the only recorded occasion the two appeared together; see [gatherings](/merits/gatherings.md#sail-oklahoma-2014-a-joint-builderowner-program-on-scout).[96]

The same thread carries the **earliest dated statement of Mulligan's [Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md) ambition**. Asked by a reader whether the engine-less run had been "a tune up for Florida," he answered that he was "positive that Scout is up to the task of the Everglades Challenge" — [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) having "proved that handily" — while doubting he was himself "a quarter the sailor he is," and confirmed that sailing progressively longer distances in her was partly a way of judging whether to attempt it.[97] That reasoning is the one his Texas 200 report returned to seven months later, when the 2016 attempt was judged a real possibility (below).

## A transom-fairlead fix for the mainsheet, refined with a fellow builder's questions

Fitting two fairleads to the top of the transom cured the mainsheet fouling Mulligan had been fighting; several weeks on, no line had gone foul since, though the fix remained untested against an outboard, not yet fitted.[64] Exiting the sheet at the transom's top carries one remaining risk: the line can still chafe against the edge of the deck/transom joint running downwind. Mulligan had tried curing that with a rub strake, but found it hard to make one strake conform to both the deck's curve and the radius of the deck/transom union at once — the very difficulty that led him to the fairlead fix instead, which he judged "not idea[l]," but working well.[65] **[sanibelowner](/people/sanibelowner.md)**, already established as the builder of "[Moxie](/boats/moxie.md)," proposed drilling his own sheet hole in the same spot, and signed this message "Andy" — the only time a first name appears in his messages on record.[66] Mulligan approved the plan, cautioning a rubstrake would likely still be needed at the deck/transom corner to prevent chafe — a cleaner solution, in his view, than Scout's own line, which exits at its plans-designed point and then runs up the transom's exterior to reach the fairlead.[67] The same exchange renewed his verdict on Henson's Sailrite sail: still setting well, with only two changes still under consideration — smaller-diameter sleeves for the yard and boom, the yard especially, being small enough in cross-section to get lost inside its own sleeve — and a window let into the sail for visibility to leeward when sailing close-hauled from the seat.[64] Further video went up in December, of ghosting Scout upwind in a dying breeze.[68]

## A further cold-weather sail, and a tacking-angle question renewed

Video shot by friends sailing a San Juan 23 nearby caught "Scout" out in an estimated 15-17 knots of wind: the San Juan ran faster upwind, but downwind "Scout" held her own in the low 6-knot range with a good wind chop pushing her and the barn door out — "could almost keep up with them." Setting more sail than usual to punch upwind through the chop held speed to about 3 knots at some cost in pointing, but made most tacks on the first attempt even in the higher gusts, a couple stalling out in gusts topping 25 knots and needing a paddle stroke to bring her round. Cold enough that the cabin vent was aimed into the wind for fresh air rather than warmth; the toe rail going under was never an issue, easing the sheet standing her right back up.[69]

**[Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)** asked how the sailing compared on a lake, free of the tidal currents that complicate the same question on his own home water, and what tacking angle the boat was actually making through the conditions shown — renewing the GPS-versus-compass tacking-angle debate worked through that October (see [pointing and tacking-angle expectations](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md#gps-course-over-ground-versus-compass-and-apparent-wind-two-ways-to-define-tacking-angle)). He added that such sailing, once experienced, "dooms" a sailor "to go through life with people refusing to believe your sailing accounts."[70]

## Confidence, reefing, and a downwind race against a San Juan 23

Mulligan answered days later with the fullest account yet of his own growing trust in the boat; his GPS-based tacking-angle estimate, and the exchange it prompted over heel angle and a mid-tack sail-trim technique, are covered in full at [pointing and tacking-angle expectations](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md#gps-course-over-ground-versus-compass-and-apparent-wind-two-ways-to-define-tacking-angle). He judged his biggest early problem had been under-canvassing the boat out of anticipation and nervousness rather than what conditions actually called for, and found simply sailing more in stronger breezes cured it. His early worries about stability were gone: "Scout" lies fine right over on her side with the side deck awash, standing straight back up as soon as sheet pressure eases or she heads up; once that trust was established he carried more sail for better power and drive, still hunting the sweet spot between sail area, wind speed, and heel, mindful that Dave sails his own boat flatter. In all but the strongest conditions he had mastered tacking cleanly; on the rare occasion windchop stalled her head to wind, carrying speed into the tack, shifting weight forward, feeding the rudder in smoothly rather than jerking it, and timing the turn against the swell mostly avoided it, with a paddle stroke as a fallback he found himself needing less and less. He rated the reefing system's speed — under 20 seconds, on almost any point of sail, without heading up — the single most confidence-building feature of the design, and reported crawling upwind off a leeward shore in 25 knots of "lake conditions," a benchmark he judged real for a flat-bottomed boat.[71]

The same message described a friendly downwind contest: friends filming from a nearby San Juan 23 watched "Scout" hold her own, busting into the 6-knot range with one momentary 7.1-knot blast on a piece of chop, still right alongside the larger keelboat after about two miles — to the disbelief of her own crew.[71]

**[Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)** replied at length, confirming the tacking-angle estimate matched a guess of his own and adding his own figures and a mid-tack sail-trim technique — full detail at [pointing and tacking-angle expectations](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md#gps-course-over-ground-versus-compass-and-apparent-wind-two-ways-to-define-tacking-angle) — plus a lee-shore self-rescue story of his own, told in full at [Little Jim — a lee-shore self-rescue under sail alone](/boats/little-jim.md#a-lee-shore-self-rescue-under-sail-alone-december-2013).[72] Mulligan thanked him for the comparison, reflecting on how few people have actually sailed a Paradox and how hard it is for anyone who hasn't to grasp what the boat can do — "most of my buddies just look at me and shake their heads."[73]

## A windward proving in 22-30 knot wind, and a further walkthrough video (January 2014)

A sail in steady 22-25 knot wind with gusts to 30 answered any doubt about the chine runners' windward ability: "Scout" went to windward just fine, even clawing off a lee shore visible in the background, and shrugged off a 2-3 ft breaking swell that kept Mulligan warm and dry — "more amazed every time" he pushes the boat, and encouragement, in the same post, for anyone mid-build to keep going.[76] A further video ten days later gave a fuller walkthrough of the boat, inside and out.[77]

## Rig deviations: a 2:1 halyard purchase, and a stainless mainsheet chainplate

"Scout"'s rig departs from the stock plans in two respects worked out through ownership. The halyard runs as a **2:1 purchase** rather than the plans' 1:1: instead of leading straight down from the masthead pulley to the yard and dead-ending there, it continues through a second pulley fitted on the yard and back up to dead-end at the masthead block (a block with a becket would be ideal). The change makes the yard feel about **50% lighter** to hoist and allows much tighter luff tension for windward work, at the cost of hauling and releasing twice the length of line.[81]

Reefing and unreefing go easiest with the topping lift pulled really tight while the furling line is worked: kept taut, it holds the tack strop standing straight up, giving the furling line a fair, low-friction lead from deck to drum; let it slacken even a little and the boom creeps forward, the strop leans with it, and the furling line's own lead angle turns sharp enough to bind. The standing routine is to tighten the topping lift just before working the furler and ease it again once done, so it doesn't distort the sail's shape off the wind. A genuine hazard of a two-legged topping lift: if the halyard and both sides of the yard end up on the wrong side of one leg during the initial hoist, the sail can only be raised as far as the branch point where the two legs meet — care is needed only for the first few feet of hoist, after which the yard cannot cross to the wrong side.[81]

The mainsheet attaches to the boom through a **solid stainless steel chainplate**, mounted about midway along the boom and extending above and below it — the topping lift ties to its top, the mainsheet to its bottom — thick and stiff enough not to flex as the square boom's rotating corners pass it while furling. An earlier, thinner stainless tang of the kind called for in the plans flexed inward enough for the rotating corners to hang up on it; the heavier chainplate solved the problem, a second, independent case of the same failure mode and fix already documented for the [boom-end strap](/components/rig/furling.md#full-hardware-and-rigging-recipe).[81]

A third departure, noticed independently by a fellow list member rather than by Mulligan himself: Scout furls in the direction opposite to the plans, an observation and its rationale — a vertical luff, and a possible windward advantage on the starboard tack — recorded in full at [furling system — a further reversed-furl account](/components/rig/furling.md#a-further-reversed-furl-account-a-vertical-luff-and-a-possible-starboard-tack-advantage-january-2014). Mulligan confirmed the choice was never a deliberate one, arrived at by feel and left unchanged once pointed out.[82][83] The physical cause surfaced a few days later: her furling-rod thimble sits to starboard, opposite the stock plan's port side — see [furling system — a furl-direction/thimble alignment rule](/components/rig/furling.md#a-furl-directionthimble-alignment-rule-and-an-alternate-mainsheet-tang-fitting-31-january-1-february-2014) for the general rule this confirms, and for the mainsheet-tang fastening method given in the same reply.[85]

## Heavy-wind sailing, and the 2014 Texas 200

Video posted of "Scout" documents her sailing in **25+ knot** wind on Lake Havasu and in brisk conditions on Thompson Bay, and shows her tacking upwind under sail.[20] "Scout" also ran the **[Texas 200](/merits/texas-200.md#the-texas-200-and-a-port-townsend-wooden-boat-show-showing)** cruise in 2014 — entered as a fundraiser for the **Livestrong Foundation**, which supports cancer survivors, those still in the fight, and those already lost, with her sides carrying all of their names[84] — and during that event towed a fellow competitor's boat, "Crippled Duck," through 20–25 kt wind.[21] A further edited video intercut "Scout" footage among clips of other boats sailed and fellow sailors met over the preceding year or two.[86]

The run itself, in a first-person account written on returning home: 200 miles covered over five days of sailing, in conditions ranging from light air to 30+ knots, the maximum reading 34 knots apparent on the masthead anemometer. On the first day several entered Puddle Duck Racers were caught out in high wind and a heavy swell, breaking rudders and masts; "Scout" hooked up to one in an estimated 20–25 kt and towed it about 20 miles to a beach, where the fleet got every damaged boat fixed on the spot before continuing on. The run doubled as a trial for a possible attempt at the **[Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md)**, judged to have gone well enough to leave a 2016 attempt a real possibility.[87] A Flickr photo set and, separately, a single consolidated video playlist gathering a short teaser, a daily video log for each of the five days, and footage other competitors had shared followed within the month.[88][89]

That November, a further overnight cruise to **Steamboat Cove** — the same anchorage as the 2013 rendezvous above — coincided with the Leonids meteor shower, with good wind sailing down to the cove with friends.[90]

## Later ownership: Chris Curtis, then Lee (Maine)

Mulligan later sold "Scout" on to **[Chris Curtis](/people/chris-curtis.md)** of **Sandpoint, Idaho** — the boat's third owner, giving the hull an ownership chain of **Henson → Mulligan → Curtis**. The purchase itself is best dated to **March 2015**: replying on the mailing list to André-François Bourbeau's own St-Johns River cruise report, Curtis — previously tracked separately as a below-threshold namesake under his `joe_mapango` mailing-list handle, an identity now confirmed to be this same owner (see [people/chris-curtis.md#disambiguation](/people/chris-curtis.md#disambiguation)) — signed off "S/V Scout" for the first time on record, alongside his Enigma 460 "Fantasma," and reported having "recently purchased a Paradox."[91] Under Curtis she has been **shown several times at the Port Townsend Wooden Boat Show** (pictured there around 2017), and in 2019 he called her "a great boat in almost every way."[22] Curtis sailed her for many years and camped aboard many nights, "primarily sailing her in North Idaho and the Puget Sound," day-sailing her hard on **Priest Lake** — where a gust once knocked her down, though not past about 90°.[23][24] He rated her built "to a very high standard," her SailRite sail "beautifully made by Lezlie," fitted with a **30-watt solar panel** and charge controller and insulated to design; her one departure from the plans was a **matchboard (hatchboard) closing the aft cabin** in place of a fixed panel, which he counted an improvement.[23] Her 2014 [Texas 200](/merits/texas-200.md#the-texas-200-and-a-port-townsend-wooden-boat-show-showing) run was during Mulligan's ownership. Under Curtis's ownership she carried a trolling motor with its control head cut off and the speed controller mounted inside the boat — small, unobtrusive, and clear of the mainsheet, working well though with limited range, enough for his own minimal power needs; a 2.5 hp petrol outboard he also owned was never fitted, its weight and sheet-fouling risk judged not worth it against the trolling motor's own drawbacks. Full account of the wider 2021 thread and the fix another owner reached independently: [auxiliary outboard power — a 2021 recurrence](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-2021-recurrence-two-owners-head-cut-trolling-motor-conversions-and-a-torqeedo-ultralight-considered).[105]

## A pre-purchase test sail against an Enigma 460

Before he ever owned her, Curtis had already sailed against "Tardis." Racing his own **Enigma 460** — a lighter-displacement Layden design he already owned — against what would later become his own Paradox, at a point when Mulligan was still piloting her with **[Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md)** herself along for the ride, Curtis caught up over a few miles in fairly strong wind despite giving away the Enigma's longer waterline; "Tardis," carrying two aboard, was more heavily loaded than his own boat at the time, and the performance difference between the two designs proved minimal, "all scratching edges."[98]

Racing the same two boats again in February 2020, by then owning both outright, Curtis got a far more decisive result: his Enigma 460 "beat the pants off" his own "Scout," which he put down to her being hundreds of pounds lighter and a few feet longer rather than to any inherent difference in sailing quality. Fuller comparison, and the general Paradox-versus-Enigma case: [matt-layden/enigma.md — a 2020 head-to-head](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2020-head-to-head-an-owners-own-enigma-460-against-his-own-paradox).[103]

By **May 2018** Curtis had opened that refit at its most pressing point: his one built-in departure from the plans, a matchboard drop-board closing the aft cabin in place of the designed fixed panel, had produced fine stress cracks in the unreinforced plywood deck around its own hatch opening, forcing him to cut off the cabin's wooden hand-holds and remove the side windows to get at the problem.[99] Clearing the old, ton-of-100-percent-silicone bedding compound out from under the removed windows drew three independent suggestions — a sharp chisel, from a fellow owner's own experience stripping every window off [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md); acetone; and a razor-blade scraper followed by rough sandpaper, from a fellow builder who had fought the identical job on his own Paradox a couple of years earlier, and who separately confirmed the same structural lesson from a second boat of his own — an as-built open rear cabin proved too hard to keep waterproof and weakening enough to the cabin that he closed it up and rebuilt her to the stock fixed panel (see [windows — the rear window is structure, not an opening port](/components/windows.md#the-rear-window-is-structure-not-an-opening-port)).[100][101][102] The wider refit — deck hardware re-bedded, windows reinstalled and repainted, and a small leak repaired at the vent-hole end-grain — stalled when he moved on to a much larger boat, and he offered her for sale from 2022.[23] Reinstalling the windows themselves, in July 2020, raised a bedding-sealant question of its own — the range of answers the fleet gave is at [window glazing materials — bedding sealant](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#bedding-sealant-a-cost-comparison).[104] In **July 2022**, by then living aboard a larger boat in Seattle and planning to leave for Mexico that September, Curtis posted a dedicated notice on the mailing list offering both **"Scout"** and his Enigma 460 **"Fantasma"** for sale together — the Paradox kept in north Idaho, trailerable to western Washington if needed and findable on YouTube by searching "scout paradox sailboat" — calling himself "a motivated seller" who needed to sell rather than wrap and store the boats before leaving.[106] In **August 2024** he sold her to **Lee, of Maine**, her fourth owner, who trailered her some 3,500 miles home — extending the chain to **Henson → Mulligan → Curtis → Lee**.[25]
