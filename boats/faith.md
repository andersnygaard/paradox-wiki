# Faith

**"Faith"** is [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md)'s UK-built [Paradox](/paradox.md), built strictly to Matt Layden's original plans. Bill started construction in August 2005 after a kit-Paradox deal fell through, and launched Faith in mid-June 2007. Her fuller day-by-day cruising record lives at [references/bills-log.md](/references/bills-log.md), the blog Bill kept throughout the build and her cruises; this page covers the durable build story, her specifications, and her notable voyages. For Bill's community/registry role and person-level record, see [people/bill-serjeant.md](/people/bill-serjeant.md).

## A kit Paradox that fell through, then a scratch build begins

In January 2005, Jordan Boats agreed to supply Bill a "kit" Paradox, working with Matt Layden on the project — but by July 2005 the kit would not be ready after all, the panel design/cutting process needing more research than could be resolved soon. The ten sheets of plywood bought for the kit were passed on to Bill regardless, arriving 1 August 2005 cut to the layout given on sheet 6 of the plans (two 18 mm sheets for the bottom, rudder case and bulkhead 3; four 12 mm sheets for the sides, bulkhead 2, the rudder blade, the cabin sole, shelves and the transom; three 9 mm sheets for the decking, bulkheads 1 and 4, and storage bins; one 6 mm sheet for the cabin roof, hatch cover and a ventilation baffle), and he built from Matt Layden's original plans instead — crediting the decision partly to trying out ["Little Jim"](/boats/little-jim.md) firsthand at Plymouth. He wanted a kit purely for speed rather than to avoid building; he still believed one could be made to work if the bottom were built from three thinner plywood layers rather than the plans' single-thickness panel. Full account at [references/paradox-uk-website.md — a UK kit-built option: Jordan Boats](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#a-uk-kit-built-option-jordan-boats).[1][2][3][4]

Deck beams were laminated from five strips of 5 mm plywood over a jig, each originally clamped 48 hours before Bill halved that once he learned other builders found 24 hours enough, pushing his production rate toward three beams a week. All seven beams are identical but for length. With the garage barely 8 ft wide against Paradox's 4 ft beam, every small part was built first and the hull itself reserved for when there would be room to work around it. Full technique record at [construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#why-4-ft-strips-are-long-enough-for-every-beam).[5][6][7][8][9][10]

## Ballast, rudder, and mast: autumn 2005

In September 2005 Bill cast the rudder's own lead ballast into its trailing edge, using a mould built from an oak plank and softwood wedges — Matt Layden had calculated the job would need "3 kilos of lead, which proved to be accurate." The lead was later faired and GRP-sheathed using a homemade sanding board built to a Don Elliott design. The hull's own main ballast called for ten pigs of scrap lead at around 40 lb each, some 400 lb in total.[11][12][13] That December, addressing a pivot-bolt water-ingress question he had first raised on the list two years earlier, Bill bushed the rudder's own pivot bolt by the oversized-hole, epoxy-fill-and-rebore method, judging it "a very good solution"; full technique at [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#bushing-the-pivot-bolt-against-water-ingress).[14][15][16] Two years on, restating the design's ballast placement for a fellow builder, he confirmed Faith's own ballast, fitted per Matt Layden's plan including the centred water ballast, had needed no adjustment since.[17]

Fitting the mast together in October 2005, Bill routed the masthead light's wiring into the hollow mast to a plug-in socket at the base, applied the plan's fibreglass reinforcement at the head, and rejected two Windex wind-indicators in turn for lacking a suitable mount or requiring a masthead VHF aerial he had no intention of fitting. Paradox's construction — many small parts joined by epoxy — suited a builder with only an hour or two to spare at a time, since work could run in parallel while earlier glue-ups cured.[18][19][20][21]

## Winter hibernation, a Birdwatcher temptation, and naming her "Faith"

By November 2005 the build had stalled for the winter: epoxy needs above 15°C to cure, and with overnight frosts setting in Bill paused formally until spring. That December he called on Alastair in Yeovil to see "Little Jim" in person, and the visit left him more enthusiastic to carry on with his own build.[22][23] Work resumed in March 2006 after "a winter's hibernation" — the hull itself barely started, though the mast, boom, yard, rudder, stock, tiller, laminated beams, reefing mechanism and ballast were all already made. That same month, weighing a Phil Bolger "Birdwatcher" as an alternative project, Bill judged Paradox the easier of the two to launch and recover while trailer-sailing and, having already bought the wood and fittings, chose to continue. On 17 March 2006, after "a long deliberation considering many names," he settled on "Faith" — "I require faith more than anything to make my dream of ownership a reality"; the roughly eighty candidate names weighed first are recorded at [people/bill-serjeant.md](/people/bill-serjeant.md#building-and-launching-faith). Putting in some five hours a day, Bill cut all four frames, the transom and the side panels by the end of March, working now on a home-built mobile trolley that let the boat be wheeled in and out of the garage.[24][25][26][27][28][29][30]

By mid-May 2006 the sheer clamps, chine logs, floor supports and water-tank frames were fixed, the transom baffle fitted, and the lower rudder gudgeon cast in a mould. Over a bank holiday weekend the bottom's two large panels were joined indoors on the lounge floor through a 48-hour cure, then levered onto the upturned hull — in what turned out to be "the wettest May since 1983." Other parts went together in the house the same way: a side panel was scarfed in the hallway and the mast built in the kitchen, both without his wife's knowledge, with carpets covered in plastic sheeting throughout.[31] By late October 2006 the build had reached the interior fit-out; Bill judged the design "well designed in the ergonomic sense," with the steering lines and transverse seat both set at a comfortable working height, and built simple stowage racks and a table doubling as chart table and galley support. There is no standing headroom unless the hatch is open.[32][33][34]

Shaping the boom and yard, Bill raised a long-running question about the yard sagging under the sail's own weight and cured a persisting sag by working on the yard's curvature rather than recutting the sail — a boom is curved specifically so it keeps its shape when furled for reefing. Full technical discussion at [components/rig/index.md](/components/rig/index.md#which-side-of-the-boom-and-yard-faces-up-and-curing-persistent-yard-sag).[35][36][37][38]

## Build-cost comparison, and a hull-assembly milestone

Against an "it's only 14 feet" quip about the scale of the commitment, Bill offered his own comparative data point: having also built a 25 ft boat, he judged there was markedly less work in that bigger boat than in the Paradox he had underway, at only marginally lower cost, and judged even a full-time professional with power tools could not complete a Paradox in under six months. See [operations/secondhand-market.md](/operations/secondhand-market.md#build-vs-buy-secondhand-a-uk-newcomers-cost-benefit-hesitation) for the fuller build-versus-buy discussion this fed into. By April 2006, with fine weather arrived, Bill was expecting to begin assembling the hull within days, with practically every other part already built.[39][40]

## A dry assembly of the hull, named "Faith"

Days later, Bill posted photographs of Faith's first **dry assembly** — the hull's panels and bulkheads fitted together unglued, to check the fit before committing epoxy. The stem joint drew praise for how well it had come together. He glued the floors and vent trunk permanently in place within days, and cut in the frames; being retired, he could put in about four days a week on the build.[41][42][43][44]

## The bottom fixed, chine runners complete, and a two-month pause for Australia

By mid-June 2006 Bill reported the hull's bottom "well and truly fixed" and the chine runners just completed, with glassing the bottom next — but had to stop building for two months while away in Australia.[45]

## Chine-runner and gudgeon photos, and Mark Waters' Paradox

Bill uploaded photographs of Faith's chine runner and lower gudgeon, and reported putting a third layer of glass on her bottom ahead of the Australia trip. He also praised photographs of a fellow UK builder's completed Paradox, Mark Waters', calling the work "superb."[46][47]

## Faith's rudder: a sheet-fairlead fix, and boom-tang strength

Back from Australia, Bill posted photographs of Faith's completed rudder, which drew two independent technical replies. The rudder's height lets the mainsheet drop into the gap between the rudder stock and transom on a broad reach or run, forcing the sheet to slide up over the pintle/gudgeon on every adjustment; Bill fitted a small wooden fairlead insert to fix it, the same problem and fix independently reported by another builder — tracked in full at [components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#rudder-stock-height-and-mainsheet-fouling-at-the-transom-notch). A second, separate issue was flagged in the same photos: the stock mainsheet/topping-lift tang is not strong enough and bends under load. Bill held off strengthening his own until he could load-test the stock part, then reinforced it before launch.[48][49][50][51][52]

## Lower pintle: an alternative to drilling the rudder stock

Bill devised and photographed a method of installing the rudder's lower pintle onto the rudder stock without drilling a hole for it — a technique he judged simpler and more accurate than drilling, since a mistake there means a major rebuild.[53][54][55]

## Ballast-access and tank-top questions

Once Faith's rudder was sorted, Bill worked through how to fix down the water-ballast tank tops (a thickened-glue-and-hand-fillet technique through the access hatches) and how to reach the inner ballast bolts once the floors were in place, settling on the tank top's sides built as separate, removable "planks" for full ongoing access to both the lead ballast and its plumbing. See [design/weight-and-ballast.md](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#reaching-the-ballast-fastenings-after-the-floors-are-in) and [construction/water-tank-lining.md](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#fixing-the-tank-tops-in-place) for the full technique.[56][57][58][59][60][61]

## A removable-floorboard technique for full storage and ballast access

Bill built his own aft floorboards to the plans but without hinges, supported instead on removable under-flaps taken from the storage-bin sides, so either side of the floor could simply be lifted and slid forward to open the whole storage compartment beneath — the same "planks" approach extended to the water-tank sides for forward ballast access. See [components/interior.md](/components/interior.md#a-removable-floorboard-technique-for-full-storage-and-ballast-access) for the cross-linked technique.[62]

## Cabin sole panels revisited, a case against built-in water tanks, and a washboard endorsed

Bill set out his own bin-and-floor system in fuller technical detail: bin sides bolted to aluminium angle (rather than screwed, so they could be removed for maintenance and better withstand gale loads), with lift-out floors sliding forward or sideways for access instead of the plans' hinges. Given the choice again, he would not build in water tanks at all — the frame is strong enough without fixed soles over the tank area — preferring loose floors held by a clasp through a full capsize. On a rear-cabin-trunk washboard, originally opposed on structural grounds, Bill came round to it after a season actually sailing Faith, on condition the washboard sides are cut vertical so it cannot work loose without a full, deliberate lift — citing the Fastnet Race, in which several yachts lost washboards to exactly that failure mode.[63]

## A trim question, and Little Jim's tacking quirk

Bill asked how closely a Paradox's fore-and-aft trim needs watching, having assumed the boat was heavy enough that shifting the ballast a little one way or the other would make no real difference. The answer drew on ["Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"'s own occasional tacking trouble — see [components/rudder.md](/components/rudder.md#sudden-total-loss-of-rudder-effect-mid-tack-and-tacking-technique) for the full account.[64][65][66]

## A drinking-water pump, and photos on smallsailboats.co.uk

Rather than build a bladder or built-in tank system, Bill solved his own drinking-water supply by fitting a hand pump to a large plastic camping-style container stowed beside the stove in the lazarette, keeping spare water in bottles forward to balance the aft water's weight. The galley stove itself lifts clear of its supporting clips easily for access, and was the same unit already proven crossing the North Sea and the English Channel aboard an earlier boat.[72][73][74]

## Tools used building Faith, revisited

Bill used neither a table saw nor a bandsaw to build Faith, relying instead on an ordinary rip saw, hand planing and sanding, and an electric jigsaw for shaping plywood, with the rip saw also cutting the mast, boom, and yuloh. The one indispensable tool was the clamp — more than 30 were needed for the sheer strakes and chine logs alone. See [construction/build-effort.md](/construction/build-effort.md#tools).[77]

## A start-versus-finish perspective, and further deck photos (December 2006)

Congratulating [Pete Martin](/people/pete-martin.md) on starting his own build, Bill offered a veteran's perspective on the hardest part of any Paradox project: making the actual start. By comparison, Bill's own build — under way since late August 2005 — had by this point reached the decks.[78]

## Deck-beam length, a tiller-curve mistake, and the helm seat

The deck-beam length can be measured straight off the lines drawing (sheet 2), adding 3-4 inches; for the general chord-length table across all seven beams see [construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#deck-beam-dimensions-and-the-deck-curves-true-shape). Bill's own tiller lacked enough curve, leaving only a close shave past the last deck beam; the fix was to thin that beam rather than remake the tiller. His helm seat is laminated from strips of wood, made a bit wider than the plans' own dimension for extra support, with a large hand grip borrowed from Alastair's "Little Jim."[79][80]

## Sheer clamp, bulkhead #1, and mast support: Bill's own choices

Bulkhead #1 is a safety feature limiting how far floodwater can spread in a wild seaway; a genuinely watertight hatch or fully sealed forward section would be a stronger but more involved option. Faith carries no added buoyancy and instead relies on being built strong to the plans throughout — Bill kept bulkhead #1 solid as drawn, unlike Alastair, who cut into his and backed the opening with foam. Bill separately fits an additional mast support at the top of the vent box, on the starboard side.[81]

## A snowfall pause, and a comparison against a firsthand New Zealand report

By January 2007 Faith's decks were in place, with sailing hoped for once warmer weather arrived; a fresh fall of snow had brought building to a halt for the moment. Faith was built strictly to plan, though without insulation — Bill chose to sail a season without any first, planning to add foam only if condensation actually proved a problem in practice. He judged Matt Layden had already thought the design through carefully for open-sea integrity and watertightness, and stuck to the original cabin design rather than modifying it.[82][83][84]

## Fibreglassing the hull, and fairing woven roving before painting

Bill fully encapsulated Faith's hull with woven roving and epoxy over "a brilliant week for boatbuilding," and compared fairing methods and build timelines with [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)'s own build to gauge how much time remained before his own targeted early-June 2007 launch. He judged his Mk1-design sail — bought secondhand from Glen Maxwell, the earlier and lower-peaked pattern rather than Glen's later "improved" higher-peaked Mark II — worked well once a persistent yard crease was cured by tightening the end ties; a technical exchange on yard curvature and Matt Layden's own eye for it is at [people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md#matt-laydens-eye-for-yard-curvature-demonstrated-on-another-builders-sail). Bill laid out a firm cruising plan for Faith once launched: the West Country by late June 2007, on to Plymouth and Falmouth by mid-July, and, weather permitting, across to the Isles of Scilly.[85][86][87][88][89][90][91][92][93]

## A yuloh pivot socket, mainsheet snagging, and a cruise plan toward the Scilly Isles

Fresh yuloh-plans photos drew out the choice between the plans' ball-joint pivot and a plain oarlock (the way Matt Layden sculls in a hosted video clip); the ball joint was confirmed as the right choice for Paradox given her hull weight, transom height, and stowage, since yulohing and sculling are different actions. The socket-material question worked through a shortlist — tufnol, copper pipe, stainless tube, graphite-loaded epoxy — before Bill made his own with a longitudinal shaft curve worked in by wedge. The same exchange settled a mainsheet-fouling problem at the yuloh's pivot ball, fixed with a wooden pad atop the pintle bracket. For the fuller technical record see [components/yuloh.md](/components/yuloh.md#ball-joint-versus-oarlock-and-pivot-socket-wear-over-time). With the technical questions settled, Bill firmed up his cruising plan for "Faith" once launched, inviting Alastair to join any part of a West Country cruise toward the Scilly Isles.[94][95][96][97][98][99][100][101][102][103][104][105][106][107][108][109][110][111] A year on, he refined the yuloh further: boring the pivot-pin hole with enough twist to bring the blade to about 50-60 degrees from horizontal by the end of each stroke, and relocating the hole 5.25 in further toward the handle end, gave more power and a more upright working stance, a modification he adopted with good results.[112]

## The cabin trunk roof, and building the hatch

Bill fitted Faith's cabin trunk roof and the hatch runners next, judging a window worth fitting into the hatch since seeing the sail from inside while sailing is otherwise difficult — one technique assembles the hatch first and cuts the window aperture afterward, avoiding the risk of the plywood bending crooked. His own hatch sides were a full metre out of true before bending and glassing corrected them, drawing a dry aside that he had built the hatch triangular rather than rectangular; the runners came from teak scraps sourced at the Beale Boat Show.[113][114][115][116]

## Cutting the polycarbonate windows

With the hatch trial-fitted "more by luck than judgement," Bill turned to shaping Faith's windows. Coarse teeth, run at a slow jigsaw speed, cuts polycarbonate best — a fine blade run fast melts the plastic swarf into a clogging lump. With windows and hatch done, only stowage racks, deck painting and hardware, Coppercoat antifouling (rated for roughly ten years), and topside painting stood between Faith and rigging her for the water.[117][118][119]

Recalling the same forward-beam curve two years later, in reply to a fellow builder puzzling over the identical problem, Bill set out his own method in full: shape the bottom support piece by planing and sanding, then make a stiff cardboard template of the polycarbonate itself to work out the forward curvature the upper beam needs; build the beam's shape by laminating thin plywood layers onto it, lay a single show face of plywood over the front for a clean finish, and bed the polycarbonate in a transparent sealant rather than screwing it down dry. See [deck and cabin beam lamination — bending polycarbonate glazing around the forward and aft window curves](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#bending-polycarbonate-glazing-around-the-forward-and-aft-window-curves).[120]

## Faith's transom camber, and a pine cleat test for Pete Martin

Faith's own transom shows 5 mm of curvature worked into both edges, matching bulkhead 4's specified camber — an improvement to appearance, though a perfectly straight-sided transom remains workable for a builder who prefers it. For interior cleat wood, Bill's field test settles pine against Douglas fir: drive a bronze ring nail into a pre-drilled sample and see whether it splits.[121][122][123]

## Antifouling before a month-long mooring, and the "Enuf" trailer bought secondhand

Faith was coppercoat-antifouled ahead of launch specifically because she might spend long periods on a mooring, with some cruises expected to run over a month. Rather than commission a new trailer, Bill sourced [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s old trailer from his Paradox "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" secondhand.[124]

## Trailer braking, and a firmed-up West Country cruise schedule

A purpose-built trailer avoids brake problems from submersion at launch and recovery, at the cost of extra strength — and so weight — in the trailer itself; the secondhand trailer from Derek Clark had none. With that settled, Faith's West Country cruise plan firmed up further: Exmouth to Dartmouth and back, then Plymouth, the Tamar, and Falmouth to sail the Fal and, weather permitting, on to the Isles of Scilly, with a loose plan to meet [Jeff UK](/people/jeff-doryman.md) and [Alastair](/people/alastair-law.md) around Falmouth/Newlyn/Penzance in mid-July.[125]

## The front window fitted, and the remaining finishing list before launch

By early May 2007 Faith's front window was attached, leaving the back and side windows already in place, with deck painting under way. The remaining finishing list ran: fitting deck fittings and rigging the boat, plus a more substantial bolt through the rudder and its stock. The same week, Faith's hatch was finished with a large polycarbonate window giving a clear view of the sail from inside — chosen with a second purpose in mind, letting sunlight reach a solar panel mounted on the cabin roof.[126][127][128]

## The hull painted, and a launch planned at Burnham-on-Crouch

Within days only the hull and bottom paint stood between Faith and the water. A first launch was planned for Burnham-on-Crouch, hoped for in early June, ahead of sea trials on a cruise to the Walton Backwaters.[129]

## A 22 mm vent trunk drain proven in a brief force 6

Bill used a 22 mm drain tube for the vent trunk, against Matt Layden's 1 in nominal pipe specification, judging 32 mm harder to drill accurately for. The strongest wind he had sailed Faith in by that point was brief periods of force 6, waves never breaking on deck, and even an occasional full vent box let only a minimal amount of water reach the cabin.[139] The vent trunk's whole side panel — rather than only its top half, as built on another Paradox — is removable on Faith and has never leaked; the drain hole under the mast step sits above the waterline, and even in rough conditions, with waves constantly washing over the foredeck and water flowing into the vent box, this has caused no problems.[140] The vent trunk's own top was simply butted and glued to bulkhead 2, with the drain pipe fitted the same way; full technique at [components/rig/mast.md](/components/rig/mast.md#attaching-the-vent-trunk-top-to-bulkhead-2).[141]

## "Faith" is born: off the trailer and onto dry land

Faith's hull left the garage for the front lawn in early June 2007 — "born" at last, in Bill's own description, after nearly two years of building — to await her first entry into the water a few days later at Burnham-on-Crouch. Bill floated the idea of linking up during the coming cruise with the Dinghy Cruising Association's North West Holiday Meeting at St Just in Roseland, running 7–14 July.[142][143]

## "Faith" was launched

**"Faith" was launched** in mid-June 2007, with Bill starting a dedicated cruise/build blog at `bills-log.blogspot.com` to record it. Faith's own log fixes the moment precisely: launched at Burnham Marina, Essex, at about 1900 on 10 June 2007 — "not the most graceful of launches for a maiden event," needing to be jigged off her trailer. A first attempt at the yuloh that evening failed to hold a course against a beam wind, leaving Faith to drift alongside the harbourmaster's launch; filling the water ballast tanks afterward "made the boat much more stable."[144][145]

## Faith afloat at Burnham Marina, and a first sea trial

Bill put Faith through two days of trials to check her handling. On calm water the first day the boat sailed flawlessly, and gave the chance to work out yuloh technique: moving the shaft horizontally and twisting it at the end of each stroke generated power, where a figure-eight, punt-style stroke did not — full write-up at [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#a-figure-eight-stroke-does-not-work-a-horizontal-twist-does). The second day's Force 3-4, gusting 5, brought the real test and a first tacking failure: changing from starboard to port tack, Faith stalled head-to-wind and drifted back, resolved by freeing the sheet to feather the sail — a technique shared with sailing a junk rig. Over-canvassed on purpose to test her stability, Faith heeled to 30 degrees in the gusts without any anxiety about capsizing; sailing with the hatch closed made oilskins unnecessary even in drizzle. Two days of sailing were judged a clear success.[146][147][148][149][150][151][152]

## Rowing thoughts, and the yuloh's limits as a paddle

Standing and facing forward to row with conventional oars was judged impractical, since the cabin top obstructs the stroke on both sides; the yuloh itself can be used as a paddle in a pinch, but only for a short time, since it takes a lot of energy to keep up. See [operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#whether-paradox-can-be-rowed-with-conventional-oars).[153]

## Hints on gluing up the hull, a single two-hour session

Answering a newcomer's worry over how to glue up a Paradox hull without losing symmetry, Faith's own experience gave a full worked method: a good dry fit first, then straps, ropes, clamps and shaped wooden blocks to spring the bow and stern into place, fitting the side panels to bulkhead 3 first, then 2, then 4, then 1 and the transom, and the stem last. Faith's whole hull went together this way in one sitting of about two hours, with a centreline check needing no adjustment. See [construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#gluing-up-the-hull-one-sitting-or-several-and-locating-the-bulkheads-with-temporary-pins) for the full technique.[159]

## A 140-mile cruise eastward from Falmouth, with Little Jim

The long-planned joint West Country cruise toward the Scilly Isles went ahead in mid-July 2007, though the Scillies themselves were not reached: unsuitable weather ruled out the crossing, and Faith and [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) instead sailed **140 miles together eastward from Falmouth**. Sailing alongside Little Jim's owner taught Bill boat-handling technique firsthand — trimming the sail by yuloh and deploying the anchor. The passage from Plymouth to Exmouth was the opening stage of that wider cruise. ## Two days of force 5 off Bolt Head, and the tacking fix confirmed again

The liveliest sailing of the cruise came off **Bolt Head and Bolt Tail**, working into **Salcombe** in roughly a force 5 with a big swell and confused, cliff-bounced seas; both boats handled the conditions impeccably, with no anxiety aboard either one. Based on that experience, Paradox should ride out a force 8 lying a-hull — uncomfortable for the crew, but safe — with anything stronger, especially running downwind, better met tracking slightly off dead downwind with a long warp trailed astern. This firsthand judgment adds a second British-built, British-sailed data point to the lying-a-hull question tracked at [operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#a-british-isles-sailors-case-for-lying-a-hull-and-paradoxs-flat-bottom). The one recurring idiosyncrasy found across the cruise — slow tacking in piled-up seas, fixed by reversing the rudder to pay her off — is likely common to all Paradoxes; apart from that quirk, Faith performed brilliantly, arguably the best boat her owner had ever sailed.[169]

## Drying out on a sunken mooring block during the West Country cruise

During the same cruise, Faith dried out directly on top of a submerged concrete mooring sinker, her bow left about 3 ft in the air. Bill climbed in and waited out the tide; once she was back on her trailer, no damage to the bottom could be found — a concrete demonstration of the confidence a heavily over-built bottom is meant to provide.[170]

## Video evidence: an easily driven hull with almost no wake

New video from the cruise, shot at an estimated Force 2 to 3, showed no bow wave or wake — consistent with an easily driven hull: heavy displacement carries her way well, low wetted area keeps drag down, and with the sail properly balanced the rudder runs almost neutral.[171][172][173][174]

## Reassurance on a wavy chine before the chine logs go on

Answering a newcomer's worry over an unfair, wavy chine noticed during a dry-fit disassembly, the chine can be expected to pull fair once the chine logs go on — fitting them on a very hot day helps considerably, and laminating each chine log from two thinner parts rather than bending it at full dimension makes the job considerably easier.[175] Years afterward, recalling his own difficulty bending and clamping Faith's own chine logs until the epoxy hardened, Bill passed the same advice to a newcomer starting a build in Sweden: laminate the logs in two thinner parts rather than bending one at full thickness, work on a hot, bright day when the epoxy also cures faster and pre-bend the plywood with towels soaked in hot water if needed, provided it is fully dry again before the epoxy goes on.[176]

## Comparing Faith to an earlier Bermudan-sloop dinghy, "Harriott"

Faith invited comparison against an earlier 14 ft Bermudan sloop, "Harriott," a centreboard cruising dinghy with a beam of about 5.5 ft, a displacement of 650 lb, and 96 sq ft of sail — dependent on her centreboard for windward performance, unable to sail efficiently to windward in under 4 ft of water. Faith, by contrast, is a little shorter and narrower (just over 4 ft beam) with only 9 in of draught, sails to windward in 2 ft of water heeling around 14 degrees, and carries a much heavier 1,410 lb displacement on only 100 sq ft of sail. Both boats will self-right, but Faith's enormous stability and lesser sail area make her much the more forgiving of the two. Paradox's advantages over an open cruising dinghy: the crew is never exposed to the elements, there is no centreplate to jam, and there is much more usable stowage space aboard; a Roamer-type dinghy points closer to windward, is faster in light air, can be sailed two-up, and can carry an outboard without fouling the mainsheet. Paradox is better described as a **single-handed miniature yacht** than as a dinghy.[177]

## The Cobnor week: a Dinghy Cruising Association meeting, and falling overboard

In mid-August 2007 Faith joined the Dinghy Cruising Association for a week's meeting in Chichester Harbour, launched at Emsworth Marina on 17 August. On the fourth day, 20 August, trying to anchor off Cobnor beach as the tide turned, wind and current carried her off her intended spot and Bill went over the side. He had forgotten his buoyancy aid, and waterlogged clothing left him "floundering"; he worked his way to the stern and hauled himself aboard using the boarding step built into the rudder stock, losing his cap, a shoe, and his spectacles in the process. The incident later settled his preference for a snug-fitting buoyancy aid over a lifejacket when working the boat. The rest of the week ran less eventfully — a near-gale force 8 was ridden out safely at anchor, and Faith's mast jammed while being unstepped when rainwater draining down the deck ventilation pipe, exactly as designed, swelled the wood at its base. Bill's own verdict from the week: "Paradox can work to windward, but not handily as a Wayfarer or a Mirror dinghy... The boat is very strong and she can take the ground; she can be sealed from the elements to provide relative comfort." ## Gale-bound at Brighton: a petrol-storage routine, and a case for reliability over electric power

During a weather-bound stop at Brighton on the same cruise, Bill set out his own long-practiced petrol-storage routine on the list, replying to a fuel-storage question revived elsewhere on the group: two five-litre cans kept in the forepeak, inspected often for leaks, the cabin always kept clear of fuel fumes, and the stove never lit while fuel is being handled — a regime carried over from earlier boats. He judged a petrol engine more reliable than an electric motor for covering real distances, framing the underlying choice as a matter of personal risk each sailor must weigh and accept for themselves; his own circumstances made the case directly — "There's no way I would be at Brighton without the engine." ## Scilly Isles storage, water, and a battery-maintenance routine

For the kind of cruising the Scilly Isles crossing involved, Faith's storage proved ample: the forepeak was never filled completely, leaving room for the bower anchor in its own purpose-built rack and a spare two-gallon can of drinking water — a spare that in the event went unused, since a similar can fitted with a hand pump in the lazarette let water be topped up at least every second day. Bulkhead #2's ridge into the forepeak — the same ridge Little Jim's owner has already cut away for his own folding bicycle's stowage (see [Little Jim — the forward-stateroom access, cut down for bike stowage](/boats/little-jim.md#the-forward-stateroom-access-cut-down-for-bike-stowage)) — is the one change Faith herself could still use, since checking and topping up the battery, done with a pipette rather than removing the battery itself, is a regular chore through that same forepeak access. After the cruise, plenty of canned food remained in the under-floor stowage, and perishables such as rice, biscuits, cereal, and chocolate, kept in plastic containers under the floor, stayed dry except for a meagre amount of condensation collecting there in extremely cold weather, which was attributed to Faith carrying no foam lining on her hull.[217]

## A laminated hatch beam that delaminated in the ply itself

While cruising in company through the Scilly Isles, a problem surfaced with Faith's hatch: its forward beam, laminated from plywood rather than solid wood, began to delaminate. On inspection, it was the plywood itself coming apart within its own lamination, not the epoxy joints bonding the beam to the hatch structure — a caution for fastening any laminated ply panel with nails or screws through it, rather than relying on the panel's own internal glue lines alone to hold it together over time. See [construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md) for the beam-lamination technique this qualifies, and [construction/fastening-and-gluing.md](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md#fastening-laminated-ply-panels-a-delamination-caution-from-experience) for the general fastening lesson it prompted.[218]

## A waterproof camera case suggested, and why a long lens won't fit

A **waterproof case for an underwater-capable digital camera** need not cost much: one such case, made for a Fujifilm compact and bought on eBay, cost under £20 including postage, worked to a depth of 10 m, and is available in sizes to fit most compact camera models.[222][223] A camera with a **10x optical zoom**, however, does not fit inside a compact-camera case at all — it protrudes too far in front, the trade-off for a lens that can fill the frame with a distant subject shot from the boat itself. **Cameras, like boats, are always a compromise.**[224]

## Refuelling practice underway, and a name's double meaning

The refuelling photographs (see [auxiliary outboard power — a side-mounted motor-and-fuel sponson considered and rejected](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-side-mounted-motor-and-fuel-sponson-considered-and-rejected-a-three-position-transom-bracket)) drew a wordplay reply worth recording in its own right: "Faith serves well."[225] Bill's answer took the phrase two ways at once. Going without a safety harness while refuelling was one of several risks he had knowingly accepted over the years — a harness gets in the way and can cause its own trouble, so he preferred free movement except in conditions severe enough that he would not be refuelling at all; running the engine in gear while it idled, he had found, made the boat noticeably steadier for the job. He closed by admitting he might one day take a risk too far, crediting "Faith" — the boat and, in the same breath, his own religious faith — with having kept him safe so far: "as Steve points out, 'Faith' has been on my side, or rather shall I say, my God. May He bless and guard you too."[226]

## A tide-and-wind case for the outboard, on the Blackwater

Replying to a suggestion elsewhere on the list that he give up his engine for sail and yuloh alone, Bill set out a concrete case for keeping it, drawn from an outing on the **Blackwater** estuary days before: with the wind out of the east at force 4 and the flood tide running the same way, he wanted to make two miles to windward as far as **Bradwell Power Station**, then run back to circumnavigate **Osea Island** before returning to his mooring, east of the island, by high water. The engine made the whole plan possible and pleasurable; without it, he judged, sailing would have been out of the question, since within the time his drying mooring allowed him he could not have left at all — wind and tide together would have carried him west toward Maldon, where he would have had to wait out the flood before the ebb could bring him back against the wind, quite possibly too late to reach his mooring before it dried. More generally, he judged handling Paradox among moored boats or in open water once the wind reaches force 3 or more calls for an engine, a yuloh being "virtually useless" in that kind of close-quarters work. He restated the safety value of carrying an engine at all, pointing to the West Country cruise's own precedent that a Paradox fitted with one can even tow another, as Faith and Little Jim had already done for each other between Fowey and Plymouth (see [above](#to-the-scilly-isles-and-back-with-little-jim)). A follow-up asking how much fuel the outing took drew a direct answer: no precise check was made back on the mooring, but the engine ran about an hour in total, burning at most half a litre — a rate Bill worked out at roughly six nautical miles to the litre, so that a 5-litre can is good for about 30 miles and his usual two 5-litre cans, some 60 miles, in calm conditions. Given the choice, he took petrol over an electric motor without qualification — power, speed, and duration all favour it — and argued a petrol outboard turns Paradox into a genuine motor-sailer able to convert instantly into a motorboat, capable of exploring the canals of Europe wherever petrol is on hand, an ambition he would not attempt relying on a yuloh alone. On the yuloh itself he was blunter still: useful on a Paradox only in near-calm conditions, since working it in a force 2 or better takes real effort just to hold a course, and even keeping the rudder raised — a tip that helps but does not fully solve the problem — still leaves the bow fighting to be blown off to one side or the other; he suspected Sven Yrvind's own boats fare better sculled in a breeze thanks to a forward drop board giving the sculler something to brace against, a feature Faith and Little Jim both lack. Glen Maxwell endorsed the whole case without qualification.[227][228][229][230] The exchange closed with a simple thank-you for fuel figures that turned out more reassuring than expected.[231] A fellow builder's own account of the same stretch adds a specific reason the learning curve ran as long as it did: despite decades of general sailing experience before Paradox, the engine got reached for every time conditions turned hard to windward in tight quarters, leaving fewer chances to build sail- and yuloh-handling skill in precisely the conditions that would have taught it fastest. See [Little Jim — a deliberate no-motor choice, and the case for leaving it at home](/boats/little-jim.md#a-deliberate-no-motor-choice-and-the-case-for-leaving-it-at-home).[232]

## An outboard-bracket depth compared against Whisper

Fitting an outboard to Faith at all took months of dithering over where to put it. A starboard-side Honda 2.3 mount, considered since spring 2008, was ruled out as impracticable given the engine's vulnerability to submersion when heeled or motor-sailing; a sponson-style fuel-and-motor extension raised its own stowage problem when not in use. The transom won out instead, protected from swamping, the sheet fouling the motor only rarely, when the motor is raised to its highest position with the shaft near horizontal. A three-position bracket (high, medium, low) followed: in the high position, with the engine vertical, only the skeg's tip touches the water, so there is no appreciable drag and the sheet runs clear. Petrol storage of two 5-litre cans at the forward end of the cabin gave no trouble either, the cooking stove never lit while refuelling; the throttle proved reachable with a bit of a stretch standing in the cabin, and refuelling manageable from inside except for judging the fuel level, which still meant stepping onto the rear deck. See [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md: a side-mounted motor-and-fuel sponson considered and rejected](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-side-mounted-motor-and-fuel-sponson-considered-and-rejected-a-three-position-transom-bracket) for the fuller comparison against another builder's side-mount sketch that prompted it.[233]

Comparing against a photograph of a similar Honda 2.3 installation on "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)," Bill found his own bracket on Faith mounted slightly higher, letting the engine run fully down in about 15 in of water, always tilted up before beaching. The same message restated the case above for carrying an engine at all against the East Coast's fierce tides, and judged a four-stroke such as his Honda 2.3 — burning no oil — leaves smells almost non-existent. A fellow builder's question about the bracket's exact make, any transom reinforcement needed, and the engine's shaft length was answered in full days later: the bracket clicks into three positions with the outboard vertical, each giving a different propeller depth — the deepest avoided when starting, since back-pressure fights the exhaust, and used underway only in rough seas to keep the propeller submerged, with the middle setting the usual choice and some cavitation showing at the shallowest, highest setting — with no transom reinforcement needed and the engine confirmed as the standard, not long-shaft, Honda. See [operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md — an outboard bracket reused across ownership, and a Honda 2.3's real-world thrust](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#an-outboard-bracket-reused-across-ownership-and-a-honda-23s-real-world-thrust).[234][235]

## A topped-up boom's other uses: readiness, yuloh clearance, and a rain awning

Beyond furling mechanics, Bill found several further reasons to keep the boom topped up. Running the engine, he sometimes topped up the furled sail to keep it clear of his head, leaving the boat ready for instant hoisting whenever he wanted to proceed under sail; he also found the yuloh usable while the furled sail was topped up in this way. A tent or awning could be deployed from a topped-up boom, particularly useful when at anchor during a rain storm. See [Furling system — a second use for the topping lift](/components/rig/furling.md#a-second-use-for-the-topping-lift-readiness-yuloh-clearance-and-a-rain-awning) for the wider exchange this answered, including an alternative sun-and-rain fix — a hatch-mounted umbrella — that needs no topping lift at all.[237] A splayed topping lift alone, with no separate support, has also occasionally supported the furled sail when stowed, with a reference photograph on file showing her rigged this way at Exmouth during the South Coast cruise to the Scilly Isles.[238]

## Rig hardware: the tack strop, the boom tang and the gooseneck

A handful of small rig fittings each earned their own fix over Faith's build and first two seasons. Her tack arrangement's spacer, a split "Y" shape wood-capped and epoxy-sheathed, proved itself over two seasons of sailing: the same spacer Bill carried a spare of, alongside a second halyard on a separate external block, in the offshore tool box and bosun's chest he judged essential after crewing a Force 8 gale on someone else's yacht. Full account at [heavy-weather tactics: a Force 8 in the Bay of Biscay](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#a-force-8-in-the-bay-of-biscay-a-worked-comparison-and-a-tool-box-lesson).[239] At the boom's forward end, the wire-bridle mainsheet's own indirect forward loading helps keep the boom seated on its tack bearing even without the small retaining screw for the hooked "gooseneck," a screw that worked loose on his own boat, after which he stopped relying on it at all, since the sheet's forward thrust holds the boom in place regardless.[240] At the boom's after end, the tang's retaining-bolt hole started splitting in June 2008; setting the bolt directly into epoxy filler, with no thread tapped, gave no further trouble. See [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling: boom-end splitting at the mainsheet-tang bolt](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#boom-end-splitting-at-the-mainsheet-tang-bolt-and-a-construction-fix) for the fuller comparison against the fixes other builders tried on the same fitting.[241]

## Everyday handling: beaching, anchoring and a wet-weather note

A handful of routine practices, none large enough for their own chapter, rounded out how Faith was actually lived aboard. Bill deliberately beached her stern-first at times, to angle the cabin sole so his head lay higher than his feet for a comfortable night's sleep; the technique carries a real wear risk of its own, and the general mechanics and the better practice for it are set out at [components/rudder.md: beaching stern-first](/components/rudder.md#beaching-stern-first-a-sleeping-comfort-technique-and-why-it-doesnt-damage-the-rudder).[242] Laying and retrieving the anchor could be done entirely from the cabin, feeding the rode from a bucket kept to starboard; see [ground tackle and anchoring: a cabin-fed bucket routine](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-cabin-fed-bucket-routine-and-treating-any-system-as-optional) for the full method, which he treated as one option among several rather than a fixed rule.[243] Sailing Faith in genuinely cold April conditions brought on real window condensation, and confirmed the plans' own drip tray, built as drawn, worked well; see [window glazing: cabin condensation](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#cabin-condensation-a-drip-rail-a-moulded-gutter-and-window-drain-holes) for the fuller comparison against an epoxy-gutter alternative.[244]

## Appraising Faith: dimensions, ballast, and handling, in retrospect

Fully ballasted, Faith displaced 1,410 lb — 397 lb of lead and 154 lb of water — on a hull just over 4 ft in beam; her shallow 9 in draught let her sail to windward in 2 ft of water, and, despite a flat and heavily rockered bottom, she was self-righting and would sit upright unsupported on a beach. The build itself ran from the plywood's delivery in August 2005 to her launch in June 2007 — some twenty-two months, not the "year and a half" Bill's own retrospective accounts sometimes gave. Running downwind from Salcombe toward Brixham she "hardly rolled... because of her flat bottom and chine runners," and her stowage struck Bill as offering "enough stowage space for an ocean crossing."[247]

Bill's rewritten and illustrated account of Faith's cruising, "Faith's Cruise," was made available on CD to anyone donating toward the upkeep of the Small Sailboats web site (`www.smallsailboats.co.uk/cd/cd.htm`).[248] He also floated sailing Faith to Holland the following year to explore its inland waterways,[248] and, taking an interest in Noli and the stretched Enigma 460 prototype then both nearing completion (see [boats/noli.md](/boats/noli.md) and [Enigma 460 — a longer, plywood-cabin variant](/matt-layden/enigma.md#enigma-460-a-longer-plywood-cabin-variant-2007)), proposed the UK's Paradox-family builders arrange a get-together in 2009 to see the boats on the water together.[248]

## A two-season retrospective: antifouling, a condensation fix confirmed, and nothing he would change

Congratulating a fellow builder nearing completion of his own Puget Sound Paradox,[249] Bill drew a reciprocal question in return — how had Faith held up, and would he do anything differently building again[250] — and answered with a fuller two-season retrospective. Maintenance had proved a "doddle": beyond a fresh coat of mast varnish, nothing had needed attention, and even the tack strop was hardly worn. His copper/epoxy-based leaching antifouling had kept Faith's bottom entirely free of growth through a full season of near-constant seawater exposure between April and September. The condensation question left open when Faith first sailed without foam insulation (see [above](#a-snowfall-pause-and-a-comparison-against-a-firsthand-new-zealand-report)) was settled by two winters' experience: only in exceptionally cold conditions did any condensation collect, and even then no more than about half a teacup over 24 hours — the worst of it at Sovereign Yacht Haven with the deck coated in snow and ice — with the drip tray around the base of the windows proving equal to the job. The lift-and-move floorboard technique adopted in place of the plans' hinges (see [above](#a-removable-floorboard-technique-for-full-storage-and-ballast-access)) was reaffirmed as the better system two seasons on. Asked what he would change building again, Bill's answer was nothing: sailing from inside, fully protected from the weather, remained "tops" for him, Faith proved superb for beaching and sailing in very shallow water, and her stability and self-righting gave a settled feeling of security; she held her course well enough, sheeted correctly, that the Autohelm aboard was seldom used. He closed by restating the name's double meaning — he had named her "Faith," and had faith in her that she would look after him.[251]

Bill's own Autohelm rig, engaging the tiller lines through a pair of rolling hitches, is set out step by step at [self-steering — a second rolling-hitch autohelm rig, engaged and disengaged step by step](/operations/self-steering.md#a-second-rolling-hitch-autohelm-rig-engaged-and-disengaged-step-by-step).[252] Eight photographs of the finished setup were later posted to a "Faith's Autohelm" album in the smallsailboats group's own photo section, fulfilling an earlier promise to document it.[253]

## A December 2008 restatement: no dissatisfied owners, and an engine added for the year's Scilly cruise

Answering a December 2008 call for owner assessments, Bill stated he had never heard of a Paradox owner dissatisfied with the boat — boats had been sold over the years, but never for that reason. He stood by every word of his own published appraisal (above), adding only that he had since fitted an outboard engine to Faith for that year's cruise along the South Coast of England to the Isles of Scilly; Paradox behaved extremely well throughout and more than met his expectations. Of the many boats he had owned over the years, Faith remained the most comfortable and the driest. His closing advice to anyone wanting a Paradox of their own: be ready to put in sustained, dedicated work building one, or find one to buy at the right price, since so few come up for sale.[254]

## The mechanics of living aboard: anchors, mattress, table, and forepeak access (December 2008)

Answering the same December 2008 cluster of living-aboard questions that drew Al Law's own account on "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" (see [Little Jim — a newcomer's cluster of living-aboard questions](/boats/little-jim.md#answering-a-first-timers-mast-stepping-question-and-a-newcomers-cluster-of-living-aboard-questions-december-2008)), Bill gave Faith's own parallel practice. The bower anchor rode in a frame on the starboard side near the forepeak entrance; the kedge anchor stayed in the boat's own loo bucket together with its chain and warp, the bucket tied to the bower anchor's shaft so it was ready for immediate deployment — see [ground tackle and anchoring](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-new-owners-stowage-question-and-two-more-anchoring-routines) for the fuller comparison against other owners' anchor stowage. A good-quality self-inflating mattress, sized to about two-thirds of the cabin's width rather than the full length, left the floor lockers reachable without moving it, and a removable table fitted across the cabin on the side bins served for meals and for a laptop. Faith herself proved very comfortable over a two-month cruise along the South Coast of England.

Bill judged Paradox unsuited to an overweight or otherwise large sailor, or to anyone who cannot kneel or bend easily: reaching into the forepeak, and especially extracting the battery, called for near-"Houdini" flexibility. His recommended fix, already adopted on "Little Jim," was to eliminate bulkhead 2's raised edge; reducing or removing the forepeak's central division altogether was a further option. [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" took a related approach, widening the forepeak entrance itself beyond the plans' own dimension by eliminating most of the starboard support. See [interior bins and floor — easing access](/components/interior.md#easing-access-eliminating-bulkhead-2s-raised-edge-and-a-wider-entrance-on-enuf) for the fuller comparison of both fixes.[255]

## Sold in 2009: the forepeak reworked, a higher-peaked sail, and the yuloh reshaped

Faith was sold in 2009 — Bill's own account of it characteristically brief: "I sold my Paradox 'Faith' to the very first person who offered to take her unto himself," with a "pang of guilt" afterward. Looking back on her a few years later, he judged her perhaps the best boat he had ever owned: a trifle small, but very well thought out for ease of working, steering from inside a snug, watertight cabin a huge bonus; the ventilation could sometimes overpower the cabin, but a towel stuffed into the incoming vent was enough to keep it under control.[258] By May 2013 her owner was [Jim Hall](/people/james-hall.md), "an accomplished sailor who [had] built a 21 ft yacht and sailed her by himself to far away places." Jim Hall had by then removed the forepeak's partition and widened its entrance, added a drinking-water tank forward, and improved access to the battery; moved the mainsheet lead forward onto the stern deck to stop it snagging the outboard; and fitted a new, higher-peaked sail — "the biggest improvement by far," which "transformed the boat so that she sails better to windward and tacks more positively," to the point that the engine was rarely needed. He had also reshaped the yuloh, giving it more twist, so the boat now had three independent means of propulsion. By 2014 Jim Hall "makes good use of her" and had recently had her repainted, closing out Faith's record in this bundle as a boat still actively sailed, close on a decade after her launch.[259][260][261][262]

## A cracked trailer axle, an indispension-unit critique, and a rebuild from a scrapped van (July 2011)

Checking the boat over ahead of a planned week's sailing turned up a serious longitudinal crack at both ends of the trailer's axle beam — a hollow galvanised square tube linking a pair of independent "indispension" suspension units — most likely caused by torque under the boat's own weight; the trailer, rated at 750 kg, was loaded close to that limit, and a welded steel-plate repair was judged only a stopgap, since the thin metal and burnt galvanising at the weld would soon start corroding. The "indispension" units themselves gave almost no springing at all: jacking the trailer frame and releasing it sharply produced barely any movement in the suspension arm, leaving a ride harsh enough to scatter the boat's own contents "as if a bomb has gone off," and judged likely to have contributed to the axle failure in the first place — their only real merit a quick, convenient bolt-on trailer build, "dreadful" in actual road use. A replacement trailer was begun instead, built around a rear axle salvaged from a scrapped van: dropped below the hubs for a lower load height, rated for a ton so amply strong enough, and expected to give a softer ride — though the van's own spring spacing proved narrower than Paradox's beam, so the hull would not sit directly between the springs. Open questions carried to the mailing list: whether rollers or slippery-plastic-covered bunks work better for sliding the boat on and off without dunking the wheels and brakes, and whether a hinged, break-back trailer bed is worth building. The fuller trailer-design discussion this opened is covered at [operations/trailering.md — Faith's trailer failure, and a rebuild from a scrapped van axle](/operations/trailering.md#faiths-trailer-failure-and-a-rebuild-from-a-scrapped-van-axle-july-2011).[272]

A strength test was also carried out on the rudder itself, anticipating that unloading stern-first onto dry land might leave the rudder briefly supporting the boat's own rear weight while the bow was slid clear of the trailer: jacked up to simulate the load, the rudder creaked slightly and rode up a little on its pintles, but held.[272]

The same indispension units were also found to have visibly lowered the trailer and grown a noticeably harsher ride as they aged — a guess offered that they are manufactured cheaply, probably somewhere overseas, with quality and durability a lower priority than cost.[275]

Weighed on a public weighbridge out of curiosity, the loaded trailer — food and basic gear aboard, but no water in the tanks — came out at 760 kg, just over the UK's unbraked-trailer legal limit; heavy gear is now loaded only after arriving at the slip, to stay legal on the road. A search of other Indispension-unit owners' own experience online turned up widespread reports of the same suspension trouble, suggesting the manufacturer's advertised load ratings are optimistic. The replacement trailer under way will run leaf-spring suspension and brakes that work in both directions, forward and reverse.[276]

## No foam flotation as built, and an improvised buoyancy solution (February 2013)

Faith was built without foam flotation at all — Bill Serjeant's own choice while building her, made for the sake of the storage and living space it would otherwise have cost. Her second owner has since improvised his own emergency buoyancy instead: the forward compartment, less useful for gear storage since it is awkward to reach, is stuffed with empty plastic screw-top bottles, enough that the bow at least should float if the worst happened; the stern end carries hollow plastic fenders, a part-full two-gallon water can, a home-made portable toilet, and snap-lidded dry-food containers, giving variable buoyancy there depending on how much food and water happen to be aboard at the time. Heavy gear accumulated under the cabin sole over the years — a 15 kg take-apart storm anchor and 15 metres of galvanised chain, tools, spare parts, a Honda outboard, and a large battery — has made the boat progressively heavier despite a stated wish to stop adding things. A manual bilge pump is fitted under the cabin sole as a result. See [plywood grade and foam flotation — a February 2013 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-february-2013-recurrence-calculating-how-much-emergency-buoyancy-is-needed) for the general buoyancy-calculation exchange this prompted.[288]

## An outboard and mainsheet lead restated in a wider list thread (April 2013)

Replying to a newcomer's outboard-sizing question in a wider list thread not specific to Faith, James Hall gave his own boat's outboard as a **2.3 hp Honda**, judging it more than enough for a Paradox and preferring as light an engine as possible, since extra weight aft is undesirable. He described having altered the mainsheet's lead to run through the aft deck rather than the transom — the fix floated as an open question decades earlier (see [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#mainsheet-fouling-on-the-rudder-and-rear-deck-with-an-outboard-fitted)) — though the sheet can still tangle around the engine if attention lapses, "as Alastair can attest," a reference to [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) witnessing the problem firsthand.[289]

The same reply drew a skeptical question about a fellow correspondent's battery-and-solar proposal in turn: doubtful what all the proposed electricity would actually power, noting batteries are expensive and short-lived without careful charging discipline, and less environmentally friendly than marketed given the toxic materials involved in their manufacture and disposal; judged a foredeck solar panel workable only if tough enough to stand on, since that space is needed underfoot when raising the mast, and noted that even a removable panel would waste space against the foredeck's triangular shape; agreed a trolling motor topped up by a generator is a clumsy solution, adding an engine's complexity without an engine's convenience. The easiest method of auxiliary propulsion after the yuloh, in his own view, remains a small outboard: having just returned from a week's sailing trip aboard Faith, he had used only **one litre of petrol** the whole week, helped along by favourable wind and no prolonged calm.[290]

He closed the exchange with a hull-speed argument against fitting anything larger than 2-3 hp at all: a Paradox cannot exceed hull speed regardless of how much power is applied, so a bigger engine only adds unwanted weight where it is least wanted and burns more fuel for no real gain.[291]

## A new sail transforms the boat, and firsthand confirmation of the old sail's link to Zoë (May 2013)

Before her new sail, Faith had been very slow to tack, uncertain going from port to starboard, and made almost no progress to windward on port tack at all — "pretty well dead in the water" — needing liberal use of the engine whenever the destination lay upwind. That old sail was, in her second owner's own words, the very first sail used aboard "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" — a firsthand confirmation of [Bill Serjeant's own account](#a-hoped-for-gathering-of-three-a-sails-earlier-life-on-zoë-and-a-west-wight-potter-compared-august-2012) of the same handoff the previous August. With the new sail fitted, "all is sweetness and light," and performance is transformed — worth investing in a good, well-shaped sail, since a bad one "will kill the boat and disappoint." The new sail itself was made by a UK sailmaker who mostly followed the plans' own sail-making instructions from the paradoxbuilders files section, adding a few personal touches of his own from long experience cutting unusual and traditional sail shapes.[292][293]
