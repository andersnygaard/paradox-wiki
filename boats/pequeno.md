# Pequeno

**"Pequeno"** is a [Paradox](/paradox.md) built by **Alan Ferris** in **Meaford, Ontario, Canada**, and launched in 2014 under the name **"Al's Boat."**[1][2] She later passed to **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)** of Saguenay, Québec, who renamed her Pequeno and keeps her as his second Paradox alongside **[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)** (renamed **Iota**) — lending her out to sailing friends so he always has company on the water.[1][3]

## In the shop, awaiting a refit (January 2017)

As of early 2017 Pequeno was in the shop, needing her whole interior redone along with a new tiller and mast adjustments, and had not yet been sailed by Bourbeau personally. Beyond continuing to lend her to friends, he was weighing shipping her to Europe for a trip there, or eventually selling her if he takes up designing and building a two-person cruiser of his own.[4]

By mid-2018 he had also corrected a non-standard departure from the plans found on the boat as bought: her rear cabin had been left open rather than closed with the designed fixed panel, which he found too hard to keep genuinely waterproof and weakening to the cabin structure, so he closed it up and rebuilt her to the stock design.[5]

## Refit completed, and launched (June 2018)

The rear-cabin rebuild above was part of a full winter's work completing the previously-unfinished hull — bought "everything but the shell" in 2017 — and Pequeño was christened on the Saguenay river that June, bringing the owner to two Paradoxes in active use alongside Iota; having just returned from two consecutive ten-day trips with friends aboard the pair of Gorfniks, he set a 250 km circumnavigation of nearby Lake St-Jean as the new boat's first outing, pending a willing partner.[6] She launched the following day in a 20-knot wind and sailed well from the start. As on Iota, no internal water tanks were fitted; permanent brick ballast, wedged in with rubber mats, was preferred instead, letting the boat go from trailer to sailing in about eight minutes with only a ratchet strap and a winch hook to release, and making the mast easier to step with the hull already afloat. Two additional 16 in square below-floor compartments were built in ahead of the two larger stock ones, more easily reached without leaving the seat, kept partly filled with water bottles or canned goods to make up the ballast they would otherwise cost when empty; the starboard cubbies forward of the central bulwark were left out entirely to keep that side clear for sleeping, where there is more room for the feet. Because brick ballast is not neutrally buoyant like water, extra flotation went in to compensate — foam glued to every wall and ceiling, doubled in places, and packed into most of the forward hatch.[7]

The launch drew a warm reception from the wider Paradox community: a fellow owner who fills his own boat's stock water tanks at home and tows them full, emptying only before retrieval and sometimes not even then, agreed permanent ballast is the simpler course — the tanks were designed with a Bahamas passage in mind, a kind of sailing he does not do, and fresh water is abundant regardless where he sails; he also allowed the extra storage compartments a genuinely good idea, though not one that solves the everyday hassle of making up and stowing the bed with the hatch closed.[8] The same owner, welcoming the idea of the Lake St-Jean trip, remarked that sailing in company with a similarly-sized, like-minded boat beats rafting up to much larger keelers, however friendly the welcome.[9] Answering that bed-stowage hassle directly, Bourbeau offered his own solution worked out over years of cruising both boats: an Exped inflatable sleeping mat kept permanently inflated, shoved bundled into the forward compartment and folded to wedge into the state-room in about 15 seconds each way. Full technique: [components/interior.md](/components/interior.md#a-sewn-foam-and-vinyl-sleeping-pad).[10]

The planned circumnavigation was in fact carried out: by January 2021 Bourbeau reported having sailed it with a friend the year before, six days to complete the roughly 250 km route around **Lac St-Jean**.[11]
