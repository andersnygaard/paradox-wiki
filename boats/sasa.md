# SaSa

**"SaSa"** is [Stephen Barwell](/people/stephen-barwell.md)'s [Paradox](/paradox.md), under construction on North Stradbroke Island, Queensland, Australia since 9 June 2013 — his first woodworking project since a school shoe rack, made 47 years earlier and long since discarded.[1] By early August 2013 the transom had been given its curve (a boomkin still to be built) and work was under way on a self-steering tiller arrangement.[1] Her overall length was later confirmed at **14 ft 2 in**, close to stock size.[9]

## A double-tiller self-steering system

SaSa is being built with two tillers rather than one: the usual below-deck tiller for interior steering, plus a second tiller above the aft deck.[1][2][3] The above-deck tiller is meant to carry a bungy-cord or surgical-rubber sheet-to-tiller self-steering rig, in a method credited to the early sailor Captain Voss.[3] It was made **withdrawable**, so the aft deck — useful at anchor for stowing gear overnight — stays clear when the tiller is not in use.[6] Asked about the mainsheet fouling an above-deck tiller, Barwell reasoned the ropes to the boom could be led clear with a boomkin, recalling — unconfirmed, by his own account — that an Argentine-built Paradox carries one for mounting an outboard motor.[5] For the wider debate among other builders over whether an above-deck tiller is worth having at all, see [components/steering.md — tiller extensions](/components/steering.md#tiller-extensions-steering-with-the-legs-or-from-the-aft-deck).

## Water ballast: bladders weighed against the plans' unbaffled tanks

The plans' water-ballast holds have no internal baffles, so a partial load of loose water — on the order of 70 lb — sloshes audibly from side to side underway and at anchor, loud enough to disturb sleep. Flexible bladders, fitted with their own taps and able to conform to the hold's shape, were weighed as a quieter alternative that also spreads the risk of contamination or failure across several containers rather than one.[3][4] For other builders' own water-ballast and portable-container solutions, see [design/weight-and-ballast.md — ballast placement, and built-in tanks versus separate portable containers](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered).

## A proposed reclining aft-deck seat

A further aft-deck idea: a two-board seat set at deck level in the sliding hatch opening, to recline facing backward with feet on the aft deck (the hinged or slotted washboard withdrawn), for reading or fishing at anchor.[7]

## A first build's learning curve

Barwell judged a first build to run to roughly a thousand separate tasks, each needing about four attempts to get right — the bulkheads alone took "days 1, 2, 3... and four (bonus!)" to draw correctly.[4] Building a hull with no keel, centreboard, or leeboards drew open local curiosity: bystanders judged the shape "more like a billy cart than a boat," steered "with ropes like a billy cart," and pronounced the whole undertaking "JPN" (just plain nuts).[4]

## A finished mast, weighed

Barwell's finished mast weighed **8 kg**, prompting a cross-owner comparison against heavier examples from Lake Havasu, Arizona and two UK builds; see [mast — finished mast weights compared](/components/rig/mast.md#finished-mast-weights-compared) for the fuller comparison.[8]

## The cabin structure solved, and steaming replaced with brute force

Building began within days of [Gunther Battista](/people/gunther-battista.md)'s own "[Manatee](/boats/manatee.md)" in 2013, though SaSa's build had by February 2014 fallen well behind. The hull itself proved fairly straightforward to build; subtleties calling for steamed bends were instead solved with brute force. The Paradox cabin, or coach-house, structure took a week and a half of thought to work out — and the result, to relief, ended up looking recognizably like the Paradox's own cabin structure.[10]

## Half-built by 2016, and a vote of confidence in the design's self-righting

Two years on, the build was described as roughly half finished. Replying to a newcomer's questions about improving a Paradox's capsize recovery, Barwell — by then describing himself as a "microcruising enthusiast" who "can even spell Sven Yrvind" — pointed to video of a Paradox self-righting in far-northern conditions as particularly convincing, judging the only real danger of a capsize in one of these boats to be whiplash on the way back upright.[11]
