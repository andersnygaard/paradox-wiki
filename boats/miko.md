# Miko

**"Miko"** is the [Paradox](/paradox.md) that **[Tony Barlow](/people/tony-barlow.md)** built and launched at **Phuket, Thailand**, in **March 2024** — the archive's only recorded build in tropical Asia, and a boat fitted from the start for the conditions she works in: persistent light airs, heat, and thunderstorms.[1] She was launched the day after the build was announced complete, sailed briefly in almost no wind, and motored to her new mooring ball.[1]

## The build (2022)

The build is on record from at least **June 2022**, under the mailing-list account `tony1mind@...` (see [Tony Barlow — disambiguation](/people/tony-barlow.md#disambiguation) for the identification). Assembling the hull, Barlow sought help interpreting Don Elliott's rudder-shaping instructions — the leading edge's radius, the "series of flats" the blade is meant to be worked into, and the "peaks" the manual's own template is meant to find — none of which clicked from the text and illustration alone. One reply recommended matching a NACA airfoil section from a published catalogue and using it as a template for full-size patterns or gauges; another, from the owner of Don Elliott's own former Paradox, reported the stock rudder comes out in practice "basically a flat blade" rather than a dynamically shaped one — general technique at [rudder — foil section](/components/rudder.md#foil-section-not-a-standard-naca-airfoil). Thanking them, Barlow admitted he was "not sure why" he had been "overly concerned about making a perfect rudder."[11][12][13][14] A fortnight later, with the bottom about to go on and the hull grown too heavy to turn alone, he asked the group for tips — straps, or some kind of leverage[15] — and had an answer the same day: a simple frame lets the job be done entirely single-handed, demonstrated on video by one experienced builder and pointed to again by a second.[16][17] The general point that turning the hull can already need two people even before the bottom adds its own weight is established elsewhere in the archive; see [hull assembly sequence — turning the hull over](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#turning-the-hull-over).

Four days later, still working on the bottom, he raised a first-time electrical question — a battery, a fuse box, switches, lighting, and solar, not knowing where to start.[18] It drew a small portable solar generator suggestion, a full equipment checklist from the owner of a fully powered, instrumented boat, and a fellow owner's own switch-panel layout aboard his own Paradox; general technique from the exchange is gathered at [radio and electronics](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md).[19][20][21] He thanked the group for detail that was "just the kind of thing" he needed, expecting to come back with further questions.[22]

## Trim at launch

The launch exposed one immediate imbalance. With the **battery and outboard both on the port side**, Miko sat leaning to port, and Barlow's first job afloat was to add **lead or other weight to starboard** to bring her upright — a practical reminder that on a hull this narrow the [ballast](/components/ballast.md) budget has to account for the systems as well as the plans' pigs.[1]

## Power: an electric installation, then two outboards

Even before the hull was finished, Barlow had settled on going electric: in July 2022, while fitting the bottom, he named "Photo Albums-Whisper" directly as his model — [Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)'s own transom-mounted outboard bracket — and planned a motor of his own the same way, possibly electric, with extra reinforcement in the transom.[23]

A year before launch Barlow mocked up an **electric trolling-motor drive** copied from another owner's boat, and documented it in enough detail to rebuild: the motor's **shaft cut down** (following the YouTube method), the **control head relocated under the tiller** with its wiring run through a hose in a hole in the transom, and the **outboard bracket modified** — springs removed and two of the arms reversed — so it lifts and drops smoothly. Power comes from a **100 Ah lithium battery** housed between **bulkhead 4 and the transom**. He installed it explicitly as a provisional arrangement: the plan was "to sail as much as possible," and if his sailing and yuloh skills came on far enough, the motor would come off again.[2] The same photographs introduced a **stainless-steel rudder gudgeon** — his epoxy casting had come out full of bubbles — and an **alternative to the sliding hatch** that frees deck area for more solar panels.[2]

In practice the light Phuket airs won. By 2025 she carried **two petrol outboards**, a working motor and a back-up for longer trips, which Barlow posted with an apology to "sailing purists" and the note that he "would sail the entire way if I could"; the cheaper **Parsun** motors he rates as a Yamaha copy.[3] A **blue tube led to the outboard** lets him put it in and out of gear more easily from the helm.[4] See [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md).

## Fitted for the tropics

Miko carries **air conditioning** — a Cybertake S1 Pro drawing about **100 W in eco mode** — which is unique in the archive and entirely rational at 8° north.[5] Barlow has also worked on a **seat that stays comfortable with the boat heeled**, putting his attempt to the group for better ideas, and rigged a **winch setup** for recovery at a ramp that looks like open beach but has a **concrete slip** running under the sand.[6][7] During the build he asked the group for photographs of how others fixed the plans' **lightning grounding plate** — a 75 mm × 1500 mm strip — and routed the associated mast wiring, a question the archive had not previously recorded from a builder in a lightning-prone climate; cross-posted to the mailing list the same day, it finally drew an answer, and by the next day Barlow said he was leaning toward leaving the grounding system off Miko entirely — see [lightning protection — skipping the system entirely](/design/lightning-protection.md#skipping-the-system-entirely-and-a-jury-rigged-grounding-chain) for the full exchange.[8][24]

He tested the [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md) on a **mock-up mast in the garden** before committing it to the boat, working from a method demonstrated on the smallwoodenboats YouTube channel and reporting that it worked really well; [Nickolas Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md) added the working technique from his own years with the gear — lift the sail on the halyard, furl a little, lift again, and repeat, which furls easily enough except in strong winds under way.[9]

She sails out of Phuket, where Barlow also records the free anchoring spots and the day-to-day of keeping a small boat in the tropics.[10]
