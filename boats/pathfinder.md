# Pathfinder

**"Pathfinder"** is the [Paradox](/paradox.md) that **[Benjamin Bloss](/people/benjamin-bloss.md)** is building **north of Seattle, Washington**, on **Puget Sound**. Plans came from **Dave Bolduc** in September 2021, the first cuts were made in **December 2021**, and she is **not finished**: as of the last recorded state, in 2025, the hull is assembled — bulkheads, transom, stem, side panels, sheer logs and chine logs — but the **bottom has not yet gone on**.[1][2][3] She is being built essentially **to plan**, with the departures confined to materials and to the order and method of work, and she is the archive's best-documented **unfinished** build: alongside his posts to the [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md), Bloss has kept a **[video build log](/references/sailing-pathfinder-videos.md)** since before the first cut.[4]

Bloss chose the design for the cruising ground rather than the other way round. He had looked at Paradox more than a decade earlier and built a Stevenson Weekender instead, because that design came with far more support for a first-time builder; returning to it for Puget Sound, he wanted a boat that was **single-handable** and closed enough for **extended-season cruising in cold water** — the enclosed cabin being the point, not a compromise.[5]

## Laid out in CAD before cutting

Before committing plywood, Bloss laid the plans out in **CAD** in order to **laser-cut a scale model**, with the possibility of using a local college's CNC to cut full-size parts later.[6] That approach put him straight into the plans' geometry: he worked through the **non-perpendicular dimension lines** on page 6 and published both his reasoning and, shortly afterward, his own correction — the figure is the **hypotenuse along the side of the bulkhead**, so the heights marked below the drawing are measured from the baseline down, with the above-baseline measurement added.[6] **Bulkhead 2** was cut as a **single piece**, for which he bought an extra 12 mm (1/2") sheet rather than joining it.[1]

## Materials

The main panels are the local yard's **ABX marine plywood**; the **deck beams** are laminated up from 1/4" Sandee — "a lot of lamination."[7] Reading Elliott's manual against the plans, Bloss also flagged an apparent contradiction over the side panels: the manual advises that with exterior plywood the **knot holes should end up on the outside**, against the more usual practice of hiding them inside where foam and sealing will cover them.[8] For the [stem](/components/stem.md) he weighed clear vertical-grain Douglas fir against #2 construction lumber at 2022 prices — $150–200 for an 8 ft 2×8 in CVG against roughly a tenth of that — for a part he judged "a bloody important part of the boat that'll take a lot of punishment."[9] The same question, cross-posted to the mailing list the same day, drew two opposing views plus further advice on grading and rot-proofing; see [stem — timber grade](/components/stem.md#timber-grade-for-the-stem). He settled it the cheaper way, picking through a lumber pile for a piece with only a few knots he could cut around, for a total cost of **$17**.[10] Epoxy throughout is **System Three Silvertip**, whose 35 °F (2 °C) working floor set the build's calendar more than any other single factor (see [a cold garage as the build's real constraint](#a-cold-garage-as-the-builds-real-constraint) below).[11]

## Bulkheads, beams, and panels (2021–22)

By **27 November 2021** the main lumber order had arrived, the epoxy and fiberglass were on hand, and the garage was cleared — the first time Bloss had built a boat in over a decade.[12] The **four bulkheads, the transom, and the four half-sections of each side panel** were cut in the last week of November 2021, with the circular-hole cutouts for steering lines and the transom deck plate deliberately left for later.[11] The cutting tool throughout was a **7 in circular saw set slightly deeper than the plywood**, which Bloss found capable of "remarkably good freehand work" against a drawn line. Curves were lofted by driving finishing nails at the control points and springing a thin 1/4" × 1 1/8" hardware-store batten against them, clamped or weighted tight to every nail and drawn against; where the curve was too tight for the batten — at the bow end of the sides — he worked in sections against a flexible 15 in aluminium ruler long enough to span four or five 100 mm stations at a time and still fair.[11]

**Cleats** are fitted to the bulkheads with a routine Bloss wrote out in full: parts cut and dry-tested against bare wood, **wire nails predrilled as locating pins**, both mating faces precoated with unthickened epoxy, **thickened epoxy (fumed silica) on one side only**, the part seated on the wire nails, **ring nails** predrilled and driven, clamped, and cleaned up with a plastic spreader. The two difficulties he reports are dialling in the epoxy quantity to avoid "a gooey, sticky mess," and getting ring-nail heads to seat flush rather than slightly proud — on his previous boat he had countersunk and used screws instead (see [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md)).[13]

The **seven deck beams** were laminated on a jig over the following winter, one more than the six the boat needs, in the expectation that one would be lost — and one was, to peel ply that wedged between two laminations as they were pushed together.[14] The **side-panel scarfs** were cut and glued in January–February 2022, the panels then left about a week to cure in a garage at roughly 50 °F.[15] That glue-up produced the build's first real mistake: the wax paper under the first panel tore, epoxy went through it, and the panel bonded to the 4×4 support beneath. A chisel and a hacksaw failed to free it; an **oscillating multi-tool** did, and the resulting shallow area was filled with thickened epoxy. The second panel was laid on **peel ply** instead, and released cleanly.[15] By August 2022 every bulkhead was glued up with its cleats, deck beams, and major cutouts, with one addition of his own: **cleats on the after face of bulkhead 1** for the side lockers running forward from bulkhead 2, which he judged missing from the plans on the evidence of other builders' boats and of bulkhead 2's own forward face, and fitted by eye — not square, and set for maximum width — clear of the hatch outline (the archive reaches the same conclusion independently; see [hull assembly sequence — bulkhead construction details not spelled out on the plans](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#bulkhead-construction-details-not-spelled-out-on-the-plans)).[16] For **bulkhead 1**'s forward opening Bloss fitted a **bought marine sealing hatch** that falls within the outline the plans suggest, hung deliberately with the **latch at the top and the hinges at the bottom** so it drops open and stays open while he works forward with both hands full — noting that other Layden builds use a five-gallon pail and its lid for the same job, a thriftier option he would consider.[17]

## The stem

The stem was laminated from **three 38 mm laminations** and shaped from drawing 7, which gives half-widths at the face and a height above the interface at each 100 mm station. Bloss cut a lettered **template for every station**, marked the same letters on his printed plan sheet, marked the controlling half-width at the top of each, and set the plans' **29° bevel** with an angle gauge to get the rest of the triangle. He laminated the lower two pieces first and marked out and cut the shape at that interface, then added the third and brought it into line, holding the assembly with **ring nails** placed with an eye to drilling through later for a towing eye. The forward curve was sawn vertically and then chiselled and sanded down.[18] He worked partly from the **[Johanna build blog](/references/pete-martin-blog.md)**, and credits it for a mistake its author shared about the half-widths at the top.[18] The advice he took from other builders — not to over-refine the stem, because the bow needs reshaping, thickened epoxy, and sanding to fit the actual boat when it comes together — proved right: fitted in September 2022 the stem stood **slightly proud forward**, as he had seen on other builds, and needed a lot of sanding, many ring nails, and plenty of thickened epoxy.[18][19]

## The vent trunk and its drain

The **[vent trunk](/components/vent-box.md)** on the after face of bulkhead 2 was the build's hardest single piece and, in Bloss's words, "an epoxy nightmare" — a lot of latent trigonometry, a 3D shape to be made from flat lumber, and a stage he found surprisingly sparse in both the plans and [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)'s guide, where other builders' photographs show only the finished product. Underneath it he built blocking from 2×4 scraps; the central filler piece, which serves as both the **mast step** and the drainage shelf, he laminated from 2×4 and 1×4 scraps whose slightly rounded edges made thickened epoxy essential to avoid voids. The sides are 3/4" plywood and 1/4" plywood. The result he judged "bloody strong" and, before sanding, atrocious.[20] Rather than leave the trunk's side off until late or glue it in for good, he planned a **light frame with a large opening and a screwed-down cover on threaded inserts**, for permanent access to the blocks and lines inside.[20][21] One intended departure inside the trunk came from other builders rather than from the plans: told that the plans' **angle for the cam cleats** is lower than most people wanted in practice — with a near-horizontal cleat giving a quick tug up or down to set or release — he intends to mount his considerably more vertical from the start.[21]

The **drain** turned on a units trap that catches American builders specifically. The plans call for 1 in pipe; in the United States nominal PVC sizes are the **inner** diameter, so 1 in schedule 40 pipe is about **1.3 in outside** — a hole Bloss mocked up with calipers and judged "enormous," enough to obliterate the block. He went instead to roughly **7/8 in outside diameter** (half-inch nominal), citing [Bill Serjeant](/people/bill-serjeant.md)'s "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" as precedent and noting that metric builders use about 22 mm, and drilled it with a **7/8 in long spade bit** — using a corded drill rested against the adjacent cleat as a guide to hold the angle, which gave a clean hole in one pass. The pipe was test-fitted but deliberately **not epoxied in**, so that it can be drilled through the bottom and bonded in one operation once the bottom is on.[22] Whether to fit the drain at all was reopened in 2023 by a viewer's report and settled in favour of the plans; that exchange is at [Sailing-Pathfinder videos: the vent-trunk drain](/references/sailing-pathfinder-videos.md#relationship-to-the-facebook-record).

## The transom cleat and baffle

The **transom cleat** — a 35° bevel following a curve 165 mm up from the baseline and rising a further 160 mm at the sides — defeated two approaches before it worked. Battens flexible enough to draw the curve across the whole transom were too fragile to survive it and kept snapping; the fix, suggested by Bloss's partner, an engineer, was a **half-width cardboard pattern** lofted around nails and used for both sides, which reproduced the drawing well. Cutting the cleat then required stock about 25 mm thick and a little over 160 mm wide, wide enough to cut the curve **directly** rather than bend a narrow piece to it — which he had already tried, and which always broke. Lacking five-quarter stock that wide, he **laminated 12 mm plywood** into a blank big enough to cut the curved, bevelled cleat out of. The feathered tip of the bevel is fragile and chipped in handling; he accepted that, on the grounds that the baffle is epoxied to the cleat anyway and the epoxy is stronger than the wood.[23]

The **[baffle](/components/vent-baffle.md)** itself took four materials to template and three to build. Corrugated cardboard was too thick and its folds made it impractical; flat poster board was only available locally up to 28 in wide, not enough; **foam poster board** from an office-supply store, cut with heavy scissors and a folding box cutter and tacked round with brads from a staple gun, wrapped to the shape and patched with scraps where needed. Taken to plywood, his **1/4 in** stock — three-ply, with a visible grain direction on the outer laminations — cracked on the curve even with the grain oriented favourably; **3/16 in** failed too. **2.7 mm (about 1/8 in)** took the curve easily. Because that is significantly thinner than specified, Bloss planned to **reinforce the finished baffle with fibreglass cloth**.[23]

## Trial assembly and glue-up (September–October 2022)

Both faces of every bulkhead were **epoxy-coated before assembly**, on the reasoning that it is easier to control runs and get even coverage on a flat surface than inside a hull.[24] On **27 September 2022** the boat was dry-assembled in three dimensions for the first time, starting at **bulkhead 3** and working forward and aft, with short **drywall screws** as temporary anchors, blocks nailed alongside bulkhead 3 to align it, and a **ratchet strap used as a Spanish windlass** to pull the sides together. The test fit taught more than it proved: the cleats need bevelling on their **sides** as well as their bottoms, and vertical alignment depends on **blocking under the cleat-to-hull junction** at each station — "some height adjustments and contouring the cleats remain," as he put it to the group the same day.[24][25] Climbing inside with a step stool, Bloss reported the cabin volume larger than he had expected from scaled-up Paradoxes seen on video, enough to provision for weeks or possibly a month, and found that reaching through bulkhead 2 into the forward compartment was awkward enough to be worth planning stowage around.[24]

The hull was **squared and strung** on 29 September, with a line run from the centre of the stem to the centre of the transom, and **glued up** on or before 11 October 2022 — bulkheads, stem, transom, and hull sides. Squaring felt impossible until two or three **1×2 diagonals** were in, at which point "suddenly it went from 'I'll never have this lined up straight' to everything looking beautiful."[19][26] Bloss's own lessons from the glue-up are unusually specific: epoxy needs **thickening considerably more for vertical surfaces** than for the bench work that preceded it, or the fillets slump; **drywall screws hold but do not index**, so Elliott's wire-nail locating pins would have made the whole operation less stressful; **alignment blocks on both sides** of bulkhead 3 — which carries no side cleats — would have kept it truly vertical, and its slight lean is the imperfection he says he will always know about; and a **second pair of hands** is worth having, with a book, for the long idle stretches.[26] A gap was left unfilled at the top of the side panels on purpose, where the sheer logs would later be cut in.[26]

His method for the **cleat bevel angles** is worked out on camera and then, six months later, disowned. He drew a tangent at each cleat-to-side junction on a scale print of the plans, drew a line parallel to the centreline, and solved the resulting triangle — finding all the angles fairly close to 90°, the largest excursion about 18°. Cutting to those figures, he found them all slightly too large: shallower angles nearer 90° would have needed less fiddling and less epoxy fill, and the angle at the top of the boat is not the angle at the bottom, because of the panels' curvature.[26] By May 2023 his verdict was that **pre-calculating the bevels from the drawings had been the mistake**, and that taking them off the boat as it comes together is the better approach — a conclusion the archive had already reached from other builders (see [hull assembly sequence — fit at trial assembly rather than pre-cutting a perfect bevel](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#fit-at-trial-assembly-rather-than-pre-cutting-a-perfect-bevel)).[27]

## Sheer logs, chine logs, and a cracked log

One **sheer log** was fitted late in 2022, indoors, in a garage cramped enough that Bloss judged it impractical to continue there — which is why only one went on before the winter.[27] It cracked partially between bulkheads 2 and 3 after being nailed solidly into place, discovered in May 2023; the options he laid out publicly were to cut the section out and patch in new wood, or to open the crack with an oscillating tool and fill it with epoxy.[28] The **second sheer log and both chine logs** went in during the 2024 season. Bloss **laminated the chine logs in place** from thinner stock, saying he could not imagine getting them in otherwise without steaming, and that if he did it again he would look seriously at a **steaming setup** — the chine log's compound curve along the bottom of the hull he rates the least pleasant work of the build so far.[3]

## Systems planned

Two of Pathfinder's intended departures are electrical rather than structural. Bloss is a **ham radio operator** and has canvassed both a **large battery bank used as ballast** — AGM or similar, charged at home between outings, so the mass does double duty — and an **HF antenna** installation on the boat.[29][30] For the water tanks he has looked at **flexible bladders** sized as close to the designed capacity as he can get, preferring them to permanently filled epoxy-lined compartments and accepting that the lost volume may mean adding lead; the **cabin sole**, rough-cut in September 2022, is to be split fore and aft with a fixed portion forward near the vent trunk, specifically to keep access to what is underneath.[31][19]

By **August 2025** the build had reached the sail: he was working through Matt Layden's revised sail document, looking for how far back along the yard the **cutout in the head sleeve** should sit, and reasoning that the halyard attaches at the yard's thickest point, about **700 mm back from the throat** (see [sail](/components/rig/sail.md)).[32]

## A cold garage as the build's real constraint

More than any material or technique, what has governed Pathfinder's pace is **temperature**. The Silvertip epoxy is rated to 35 °F, but Bloss wants 50 °F held for a full 24 hours after a pour, and the rented garage he worked in for the first three years was a shared space with a door that could not be sealed and leaked air badly; an oil-filled radiator could help it hold heat but not enough, for long enough, to be worth the energy. Work stopped over Christmas 2021 and again for the whole winter of 2022–23.[11][27] Dust was the other constraint on the same space: hanging plastic sheeting on wax-linen cord helped but not much, so from mid-2022 he built the boat on a **2×4 dolly running on six locking 5 in casters rated 330 lb each** and wheeled the whole thing out to the driveway to work, vacuuming the driveway afterward.[16] That dolly is also why the boat could be stored assembled — and Bloss's strongest piece of advice to other builders comes from that: getting the hull **into three dimensions before a winter layup** is worth pushing for, because walking past something that looks like a boat every day is an inspiring thing rather than a sad one, and he doubts he would have carried the project through a second winter of flat panels.[27]

## A mouldy shed (2023–25)

The build's largest setback was not structural. Bloss's partner bought a house and he moved with it, which cost most of the 2023 season and part of 2024; on moving in he stored the boat's remaining **lumber and plywood in an existing shed**, where it grew **mould and mildew**. He will not build with compromised wood — "why would I start that way?" — so the stock was written off. The expensive part is the **two 4×8 sheets of 3/4 in plywood for the bottom**, and, no longer on a tech salary, he has been replacing the timber a paycheque at a time. The one consolation of the move is that the new house is a little over a mile from a good lumber yard, close enough that he carried the 16 ft stock for the chine logs home over his shoulder.[3] As of May 2025 the plan was **framing below the cabin sole, then scarfing new bottom panels and getting the bottom on**, worked up from a fresh notebook of the plans and Elliott's guide made to get reacquainted after two years away.[3]
