# Zoë

**"Zoë"** is the [Paradox](/paradox.md) built by professional boat builder [Glen C. Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md), who founded the original `paradoxbuilders` Yahoo group.[1] Her build hours, ballasting, interior fit-out, and a Cumberland Sound sea trial supply some of the best-documented real-world figures for the design.

# The build

- The build took **roughly 1,000 hours spread across about two years** of on-again, off-again work (as a fun project rather than a job, "as the spirit moves me"), with an intense final push in the **last three months** — a thousand hours works out to roughly six months of full-time (40 hr/week) labor, and the boat could have been finished in considerably less elapsed time had the work been continuous. The **vent trunk** stood out as a particularly labor-intensive single component.[2][3] Finish quality is what drives total build time: an early, partway-through estimate of ~500 hours was superseded by the settled ~1,000-hour figure once the boat was complete.[4] See [build effort](/construction/build-effort.md).
- Ahead of painting, the non-skid deck pattern (walnut shells in epoxy; see [non-skid deck](/construction/non-skid-deck.md)) was laid out after firming up all deck-hardware positions, with the bottom already painted (see [bottom painting](/construction/bottom-painting.md)).[5]
- Construction photos of "Zoë" seeded the group's files area (the "construction" and "stove" folders).

Techniques used on "Zoë" are documented as general concepts elsewhere: [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md) (screwing the bottom on from the stern), a preference for removable screws over left-in nails ([fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md)), and [panel layout](/construction/panel-layout.md) choices. She was built to the plans on anchor stowage despite a builder's dislike of a below-deck anchor (see [ground tackle](/operations/ground-tackle.md)).[5]

"Zoë" was built using a lower, **"ext A/B"** grade of plywood rather than Marine grade. The extra finishing labor needed to reach a fine finish with that grade proved unacceptably high, though it did give an acceptable **workboat finish** with less labor overall.[6] See [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md).

## Launch and first sail

"Zoë" was **launched on Friday the 13th** [of November] 2001[7] and taken out for her first sail:

- Conditions were **light**: less than 5 kts of wind (measured by an anemometer).
- Boat speed **"ghosted" along between 2.2 and 3.3 kts** by GPS — respectable progress for such light air.
- "Zoë" was **very light** at this point: only about **75 lb of ballast** and **no water on board** (contrast the full loaded-ballast design figures in [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md), which call for 400 lb of lead alone).
- Even in this under-ballasted state, **stability was still quite good**: she could only be heeled to about **45 degrees** by standing on the dock and pulling the mast down by hand, and was expected to be "a treat when properly ballasted."[8]
- She sailed for about **two hours** before hauling out for a trip to Philadelphia, and all went "very well."[7]
- Her finish looked "as if she just left the ..factory."[9]

## Trailer modifications for shallow launching

"Zoë"'s trailer was modified to allow launching in **less than two feet of water**, illustrated with photos posted to the group's albums (under "Zoe"):

- The **axle is lengthened** (visible from the extended fenders) and mounted **on top of the leaf spring** rather than under it, raising the hull.
- The **stub axle/spindle is welded to the top of the axle** tube.
- The **bunk boards are mounted directly on the trailer frame**, rather than raised on separate risers.[10]

A same-base-size trailer needs only these axle, fender, and bunk-board modifications to reach a similarly low launch height (see [secondhand Paradox market](/operations/secondhand-market.md)).[10]

See also mast fit lessons from finishing "Zoë"'s spar in [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md) — the completed mast, with anchor light fitted, proved about 1.5 in too long to stow with the inspection port fully closed.

## Scale photos and first overnighter

Because a transom-port photo alone made "Zoë" look "enormous," a photo with **one or two people aboard** — including one of the builder sitting inside — was posted to give a true sense of her scale,[11] alongside four more photos from "Zoë"'s **first overnighter**: the boat was loaded with a week's stores and full water tanks, and "all went well." She could still use **another ~100 lb of ballast** to stiffen her up further — as built and loaded for the overnighter she was still "a little too tender" when moving forward on deck to handle anchor or dock lines, though walking around would be easier once more weight went in. A week-long sea trial on Florida's west coast followed once further ballasted.[12]

The photos also prompted a question about plywood **checking**, since "Zoë" was built of fir plywood; see [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md) for the fuller exchange on that question.

## Apalachicola Bay scouting trip

In February 2002 "Zoë" made a solo scouting trip to **Apalachicola Bay and St George Sound**, launching from Carrabelle Beach and anchoring near St George Island State Park.[13] See [Apalachicola Bay — a proposed meeting ground, scouted solo](/merits/gatherings.md#apalachicola-bay-a-proposed-meeting-ground-scouted-solo-2002).

## Sea trials route: Cedar Key to Clearwater

The long-promised week-long **sea trials** (see above) in fact ran from **Cedar Key**, sailing "Zoë" down to **Clearwater**, FL — the first record of the actual route the west-Florida sea trial took, filling in a gap left open since the original November 2001 plan.[14] See [Cedar Key — the annual gathering](/merits/cedar-key.md#origins-the-annual-gathering) for the fuller context, including the plan to sail "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" and "Zoë" together for comparison.

The trip was subsequently written up as a first-person article and published twice (see [Citations](#citations)), which makes it the **best-documented single cruise in the archive for any builder-completed Paradox**. It ran **3–9 December 2001**. The 57 nm direct crossing of the Gulf to Port Richey was deliberately declined in favour of a coastwise track, on the reasoning that following the shoreline would afford the opportunity to explore new ground. Departure from Cedar Key was at 12:40 on 3 December in a dying breeze, and the cruise ended a week later on the beach at the **Honeymoon Island** causeway, just north of Clearwater.[15]

Daily runs were **11.8 nm** (Cedar Key to an anchorage at the mouth of Deep Creek), about **33 nm** (to a small key a mile and a half offshore among the **Chassahowitzka Reefs**), and **25.1 nm** (to a family dock at Port Richey, the last 1.6 miles sculled up the channel over a little more than an hour). A layover day followed for repairs and modifications, then legs to **Ancolote Key** — where a night was spent at anchor in a tidal creek reached under sail in about six tacks, the boat never failing to come about — and finally to Honeymoon Island.[15]

The published log is the archive's densest set of real-world **speed figures** for the design: **2.5–3 kt** in zephyrs of about 5 mph; **4 kt** in no more than 8 kt of breeze, reckoned only ¾ kt short of calculated hull speed; and, in 12–15 kt, a sustained **5.5 kt gusting to 5.9 kt**, with a trip maximum of **6.3 kt** recorded by the GPS but never actually witnessed on the display. Over the shallows off the Bird Keys and St Martin Keys the boat held **2.8–3.2 kt** in water shoal enough that the grass could occasionally be heard scraping the bottom — a passage logged at the time as "so much FUN!" Even at **9 in** draft, however, dead low water left her unable to close within half a mile of the dock at Port Richey.[15]

The trip also settled a question of **deck security**. Properly ballasted, the boat stops about **8 in** short of putting her rail under when weight is moved onto the side deck — initially likened to walking the gunwale of a canoe. The confidence this earned was measurable over the week: at the outset the passage fore and aft was made hunched over the cabin gripping the mast for fear of capsize, while by the end the side deck was walked freely carrying a retrieved anchor, chain and rode in a canvas bag.[15]

Several of the design's systems were tested here for the first time in earnest, each documented on its own page: the **yuloh** coming off its bronze ball and the captive-line fix improvised underway (see [sculling and yuloh technique](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md)); the **below-decks helm station** and the hatch port wanted for it (see [hatch](/components/hatch.md)); an improvised **tiller-line self-steering** rig (see [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md)); a boom-tang and **furling** failure cured with a temporary topping lift (see [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md)); and the discovery that the boat did not roll her guts out downwind despite having no boom vang (see [how chine runners work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md)). The design's accommodation was credited to [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) directly — the boat judged "so well appointed thanks to the attention Matt Layden had given her when he designed the Paradox," in what is also one of the archive's earliest uses of the term **"micro-cruising"** by a builder afloat.[15]

The cruise closed with a **punch list** that reads as the agenda for much of "Zoë"'s later development: a skylight in the hatch, a chart table to keep the chart off the deck, a permanent topping lift controlled from below, a better mattress, and a remedy for the seat board, which no cushion arrangement had yet made comfortable. The first two were fitted and put to use within a few years — see [interior size and the chart table](#interior-size-and-the-chart-table-shown-photographically) and [the skylight and chart table in practical use](#the-skylight-and-chart-table-in-practical-use-a-cumberland-sound-cruise) above, where the hatch skylight does exactly the job wanted for it here.[15]

## Interior size and the chart table, shown photographically

How large a Paradox's interior really is, and how big a crew she can handle, is answered here visually rather than with more figures — a recurring question going back to the December 2001 ["wearing the boat"](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md) discussion. Photos of a person **sitting inside "Zoë"** were posted to the group's files area (Interior folder) to give a real sense of interior scale, alongside separate photos of a **chart-table solution**, including one showing it in use while seated. The tight, single-hander-oriented interior is underscored by a wry aside that the images should let viewers "figure out how many close (intimate) friends" they would want to go sailing with. This is the first record of a **chart table** being fitted to a Paradox, though no further detail on its construction or location survives beyond the photos themselves.[16] See [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md) for the fuller "wearing the boat" discussion these photos speak to.

## The skylight and chart table in practical use: a Cumberland Sound cruise

Months after first showing the hatch skylight and chart table only in still photos (see [interior and chart-table photos](#interior-size-and-the-chart-table-shown-photographically) above), a weekend cruise of **Cumberland Sound** (St. Mary's River entrance, GA) put both to actual use — the fullest single trip report on record for "Zoë" since her original launch. On an overcast day spent helming from below with the hatch closed, the **skylight** allowed seeing the sail for trimming without going on deck, and, once heeled over, seeing to leeward without bending down to the leeward windows — an unanticipated bonus. The **wheelbarrow inner tube** seat cushion (an idea from *Sailing Just for Fun*, previously only proposed — see [Glen C. Maxwell — a seat-cushion comfort tip](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md)) "worked out better then hoped for" once actually used underway.[17]

The same cruise supplied a real-world **windward-performance** data point for the [chine runners](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) (nearly five hours short-tacking against 12 kt winds and a foul current, averaging 3.6 kt over 18 nm) and turned up a newly found **mast-heel play** problem possibly costing some of that performance (see [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md)). It also yielded a **helm-balance fix** via halyard position (see [sailing characteristics and rig balance — Glen's CE/CLR diagnosis](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md) for the related "Sail balance" thread this experiment answered), a cold-night cabin-heat data point (see [cabin heating for cold-weather sailing](/operations/cabin-heating.md)), and two on-the-water mishaps recovered without outside help: a **trailer-tire blowout** before reaching the ramp (see [trailering — a blowout despite a new trailer](/operations/trailering.md)) and a **halyard-knot failure and furling-line jam** cleared underway (see [furling system — a halyard-knot failure and a furling-line jam](/components/rig/furling.md)).[17] See [Glen C. Maxwell — "Building encouragement"](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md) for the full, single-message trip report all of these threads are drawn from.

## Condensation, CE/CLR, and a third weight estimate

"Zoë" carries **no insulation** and has not suffered for it on window-condensation drainage: a lip surrounding the cabin collects condensation running off the windows before it can pool, and through-ventilation carries off cabin moisture at the cost of a colder cabin (see [cabin heating — a window-condensation channel](/operations/cabin-heating.md)).[18] Her CE/CLR relationship was never worked out numerically; the low-tech hand method for finding both is documented instead (see [sailing characteristics — a newcomer asks Glen to quantify CE/CLR](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md)). A **third, independent weight estimate** for "Zoë" put her at 650-750 lb empty with ballast, roughly 900 lb loaded for a month's cruise, against a 1,400 lb designed displacement — without ever actually scaling her (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — a third weight figure](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)).[19] On the **hatch skylight**, some two and a half years later: it produces no condensation of its own.[20]

## Sailing with no water aboard, and a tiller stop

"Zoë" has sailed with **no water aboard**, and "did just fine" — though [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) judged her to look "a little low in the water," suggesting either **removing some ballast** or simply day-sailing without filling the tanks, rather than always loading her to full cruising trim. This is a small but concrete real-world data point on how a Paradox handles lightly loaded, complementing the stability diagnosis for "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"'s own unballasted launch (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — an unloaded launch](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)).

A **tiller stop**, once fitted, "does help a lot" with the recurring difficulty of finding the right spot to tie off the tiller while working the yuloh — a problem that "does take some getting used to."[21]

"Zoë" was also fitted with a purpose-built windvane self-steering gear, sea-tried for the first time at the 2007 Cedar Key gathering, where the mainsheet broke its oar carrier before a full trial could be made. See [self-steering — Glen Maxwell's horizontal-axis wind-vane concept for "Zoë"](/operations/self-steering.md#glen-maxwells-horizontal-axis-wind-vane-concept-for-zoë) and [self-steering — sea trial and damage at the 2007 Cedar Key gathering](/operations/self-steering.md#sea-trial-and-damage-at-the-2007-cedar-key-gathering) for the full design and trial account.

## The choice to leave "Zoë" without foam flotation

"Zoë" carries no foam flotation at all — a deliberate choice, restated and explained in full more than three years after launch. Across 45 years of sailing — including an Atlantic crossing aboard "[Hogfish Maximus](/people/chris-morejohn.md#contributions-and-stories)" — Glen never owned an unsinkable boat, and weighs flotation against a competing priority: being able to inspect behind it for dry rot or accumulated moisture. The plans' specified foam, concentrated in the deck head above the tiller-port opening, is suspected of doing more duty as **insulation** than as reserve buoyancy, and costs precious interior volume in so small a boat. The door-skin **[removable foam panel](/construction/removable-foam-panels.md)** technique — mounting foam on a paintable, unscrewable panel rather than gluing it to the hull — resolves the inspection problem in principle, but on "Zoë" herself the foam is left out altogether, with interior fittings mounted directly to blocks glued to the hull sides and deck head instead. The choice was considered unsafe elsewhere on the list; the settled verdict, after three years' sailing without it, is that the trade-off is worth making on this boat.[22] See [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md) for the fuller foam-vs-no-foam debate and a contrasting experience with glued-in foam on "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)."

## A rudder up-haul safety lapse

A 50-mile round trip to try the wind-vane gear again turned up a rigging lapse: the rudder up-haul line had come loose, or had been left loose in the rush to get sailing, costing roughly **15%** of the rudder's area by the time the boat reached the ramp. The usual practice of running the mainsheet through the up-haul to safety it against exactly this loss was forgotten as well. Rather than abandon the outing, the exposed end-grain wood was coated with **Marine Goop** and the boat launched regardless — sailing in **16 mph gusting to 18 mph** with little if any noticeable difference in the helm despite the reduced rudder area. See [rudder — a lost rudder up-haul, and sailing on with reduced rudder area](/components/rudder.md#a-lost-rudder-up-haul-and-sailing-on-with-reduced-rudder-area) for the cross-linked rudder-hardware angle.[25]

## A full sea trial and course-holding test

The wind-vane gear (see [self-steering — Glen Maxwell's horizontal-axis wind-vane concept for "Zoë"](/operations/self-steering.md#glen-maxwells-horizontal-axis-wind-vane-concept-for-zoë) and [self-steering — sea trial and damage at the 2007 Cedar Key gathering](/operations/self-steering.md#sea-trial-and-damage-at-the-2007-cedar-key-gathering) for the design and its earlier, damage-cut-short Cedar Key outing) got a full, dedicated sea trial on 8 July 2007, once repaired. After sorting out minor teething problems underway — the oar tiller jumping out of its crank, the vane blade blowing out of alignment in strong gusts, and vibration-loosened screws — the gear held a steady 180° heading, plus or minus a couple of degrees, after a short period of tuning the course-setting lines, in 16 mph gusting to 19 mph wind. Full technical account of the trial and the vane's drive mechanism (explained afterward in response to reader questions) is at [self-steering — the 8 July 2007 sea trial: a full course-holding test](/operations/self-steering.md#the-8-july-2007-sea-trial-a-full-course-holding-test) and [self-steering — the vane's drive mechanism, explained](/operations/self-steering.md#the-vanes-drive-mechanism-explained).[26][27][28][29][30][31][32][33]

## Foam flotation on "Zoë" revisited, and window tint

A foam-flotation question drew Glen's reasoning on "Zoë" full circle: foam was left off her side walls and top deck specifically to keep the hull's inside visible for moisture inspection (see [the choice to leave "Zoë" without foam flotation](#the-choice-to-leave-zoë-without-foam-flotation) above), with the door-skin removable-panel method — worked out only after "Zoë" was finished — as the way to have both foam and inspectability on a later build; see [removable foam flotation panels](/construction/removable-foam-panels.md) for the technique in full.[34] A companion article on painting the hull, referenced from memory in the same reply, could not be relocated in the files section despite a search.[34]

Asked to confirm whether "Zoë"'s cabin windows were tinted, Glen reported barely being able to tell they were tinted from inside even at night, with very good visibility — the difference only becomes noticeable when the sun is shining directly on the glazing. See [window glazing — window tint color](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#window-tint-color) for the fuller tint-color record this adds to.[35]

## Continued vane refinement: an undersized blade, and the autopilot coupling

Renewed light-air testing of "Zoë"'s wind-vane self-steering gear went poorly in November 2007, blamed on an undersized vane blade due for enlargement, with drawings deferred until the gear worked reliably; a run of minor assembly troubles was corrected as it turned up. See [self-steering — renewed testing: an undersized vane blade, and a rolling-hitch tillerpilot adapter](/operations/self-steering.md#renewed-testing-an-undersized-vane-blade-and-a-rolling-hitch-tillerpilot-adapter) for the fuller technical exchange, including the Autohelm 800's own tiller-line rolling-hitch coupling method.[36] Photographs of the Autohelm coupling followed, posted to a new "Zoë's Autopilot Setup" files folder;[37][38] mounting on the port side was judged the easier of the two, keeping the pilot clear of the compass.[39] See [self-steering — photographs of the autopilot coupling, and further tiller-lock variants](/operations/self-steering.md#photographs-of-the-autopilot-coupling-and-further-tiller-lock-variants) for the fuller exchange, including a further tiller-lock design shown in the photos.

## Press coverage: two 2003 magazine articles

"Zoë" and her 2001 shakedown cruise reached print twice in the same year. *Messing About In Boats*' August 15, 2003 issue carried an article with photographs from the six-day, west-Florida shakedown cruise (see [sea trials route](#sea-trials-route-cedar-key-to-clearwater) above), praising "Zoë" throughout, listing several in-progress improvements made along the way, and including tips on catching fish for dinner underway.[40] A change of postal address had left the boat's builder roughly four issues behind on his own subscription, without a copy in hand until the editor arranged to send the missing copies.[41] The article was later scanned and uploaded to the group's files section — after a few failed attempts,[42][43] and with the photos stripped out to cut file size — as both a full-color version (nearly 2 MB, a long download over dial-up) and a smaller photo-free version, several months after the original print date.[44]

The article's UK readership led to its reproduction on the **paradox-uk.co.uk** website (copyright remaining with the original author),[45] posted as "Zoë's Sea Trials" at `www.btinternet.com/~w.serjeant/paradox/trials.htm`.[46] See [Small Sailboats / Paradox-UK websites](/references/paradox-uk-website.md) for that site. Reading the article prompted a **topping-lift question** — full detail at [furling system — a UK builder's topping-lift question](/components/rig/furling.md#a-uk-builders-topping-lift-question) — along with an unanswered question about whether "Zoë" keeps a chain and warp permanently attached to her stowed main anchor.[45]

The *Small Craft Advisor* Sept/Oct 2003 issue then carried "Cedar Key Small Boat Meet," which devoted substantial space to "Zoë" and "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" — the two Paradoxes present — praising both, and noting at one point that "Zoë appeared to be sailing to windward higher and faster than my Sea Pearl with its leeboard down." The article also mentioned seeing designer [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md), described as "the reclusive designer," at the same gathering, and ran four photographs of "Zoë" and "Whisper" alongside others from the meet.[47] See [the 2003 Cedar Key messabout](/merits/cedar-key.md#the-2003-cedar-key-messabout-dates-and-logistics) for the gathering itself.

## The 2010 sale

In November 2010 Glen C. Maxwell offered "Zoë" for sale at **$6,000**, downsizing to prepare for a planned circumnavigation aboard his partner's Tanton 43.[50] The listing drew unqualified praise from fellow owners: Geoff Davis (of "[Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md)," Australia) called her "easily the best example built," judged the asking price only enough to cover raw materials for whoever bought her, and thanked Glen for the inspiration his build had given to "our various builds";[51] David Beard, who knew "Zoë" personally, judged whoever got her "will have a work of art," and thanked Glen "for all your help";[52] and Rodney Cannon (see [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md)), then still building his own Paradox, credited Glen's own boat and adventures as "the driving force" behind his choice to build one, judging the asking price roughly half the boat's true worth.[53] A follow-up request for the "informative website" Rod had praised drew Glen's own pointer to the group's file archive rather than a personal-site link,[54][55] and, from Rodney Cannon, the archive's most detailed firsthand account of how a newcomer actually prepared to build — see [Rodney Cannon — building Wizard Spawn](/people/rodney-cannon.md#contributions-and-stories) for the fuller account, including the direct link to Glen's own personal website he supplied.[56] Glen also confirmed the sale included a trailer and virtually everything shown in the group's own Files section, excepting a small GPS unit and an Autohelm 800 autopilot, both already in service on his other boats.[57]

## A January 2011 classifieds listing

Two months after the November 2010 mailing-list offer, "Zoë" appeared for sale to a wider audience on the Sailing Texas classifieds site, described there as "well built and proven" — "a great deal for someone wanting a Paradox without all the construction."[58] The listing drew further comparative praise: Alastair Law (of "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)") judged it a bargain he wouldn't match selling his own boat, rating "Zoë" a much higher-quality build;[59] and Rodney Cannon, still completing his own Paradox, reflected that two more years' patience would have let him buy her outright — though, having already invested too much of himself in his own hull to abandon it, self-deprecatingly called his the slowest build yet.[60]

## A new owner, and an offer of Bay-area company

The classifieds listing found a buyer within days: a longtime follower of Paradox threads who had never expected the chance to own one himself took over "Zoë," planning to learn on her progressively — local-lake outings first, then San Francisco Bay, and eventually coastal sailing beyond the Golden Gate if it suited him — judging the risk worth taking since a boat of her reputation should resell easily if he found himself over his head.[61] [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md), a fellow owner in the nearby Sacramento area, welcomed the sale and proposed sailing together on the Bay that summer, having so far sailed only Folsom Lake since his own move to the area.[62]

News of the sale drew a wider round of goodwill from the fleet: [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md), finishing his own Paradox ("[Wizard Spawn](/boats/wizard-spawn.md)") in Los Angeles, called it having "Zoë... on the West Coast" and reported his own build almost done,[63] and Mark Waters counted **three Paradoxes now on the West Coast** — Rodney's, his own "[Raffles](/boats/raffles.md)" in the Sacramento area, and "Zoë" herself — floating a get-together of the three as "a first."[64] Mark Waters also asked whether "Zoë" had come with the wind-vane self-steering gear Glen Maxwell built for her, revealing he had finished building his own version of the same gear in his workshop but had not yet fitted it, and wanted to know how well it performed once "Zoë"'s new owner reached that point.[64]

The new owner filled in his own picture in reply: he lives in the **San Francisco East Bay**, is new to sailing outright — his only prior time aboard a sailboat was as a passenger drinking beer while the skipper "tuned, changed, and futzed" — and was drawn to Paradox by her drawings and the idea of an enclosed cabin on a micro-cruiser rather than by performance sailing.[65] A beginner's how-to-sail rundown followed in reply, along with reassurance that Paradox tolerates a beginner's mistakes well; see [operations/first-boat-suitability.md](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#a-first-timers-basic-handling-hoisting-steering-to-a-heading-and-gybing-safely) for the technique in full.[66] Glen Maxwell, in turn, was confirmed to be providing "Zoë"'s new owner with all the plans and paperwork that came with her; the new owner expected to have her out sailing within two or three months, once his schedule allowed, looking forward less to trimming lines than to simply sitting comfortably and relaxing under way.[67]

## Destroyed by fire in transit (2011)

"Zoë" never reached her new owner. In June 2011, while under road transport from Florida to California by an enclosed trailer, the hauler's trailer caught fire for undetermined reasons and could not be extinguished before the boat and trailer were a total loss.[68] The buyer had wanted a Paradox for more than fifteen years but lacked the woodworking skill to build one himself, and judged the loss's intrinsic value "nearly priceless" despite the insured monetary loss being covered.[68] A fellow owner who had personally been aboard "Zoë" confirmed she was "an incredible boat," praised Glen Maxwell's build and refinement of her, and urged the buyer not to abandon the dream — to keep looking for another Paradox or Layden-designed cruiser, or to commission a new build, using a professional builder's quote (Glen Maxwell's own among them) to support an insurance claim.[69] The buyer pursued the hauler's insurance for the boat's full replacement value, expecting a quote in the **$22,000–25,000** range against his own rough triple-material-cost estimate of **$13,500**, which others on the list judged too low; on describing the design as a keel-less, centerboard-less "micro-cruiser" to one traditional boatbuilder, he met open skepticism.[70][71] The loss was independently guessed, by a fellow correspondent, at roughly **$20,000** to replicate through a professional builder.[72] After talking further with the hauler, the buyer came to believe the fire had been accidental rather than deliberate: the trucker had wrapped the boat and trailer heavily in padding meant to survive even a major highway collision, and in the buyer's own view that same padding was most likely what let the fire take hold so readily, leaving only ashes and metal framing in the salvage photographs. A suggestion aired on the list that the trucker might have staged the fire — swapping the boat out beforehand to avoid liability — struck both the buyer and a fellow correspondent as unlikely, given how obscure the design is outside the Paradox community.[73][74][75] Afterward, a Berkeley, California yacht builder who reviewed photographs and drawings of "Zoë" grew increasingly impressed on his own, taken by the roller-furled sail's low center of effort when reefed and by the chine-runner concept, and remarked the boat would have been a hit at his own boat shows; with builder quotes expected the following week, the buyer revised his own rough guess for a professional rebuild to **$18,000–24,000**.[75] Two entries in Bill's Log, written years later and sourced only to photographs seen online, independently corroborate the same fire.[48][49]

News of the loss drew tribute from across the fleet. One owner recalled poring over photographs of "Zoë" while building "[Small Fry](/boats/small-fry.md)," finding in her both a practical guide through the construction and a source of motivation on days his own progress felt tedious.[76] Another credited Glen Maxwell's own published account of "Zoë"'s shakedown cruise with having "probably sold" him on the Paradox design in the first place, reflecting that "her spirit lives on in others boats."[77] A third, a fellow West Coast owner, judged that Glen had "set the bar high" building "Zoë" and credited that standard with the many high-quality Paradoxes built since, recalling that he had kept her photographs alongside his own plans throughout building "[Raffles](/boats/raffles.md)"; he asked whether photographs of "Zoë" — and of Bill Serjeant's "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" — were still available anywhere online, having lost track of copies he once had, and offered the buyer a visit to see his own boat.[78] The buyer, still absorbing the shock of the loss, accepted the offer and confirmed the Berkeley yacht builder noted above as **Berkeley Yacht Center**, which would also offer a fiberglass-panel alternative to plywood and had grown intrigued by [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s design more broadly.[79] He was glad, regardless, to see new Paradoxes being built.[80] Asked whether he had received quotes from other builders yet, he confirmed he had submitted "Zoë"'s plans to only the one builder so far, doubted the transport company's insurance would pay out full replacement value, and expected a quote within a day or two.[81][82]

## Considering a commissioned rebuild

Still waiting on quotes and the insurance outcome, the buyer opened a broader question the following month: since a used Paradox comes up for sale so rarely, having one built to order looked like the more realistic path to actually owning one, and he canvassed the list for modification ideas he might specify to a builder.[83] The replies amounted to a working design review of several stock features. Whether a hinged hatch would be safer than the standard sliding one, and what to do about ventilation and a possible second, forward hatch, are covered at [hatch — a 2011 canvass for a possible commissioned rebuild](/components/hatch.md#a-2011-canvass-for-a-possible-commissioned-rebuild-hinged-versus-sliding-and-a-washboard-alternative) and [vent box](/components/vent-box.md#design-rationale-ventilation-for-the-bahamas-and-cold-climate-adaptations); why the hatch does not admit dangerous amounts of water even in a knockdown is covered at [self-righting and capsize recovery — a 2011 restatement of the 2006 knockdown account](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#a-2011-restatement-of-the-2006-knockdown-account); and why the mast itself sits off the boat's centerline — a separate question, unrelated to safety — is answered at [rig — why the mast itself sits off-centre](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-mast-itself-sits-off-centre). [Bill Serjeant](/boats/faith.md), drawing on his own ownership of "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)," recommended reinforcing the tack fittings with epoxied hardwood (see [tack strop — a further builder's own reinforcement](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#a-further-builders-own-reinforcement-and-a-washboard-alternative-proposed-for-a-commissioned-rebuild)) and judged an outboard engine indispensable for the kind of coastal cruising "Faith" had done, the yuloh alone being usable only in calm conditions and much slower than the engine (see [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-firsthand-case-for-the-petrol-outboard-tide-wind-and-a-drying-moorings-deadline)) — closing with a warning that, given how time-consuming Paradox is to build, a professionally built example "will cost a mint."[84] Satisfied that his real concerns had been answered, the buyer concluded he was "going forward," his only regret being that no CNC-cut kit version of the design exists.[85] By late July, that commission had moved from concept to conversation: he reported he was talking to someone already about building him a Paradox "for a very reasonable cost," made in passing while thanking a fellow correspondent for offering unofficial Enigma DXF hull files — alongside which he gave his own long-held impression that Enigma performs better to windward, whatever Paradox's other advantages. See [Enigma — an unofficial DXF hull layout, and a 2011 update on both Yahoo groups](/matt-layden/enigma.md#an-unofficial-dxf-hull-layout-and-a-2011-update-on-both-yahoo-groups) for the exchange this remark appeared in.[86]
