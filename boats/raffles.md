# Raffles (Mark Waters)

**"Raffles"** is the Paradox built by [Mark Waters](/people/mark-waters.md) in Los Angeles, California, and completed in 2006. She was built strictly to Matt Layden's plans, and her finish — a mahogany interior in particular — made her one of the best-regarded hulls in the fleet, described in a 2014 retrospective as "one of the best built" Paradoxes.[1][2][4] After completion she traveled by road across the United States and back, settling on the Columbia River at Portland, Oregon.[3][11] Her one long-running post-launch project was a self-steering wind vane, which took roughly two years from first enquiry to a finished gear.[5][10]

## Build

Waters introduced the boat to the group in February 2006, when she was already 90% complete and still unnamed, registering her with the UK Paradox build registry and looking for other builders and owners in the Los Angeles area; he expected to finish that summer.[1] Full detail of the registry entry is at [references/paradox-uk-website.md](/references/paradox-uk-website.md#a-2006-database-renewal-and-a-new-los-angeles-builder). She was built strictly to the plans, with no departures from the stock design at the hull level.[1] Despite prior boatbuilding experience — Waters had already built a Selway-Fisher 50/50 sailing canoe — the Paradox's construction proved more complex and detailed than expected, and took the better part of a year.[1][12] By June 2006 photographs of the completed boat were circulating in the group, drawing the verdict that she was "a really super job."[17] Throughout the build, Waters kept his plans laid out beside photographs of "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," Glen Maxwell's own Paradox, for reference and motivation.[20]

The whole boat was built with **System Three** epoxy, chosen for its price, ease of mixing, and customer service; Aircraft Spruce in Corona supplied it locally. See [epoxy brands, chemistry, and cure behavior](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#mas-epoxy-validated-for-general-use-and-a-california-supplier) for the wider brand comparison.[15]

## Ballast: steel weight-lifting plates instead of lead

Waters voted for 300 lb of ballast in an August 2006 group poll, one of only two votes cast.[13] For the boat itself he settled on **steel weight-lifting weights** rather than lead — bulkier for the same weight, and so more costly in stowage space, but chosen deliberately to keep toxic lead away from his young son. The choice brought a practical advantage of its own: such weights come in a range of sizes, so they can be rearranged and re-secured easily to trim the boat.[14] See [weight, ballast, and displacement — ballast material choice](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#ballast-material-choice-lead-against-concrete-iron-and-other-alternatives).

## Interior and modifications

"Raffles"' interior was finished in **mahogany**, and the work was admired enough in photographs that a newer California builder e-mailed Waters directly to ask what wood he had used, then adopted the same choice for his own boat — see [boats/wizard-spawn.md](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#mahogany-for-the-interior-inspired-by-another-builders-finish).[16]

Her departures from stock are all above the waterline, and all made after the hull itself was built to plan:[12]

- The **built-in water tanks** were removed.
- The rudder's [swim step](/components/rudder.md) was done away with.
- A **raised seat** was built, bringing the seated position level with the deck — a way of gaining the sightline a companionway would give without compromising the doghouse, and so preserving the cooling breeze on hot days.
- An **outboard** was fitted.

Looking back on the build, Waters judged he would make three changes given the chance again: the bulkhead #2 adjustment other builders have made, either removing the side bins or building in proper cabinets in their place, and making the battery compartment easier to reach — lifting a fifty-pound battery into that tight space on his back he called "quite a feat of agility."[12]

## Sailing record and an owner's assessment

Personal circumstances limited how often Waters got "Raffles" out over 2007-2008, but every outing exceeded his expectations. He sailed her especially in the cold, rainy weather that keeps other boats at the dock, and found her easy to rig, launch, and sail.[12] He was happily surprised by how well she tracked under sail — well enough that a simpler, old-fashioned direct vane, without a servo-pendulum linkage, looked as though it might be enough for her.[5]

At **5'10" and 160 lb**, Waters found the stock size suited him without any changes, though he suggested sailors over 6 ft think twice — see [crew size and cabin fit — reference sailor heights and weights](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#reference-sailor-heights-and-weights-and-a-two-up-alternative-the-bolger-micro).[12] Of the auxiliary arrangements, he found the sculling oar effective but difficult to use except in a near-dead calm, and his portable "cruise'n'carry" motor had problems of its own.[11][12] His overall verdict was a great boat, customizable "to your heart's content," with fitted cabin-sole cushions still to make and no shortage of projects in sight.[12]

## Travels: Los Angeles to Portland

Built in Los Angeles, "Raffles" had by December 2008 traveled by road to Nashville and back to the West Coast at Portland, Oregon, with a further move to the Bay Area expected soon after.[12] Reporting the move to Portland in October 2008, Waters noted he was now not far from Puget Sound and proposed sailing in company with another owner there, as "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"'s and "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"'s builders had done; he had not yet had her out on the nearby Columbia River, being still occupied with the wind vane.[3] See [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#mark-waters-a-shared-puget-sound-and-columbia-river-cruise-proposed-and-a-confirmed-move-to-portland) for the exchange that followed, including a proposed "Columbia 150."

## The self-steering wind vane

The vane gear was "Raffles"' longest project, and the archive's fullest single record of a Paradox owner designing and building one from scratch. Waters opened the search in August 2008, asking for good design sources, whether his own thinking resembled the vane built for "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," and whether a sheet-to-tiller arrangement might be worth trying instead.[5] He passed along a free PDF of *Self-Steering Under Sail*, judging it strong on theory and useful for understanding commercial systems from a "reverse engineering" perspective, though it offered no step-by-step building instructions.[6] See [self-steering — a further owner's tracking observation, and interest in a simpler vane](/operations/self-steering.md#a-further-owners-tracking-observation-and-interest-in-a-simpler-vane) and [a recommended self-steering reference](/operations/self-steering.md#a-recommended-self-steering-reference-catalogued-and-freely-available).

Pursuing the dimensions behind "Zoë"'s own gear drew detailed servo-blade proportions in reply, along with a recommendation to study the commercially built Cape Horn gear; Waters followed with a question about whether the Cape Horn oar swings freely, and about a **Rudder-Head-Mount (RHM)** design — the Model 90-93 on the Walt Murray site — that combined a trim tab's and a pendulum's advantages.[7] Having built a model of an RHM gear over a weekend and found "the penny finally dropped," he put four detailed practical questions to the list: whether the rudder tiller is left to swing completely free, whether the pendulum swings out if not secured, whether the securing line around the pendulum shaft causes noticeable friction or would call for a bearing, and where the vane setting lines are best secured — including whether they can be reached safely from the cockpit in a seaway.[8] See [self-steering — quantified servo-blade proportions, and the rudder-head-mount principle explained](/operations/self-steering.md#quantified-servo-blade-proportions-and-the-rudder-head-mount-principle-explained) and [practical rudder-head-mount building questions, answered](/operations/self-steering.md#practical-rudder-head-mount-building-questions-answered) for the answers these drew.

Three weeks later he had two rival models under way at once — an upside-down design and a conventional one, to compare which performed best — and asked specifically about the Walt Murray system's own **5 in x 32 in x 9 in** vane-blade dimensions, having read of light-air trouble with a vane built to that size; he floated building in the ability to switch between a larger and a smaller blade to suit conditions rather than settling on one size.[9] See [self-steering — progress on two competing vane builds, and a question of blade size](/operations/self-steering.md#progress-on-two-competing-vane-builds-and-a-question-of-blade-size).

In June 2009 Waters reported the vane gear finished at last — it had taken five times longer than he first expected — and immediately ran into a mounting problem. Set far enough aft to clear the boom, the vane was awkward to reach for routine adjustment without risking "an unplanned swim," which prompted him to consider a mount that could bring it inboard when needed.[10] See [self-steering — the vane gear finished at last, and a mounting-adjustment problem](/operations/self-steering.md#the-vane-gear-finished-at-last-and-a-mounting-adjustment-problem). As of January 2011, the gear was still unfitted to the boat — finished in the workshop but not yet mounted, the same clearance problem apparently still unresolved a year and a half on.[19]

## Ground tackle and a pedal-drive enquiry

Planning sailing that would need a fair amount of anchoring chain, Waters weighed fitting a sealed chain pipe and asked how others store and drag chain through the boat — prompting the archive's rule of thumb on chain quantity: 4 metres of chain backed by a 30 metre warp typically covers most conditions, with a minimum of two anchors carried. See [ground tackle and anchoring — a 2009 chain-quantity rule of thumb](/operations/ground-tackle.md#a-2009-chain-quantity-rule-of-thumb-and-a-considered-chain-pipe).[10]

He had thought the wind vane would be "Raffles"' last major project, but a pedal-powered outboard proposal put to the list gave him another: he asked about optimum propeller size and how to get power to a propeller without major hull-compromising holes, floating a series of universal joints so only one hole through the aft deck would be needed.[11] He later pointed to a commercial pedal-generator product closer to what he had in mind, needing only one hole safely above the waterline — but priced over $3,000, too expensive to buy outright and more useful as a model to reverse-engineer.[18] See [auxiliary outboard power — a pedal-drive outboard conceived for Ironsides, and the alternatives it drew](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-pedal-drive-outboard-conceived-for-ironsides-and-the-alternatives-it-drew).
