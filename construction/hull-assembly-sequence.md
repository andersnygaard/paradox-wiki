# Hull assembly sequence

Once a builder has finished cutting and standing up the [bulkheads](/construction/panel-layout.md), the next stage is pulling the hull itself together. The recommended method for this stage is laid out in the "Building the Paradox" articles.

## Recommended order

1. **Make the stem** first — a board wide enough to cut the whole stem piece from in one go is worth sourcing ahead of time.[1]
2. **Make the tank top and floor** — build the floor "doors" as **one big piece for now** and cut them to their final divided form later. Put these in position: doing so early **helps align the hull itself**.
3. **Pull the side panels to the stem.** This requires real force, since the panels must bend into shape. A useful trick: make a **hot, wet blanket** and wrap it around the panels for a while — the heat and moisture make the plywood noticeably easier to pull into its curved shape.
4. **Dry-fit repeatedly.** Expect to **assemble the hull two or three times with no glue** ("tack" only) in order to bevel the mating edges and get everything sitting correctly before committing to epoxy.
5. **Trace for the insulation.** While the hull is temporarily assembled, **mark around the bulkheads and other members in pencil**. These outlines are then used to **cut the insulation pieces** to match, which "assures a good fit."[2]

A fully photographed example of this dry-assembly stage — panels and bulkheads fitted together unglued to check fit before committing epoxy — is documented for [Faith](/boats/faith.md#a-dry-assembly-of-the-hull-named-faith).

In a **2 ft-clearance garage**, the assembled bulkheads can leave the hull feeling **both bigger and smaller than expected** — big enough that working on it with the garage doors shut in the rain is a squeeze, yet seemingly too small for everything that has to fit inside.[3]

A further recommendation on sequencing, beyond the stem-first order above: make all of the boat's smaller ancillary parts — rudder, mast, and the like — **before** starting the hull itself. This pays off two ways: physically, it is easier to shape and fit small parts without a part-built hull already clogging the workspace; and psychologically, having a finished piece of the boat early keeps motivation up, and means the boat is ready to launch as soon as the hull itself is finally done.[4]

**A garage-constrained build order, worked through in practice.** One recorded build shows this small-parts-first principle worked out concretely against real workspace limits. With only two feet of clearance on every side of a garage barely big enough to hold the finished hull, the practical answer was to build every small part first, under cover, before the boat grew too large to fit alongside them: deck beams first, then the cabin and hatch beams, then the spars (including the mast), the tiller, and the rudder — all done while the weather was still warm enough for epoxy — followed by the bulkheads with their cleats, blocks, and floors, leaving the hull itself to be assembled only once the following spring arrived, on a mobile platform that could be shunted in and out of the garage as the weather allowed.[5] The following spring, with the frames, transom, stem, and side panels being offered up and checked for a snug fit ahead of gluing, a purpose-built **mobile building trolley** let the part-assembled hull be wheeled in and out of the garage at will — working outdoors when there was room to do so, and moving back under cover to protect the boat from weather whenever work stopped.[6]

A related low-headroom-garage technique — assembling the vent box onto bulkhead 2 before the hull, so the mast can be fitted and checked without lifting it bodily in and out of a finished hull — is covered at [vent box — assembling the vent box onto bulkhead 2 before the hull](/components/vent-box.md#assembling-the-vent-box-onto-bulkhead-2-before-the-hull-in-a-low-headroom-garage).

## A beginner's order of work: spars first, hull second

The small-parts-first principle above has a published counterexample from the other direction — a first-time builder who did it the wrong way round and said so in print.[87] Having built the hull first, he was left making the **mast, yard, boom, yuloh, rudder, bottom pintle, tack strop and boom axle** with a finished hull standing in the workshop waiting for them, which he found genuinely demoralising; his stated advice, if starting again, is to make all of those first, then the deck beams, then the bulkheads. Only one of them gave him trouble to build: the mast, which he could not interpret from the drawing at all (see [mast — an exploded drawing that made the plans' mast intelligible](/components/rig/mast.md#an-exploded-drawing-that-made-the-plans-mast-intelligible)).[87]

His other sequencing points bear on decisions this page treats elsewhere:

- **Build the vent box onto bulkhead 2 while the bulkhead is still on the bench.** He was told to and did not, and rated working on it afterwards inside the hull as thoroughly unpleasant — an independent confirmation of the technique at [vent box](/components/vent-box.md#assembling-the-vent-box-onto-bulkhead-2-before-the-hull-in-a-low-headroom-garage).[87]
- **Follow drawing 6.** He found the plans' own construction sequence for the hull given there in some detail and reported that it works well.[87]
- **Do not chase the bevels.** He gave up working them out and relied on thickened epoxy to fill gaps, wooden infills in the large ones, and fresh wood to build panels back up where they were cut undersize or at the wrong angle — and recommends cutting **oversize and trimming to fit**, since the alternative is discovering a panel is short. See [fit at trial assembly rather than pre-cutting a perfect bevel](#fit-at-trial-assembly-rather-than-pre-cutting-a-perfect-bevel).[87]
- **Make the stem to fit the panels during the dry fit**, rather than in advance as step 1 above has it: his pre-made stem only touched the side panels here and there as he pulled them in.[87] This is a genuine disagreement with the recommended order, not a restatement of it.
- **Nail scrap between the parallel 4×4 sleepers** so they cannot move. While his hull sides were bonding to the bulkheads, someone tripped over a sleeper end, it shifted, and the sides set slightly out of true — corrected with a plane. Make a **trolley** at this stage to move the hull about.[87]
- **The foredeck may be easier in two pieces.** His rear deck went on without trouble; the foredeck would not, so he cut it along the line of bulkhead 2 and fitted it in two parts.[87]

He also questioned whether the **chine logs and sheer clamps** are needed at all, having halved the width of his sheer clamps to make them manageable: next time he would omit both, join bottom and deck to the sides by stitch and glue, nail only along the bulkhead lines, and let the sheathing turned over those joints carry the rest — see [chine log vs. taped seam](/construction/chine-log-vs-taped-seam.md).[87] On the plans' note that fastenings back up the glue bonds permanently, his reading of modern epoxy practice was that they are not structurally necessary, though he drove bronze nails anyway because they are, in his phrase, "awfully boaty."[87]

A further, independent voice reached the same order by a different route: make all the spars, rudder, and other removable gear first, while the workshop is still empty and uncluttered by the hull — the payoff being that once the hull itself is finished, the builder can go sailing immediately rather than facing a further round of small-parts work with a finished hull standing in the way.[94]

## Floor-member bevels, and a glue-oversize-then-bevel alternative

The **floor members** that attach to the bottom of the forward and aft bulkheads need the **same side and bottom bevels** as the bulkheads themselves.[7]

The ["Building the Paradox" articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) are essential to attempting the boat at all — "if it wasn't for the articles I would have never attempted building Paradox."[8] A builder-level variant on that sequence: rather than dry-fitting and tacking 2-3 times to bevel before gluing (step 4 above), **cut all parts oversize, including the floor members, then glue and nail everything first**, and cut the bevels into the assembled structure in one pass afterward.[8] This is a workable alternative worth weighing alongside the original method.[8]

Floor members **do need beveling** like the bulkheads — or, as a shortcut, can simply be **cut a little short** so they don't require beveling at all, an alternative to the cut-oversize-then-bevel method above.[7] The same convention is recorded on the drawings under the note **"Bottom Bevel Left Standing"**: both bulkhead and floor timber are beveled, most easily done by leaving both **proud** after gluing and cutting the bevel into both together in one pass. See [boom-end tack line holes and drilled-hole rot protection](/construction/boom-end-tack-line-holes.md) for that exchange.

Getting the proud amount exactly right does not matter, since either direction of error is recoverable: leave the timber a little proud of the bulkhead, guessing the amount by measuring off the plans rather than calculating it exactly, then plane off any excess later; too little proud instead is filled with thickened epoxy, and a badly misjudged cut can even be built back up with an extra glued-on strip of wood. The bevel's final shape is properly settled only during the trial fit — each critical stage assembled without glue and adjusted until it sits right, an assembly method that "doesn't allow you to get it wrong." Cutting the bevels into the bulkheads themselves before that trial fit, rather than after, is a documented cautionary case: none of the bevels came out right at the trial assembly, recoverable only because thickened epoxy filling the resulting gaps is stronger than the surrounding wood.[65]

## Turning the hull over

Turning the hull over — once the sides are assembled to the bulkheads and the sheer strakes are fitted — is described in the articles as "easily done without outside assistance," though in a **2 ft-clearance garage** it can in practice take **two people**, leaving open how the same hull will get turned back over once the bottom is fastened on. A temporary chine-log fitting technique (wedge-and-glue into an already-positioned joint) can come from the same session.[9]

Turning the hull is easy while it is still light — two people can lift an unturned, bottomless hull without trouble — but gets markedly harder once the bottom is fastened on and the assembly gains real weight. A bottomed hull can still be managed with a couple of pairs of roller skates underneath it and some luck. The structure also gains real rigidity only once the stem goes on: before that stage the hull is still noticeably floppy, becoming a single stiff structure only afterward.[10] One finished hull answers the same problem a different way: an overhead hook, rather than skates or a second pair of hands, tips and holds the hull to seal the bottom and again to lower the lead ballast into the bilges.[93]

Cushioning the hull's corners against the ground is worth planning for regardless of method: small boat-trailer tires — not steel-rimmed ones — worked as corner cushions with no resulting damage in one build.[11] A different builder's early turns instead relied on garbage bags stuffed with Christmas wrapping paper as padding, before switching to simple built frames partway through the build, judging the frames easier to live with given the sheer amount of associated work — most of it, in practice, just getting the hull on and off the trailer rather than the turn itself.[12] A frame built along these lines has since let a hull be turned over entirely single-handedly, demonstrated on video and independently pointed to by a second builder.[95]

## Elevating the hull to work underneath, before fitting the bottom

Once the chine logs are installed and the bottom panels scarfed together, fitting the bottom calls for access underneath the hull: to mark bulkhead and floor-member positions for pre-coating, to check that clamping pressure is reaching every joint for a proper epoxy squeeze-out, and to clean up that squeeze-out before it sets hard against the bulkheads and side panels. Elevating the hull on trestles high enough to crawl underneath is worth the extra hassle for this stage, rather than working with only a foot or so of clearance; a car inspection trolley is a workable way to slide underneath once elevated.[13][14][15]

### Fitting the chine runners hull-up or hull-down

A related sequencing question is whether the chine runners themselves go on more easily with the hull upside down or right side up. Shaping the runners is arguably easier with the hull right side up, but that order forces a second full turn-over to glass the bottom afterward — a genuine tradeoff rather than a clearly better choice either way. One builder's own recollection favors doing it right side up regardless, on the reasoning that holding the runner's bend in both planes at once proved impossible, so the runners ended up cut into roughly one-foot lengths and assembled like a three-dimensional jigsaw with generous epoxy — the same practical fix already recorded at [chine runner construction technique — Method 3, batten-and-fill](/chine-runners/construction.md#method-3-batten-and-fill).[14][15]

Wet-laying the fiberglass sheathing on each side of the hull, prepared over several preceding days, took about **two hours per side** for one builder working alone — a workload worth planning against a target turnover date, since the recommended 48-hour epoxy cure between sheathing and turning may have to be shortened under real-world time pressure (a helper's availability, a festival deadline) rather than always followed to the letter.[16]

A hull moved solo from a garage onto a driveway can be turned over without a crane or hoist, using materials already on hand: slide it out of the garage off temporary support blocks onto **round fence poles** used as rollers, flip it onto its side using a pair of long timbers, land it on a sheet of scrap plywood, flip it again onto its deck, then roll it back into the garage and up onto blocks once more. One such turn was done with **four people, all strong**, though the builder judged three could have managed it — a useful data point for scaling the moving crew to the number of helpers actually on hand, consistent with the crew-size guidance elsewhere on this page.[17]

With the hull upside down, the bottom is smoothed and then sheathed in the same **biaxial cloth** used on the sides, carried about an inch up the sides all round to protect the bottom edge and the chine runners — radiusing the chine beforehand allows the cloth to wrap that corner cleanly.[18] Small white spots can appear in the angle between a chine runner and the side, where the sheathing failed to bond fully into the corner and epoxy did not reach underneath the cloth — the fix is to **inject epoxy into the void rather than cut the spot out**, since cutting sacrifices the fiberglass cloth's own strength and continuity at that spot.[18][19]

## Assembling bulkheads out of sequence, and squaring the resulting box

When the stem's shape proves difficult to get right, or the bow will not pull in to meet it, that stage can be set aside in favor of assembling **bulkheads 2, 3, and 4 into a square box** first, using the floor panels and temporary bin sides — a workable deviation from the plans-book order. Squareness is checked by comparing the box's corner-to-corner diagonals; on one build these came out within 2 mm of each other once glued, clamped, and nailed — close enough to proceed.[20] Bulkhead 1 follows the same way once the box is square, gluing, clamping, and nailing it home in turn. A garage opening onto a roughly 30 ft by 12 ft concrete area gives enough room to **turn the hull over alone** at that stage, though a helper is likely needed once the bottom's added weight comes into play (see [Turning the hull over](#turning-the-hull-over) above for the general two-person-job finding this corroborates).[21][22]

A 2024 exchange added a further, independently worked-out bulkhead-gluing sequence and technique. One builder, ready to glue in his bulkheads, had locator blocks fixed with hot glue and a pair of large roof nails as lineup pins at the top and bottom of each bulkhead, ring nails standing by in pre-drilled holes though also weighing silicone/bronze screws instead; planned to start with bulkhead 3, was unsure of the order after that, and asked whether the hull needs to be disassembled and reassembled for the glue-up or whether the sides can simply be spread apart and the bulkhead glued into the resulting gap, intending to do the whole job in one session.[101] The answer: assemble and align everything except the stem first, without glue, and drill 6 mm alignment holes for wooden locating plugs through the sides into each bulkhead; then disassemble, and mount the bulkheads for the glue-up itself starting from bulkhead 3 and working outward — 3, 4, 2, the transom, then 1 — using ratchet straps to bend the sides into place and temporary wood screws to hold each bulkhead while the epoxy cures, removing the screws and filling their holes with epoxy once cured.[102] Timing matters as much as method: a day around 15°C keeps the epoxy from setting too quickly for a job of this size.[103]

## Truing the hull: the diagonal-comparison method

Dry-fitting the side panels to the bulkheads for a full trial assembly is, at bottom, simple pulling and pushing once checked against the same diagonal comparison used for the smaller 2-3-4 box above: at each section, compare the two corner-to-corner diagonals — for example, the port corner of the transom to where a given bulkhead meets the starboard side, against the starboard corner of the transom to where the same bulkhead meets the port side — and pull whichever measurement comes out longer until the pair is equal. Treating the aft half and the forward half of the boat as two separate tasks, each on the horizontal plane, keeps the job manageable; fitting the stem is a third, harder task of its own, needing attention to the vertical as well as the horizontal plane, though it answers to the same basic technique. Two people manage the job far more easily than one.[81] A second hull, built at a full 20% larger than the plans, confirmed the same method holds at a different scale, and added a refinement: check and true the diagonals separately at every section rather than assuming one setting carries through the whole boat, since different sections can be out of true by different amounts.[83] A further check applies once the bottom itself goes on: sighting a straight aluminium bar along the chine logs confirms they run level to each other and to the bulkheads over the full length, corrected with a block plane and finished with a sanding block. Before gluing a dry-fitted bottom on permanently, drilling a pair of witness-dowel holes through the bottom panel into a mid-length bulkhead lets the panel be relocated in exactly the same position for the glue-up, rather than trusting a second dry fit to land the same way.[84] Even a good fit leaves gaps where the chine logs pass through each bulkhead; filling them with thickened epoxy is expected as a matter of course rather than a sign of a poor fit, extending to the bulkheads the same reasoning already noted above for the chine log's stem joint.[85] A lighter-touch way to correct a single bulkhead's bevel during that same dry-fitting stage avoids a full teardown: pressing the sides a little way apart frees one bulkhead at a time to be pulled, adjusted, and reinstalled immediately with thickened epoxy, found easier than disassembling the whole box again.[86]

The moment a hull first goes three-dimensional under dry fit is not an experience unique to Paradox: a builder assembling an unrelated small-boat design independently reported the identical feeling.[82]

## Pulling a hull's sides together with a Spanish windlass

Beyond the hot-wet-blanket method (see [Recommended order](#recommended-order) above), a **"Spanish windlass"** is a further technique for pulling a hull's bow panels together: loop a length of fairly heavy rope loosely around the outside of both sides, like a large noose, then pass a round dowel or pipe under the loop and twist it — the loop gradually closes and draws the sides toward each other. Two temporary uprights installed just ahead of the loop stop it slipping forward as the sides close up, and two temporary scabs at the bow ends let clamps be applied and supported once the ends come together; once the bow is closed up, an inner stem shaped to fit can be installed, the clamps removed, and the stem profile shaped before fitting an outer stem piece. The windlass itself needs a helper to hold it in place unless it can be locked off — a turn or two of twine seized around the dowel and the rope loop does the job.[23]

The sharp angles and curves near the bow can make it very difficult for the rope loop to grip against, and a slipping Spanish windlass's twisting dowel can snap back fast enough to strike a hand or head — **ratchet straps** are a safer alternative for pulling sides together at the bow.[21]

A related difficulty showed up on a reduced, 85%-of-plan-scale build: pulling the sides in with a ratchet strap only at the top and bottom of the bow left the middle of each side panel bulging outward, since the strap turns the bow's cross-section from a rectangle toward an oval rather than drawing the panels to a point at the stem. Zip-ties applied around the panels partway up the bow, in addition to the strap already in place, drew them together at the middle as well and closed the gap the strap alone had left.[88] A different method available for the same job disassembles the hull first: unscrew each bulkhead and the transom on alternate sides so the frame stays upright without falling over, then reassemble stem-first, clamping, gluing, and nailing progressively back toward the transom — splaying the sides wide open at the stem rather than fighting them into place at the very end — guided by a preliminary dry fit with the sides bent around the boat's widest beam to locate the guiding screws this needs. Because clamps tend to slip on the stem's curved timber, drywall screws driven in immediately ahead of each clamp give it something to bear against; the resulting screw holes are no concern on a hull due to be sheathed in fiberglass regardless.[89]

## Finding the documented build sequence

Beyond what the plans themselves show, the fullest documented build sequence is the paid 16-part "Building Paradox" article series, rich with pictures, sketches, and tips, obtainable from the author by e-mail.[24] A spare plans CD containing the sequence has also circulated among builders.

## Bulkhead #3 has no side cleats, by design

Bulkhead #3 — the 18 mm ply bulkhead in the middle of the cabin, the only one with no deck beam — shows no **side cleats** in the plans, unlike the other bulkheads. This is correct **by design, not an oversight**: the side panel **glues (and nails) directly onto the edge** of bulkhead #3 itself, so no separate cleat is needed there — the other bulkheads carry deck beams and therefore need cleats to support them.[25] See [deck and cabin beam](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md) dimensions for the related question this point was first raised alongside.

A 2009 exchange confirmed the detail from an actual build: the nails at bulkhead 3 go in as two locating pins per side, into pre-drilled holes in the bulkhead's own exposed edge, rather than into a cleat as at every other bulkhead station.[26] This looked, to more than one experienced builder encountering it for the first time, like a departure from ordinary plywood-boat practice — a bulkhead-to-hull joint is more commonly made with fiberglass tape and glue than a nail through a panel's edge, with edge-nailing more characteristic of traditional rib-and-plank construction, done through wood-framed ribs before the outer skin goes on — until the objection was withdrawn on recognizing bulkhead 3's documented exception above.[27][28]

## Transom and bulkhead #4 crown, and fitting bevels at trial assembly

The plans are inconsistent about whether the **transom**'s side framing (and **bulkhead #4**) should carry any **crown** (compound curvature), rather than being flat: different plan sheets show it differently — one page draws the transom framing flat, another shows a significant crown with a correction line, and a third shows an unspecified ~5 mm crown.[29] In practice this inconsistency does not matter much: a **transom crown on the order of 5 mm** is trivial, easily removed during the dry run if it turns out to be a problem, and a matching crown is reasonable to carry through to bulkhead #4 if the transom and bulkhead #3 both have it.[30]

Builders differ on how precisely to shape bevels (including the **stem bevel**, which can run to a steep angle — as much as 29 degrees at its maximum)[29] ahead of trial assembly. One approach is to hold off on any bevel adjustment until the **dry fit**, since cutting a bevel before knowing where it needs to "roll" risks having to redo it.[30] A looser, faster alternative: do not fuss over shaping bevels precisely at all beforehand — instead, do the **trial assembly first**, then take the bevels off freehand with an **angle grinder and sanding disc** once the parts are offered up together. Any resulting gaps are simply filled with a bit more epoxy. The larger point behind this looser approach: getting a Paradox built and sailing matters more than chasing perfect joinery — "it's too much fun to sail to spend forever building it."[31]

A 2014 newcomer starting construction asked whether the transom sides should be curved and what size the bulkhead #4 side cleat should be.[69] They should be curved, sized either straight off the drawing or, more likely, matching the same 5 mm crown already shown at bulkhead 1; a **25 x 35 mm** cleat — the same size used at bulkheads 1 and 2 — is not a critical dimension, since its only job is giving the nails something to bite into. Bevels on the cleats are best left uncut until trial assembly of the sides rather than formed in advance, since a bevel cut too early only has to be redone once the true angle is known, and the transom's own cleat bevels end up standing proud, wider than the plywood they meet, rather than short of it. The same reply restated the broader sequencing advice already recorded above ([Recommended order](#recommended-order)): make all of a boat's smaller parts — rudder, mast, yard — before finishing the hull, so a completed hull is not left waiting on the odd bits still needed to actually sail it.[70]

Putting that same make-the-small-parts-first advice into practice a few weeks later, that same newcomer had all the bulkheads cut and the deck beams and cleats close to finished, framing the remaining work as "a couple more years" before sailing — met with brief encouragement in reply.[77][78]

The same reassurance recurs whenever a newly arrived set of plans is first read closely: the stem piece block's dimension for its back face is not tabulated on sheet 7, leaving a first-time reader wondering whether it must be worked out by trigonometry from the sheet's 29-degree cross-section — but the plans do not need to be trusted to that precision, since cutting oversize and trimming the bevel freehand at trial assembly is straightforward once actually attempted, "epoxy is your friend" even against a stem bevel calculated exactly in 3D CAD beforehand.[32][33][4]

A worthwhile habit when templating parts like the **rudder**: step back and recheck the work after a break, rather than pushing on while tired. A rudder template can drift into showing a plumb transom rather than the plans' raked one — a fatigue-caused drafting slip, not a plans error — the kind of mistake best caught by comparing again against the design the next day.[34]

## Finding bulkhead-member angles from the drawings

The plans do not tabulate a cutting angle for every stick used on the bulkhead sides — angles are simply the same **at any scale**, so a builder can lift them directly off the drawing with a **bevel square**: set the square against the drawn lines, then either mark and cut the timber to that angle or transfer the setting straight to a saw.[35] A looser alternative is to leave the pieces a little long, assemble them, and **plane or fair the bevels afterward** with a batten. The lift-from-drawing method can still leave some angles wrong on the first try, needing the gaps padded out with a strip of wood and re-cut — suggesting the looser after-assembly fit may be easier in practice.[36] Neither approach needs to be especially precise, since epoxy fills the difference.

## Bevel order for the chine log and sheer clamp: cut before or after installing

For the chine log and sheer clamp, the bevels are best cut **before** installing each log rather than shaped **after** it is already in place — cutting them in place afterward is harder.[37] One builder installed his own chine logs only after the hull sides were already joined, doing so partly to help fair the hull's curve, with sailmaking (5.5 oz dacron) and epoxy-coating the interior proceeding in parallel.[38] A fuller method: cut the chine log and sheer clamp to the profiles shown on plan sheet 5, spring the sides into position onto the bulkheads once fitted, then trim the bottom and deck to fit as they go on. Bulkhead bevel angles come off plan sheet 2, though in practice they need real adjustment (including adding material back) during trial assembly — better handled by cutting bulkhead cleats **oversize** and taking the final angle off during that trial fit, rather than trusting the plan angle exactly. Buying a metric tape measure and working entirely in millimetres, rather than converting back and forth, is "much easier" once committed to, since millimetre measurements come out in tens rather than twelfths.[38][39]

## How the chine logs and sheer clamps join the stem

The **chine logs** and **sheer clamps** attach to the **stem** the same way they attach to the transom: both members are inlet/notched into the stem, per drawing #4. The notch's cut angle need not be perfect, since thickened epoxy ends up stronger than the wood. See [notching the stem for chine logs and sheer clamps](/construction/stem-notching.md) for the fuller technique.

## Bulkhead construction details not spelled out on the plans

Working through the bulkhead drawings sheet by sheet answers most of the bulkhead-construction detail not otherwise spelled out on the plans:

**Bulkhead 1.** Both the bottom and side edges are beveled to fit the panel each joins; where the plans show a 5 mm curve in the sides, both the plywood and its cleat share that same curve. The forward opening's curve is not dimensioned — cut it "artistic," staying large enough to fit a head through. Deck beams, if laminated, come out oversize and are trimmed back to the plywood edge regardless of whether they land over or between the side cleats, so the order does not much matter. The vertical cleats seen in some builders' photos but not on the plans are a plans omission — the port cleat should line up with bulkhead 2's cleat, and the starboard cleat can be set independently (one builder used 220 mm from the centerline, successfully).[40] A 2022 recurrence of the same question narrowed exactly where the gap sits in the drawing set: the cleats for the port and starboard lockers appear on Drawings #3 and #4 but are absent from Drawing #7, the bulkhead's own detail sheet.[96]

**Bulkhead 2.** The limber hole is for drainage, and should be cut generous enough to get a finger through for filleting the joint once the bottom is on. Where the plans show a 6 mm ply "scab" under a cleat, the cleat can simply be fitted directly over the scab, though the hatch must then be cut to clear it. The diameter symbol on the tiller-rope and wiring holes is the standard ⌀ mark; the wiring hole's size is up to the builder — running extra holes through each bulkhead early, for wires and hoses not yet planned, is easier than drilling and sealing them into a finished boat later, at the cost of them rarely ending up exactly where wanted.[40] The vertical cleats are beveled roughly 45° on top for safety. The V-shaped filler piece between the V-shaped cleats is solid wood, not plywood, and becomes part of the mast step.[40] The "2 mm dado" notation on the plans defeated interpretation and was simply ignored without ill effect — the term itself was later explained on the list: a **dado** is a groove sawn or routered into wood, sized to take a mating piece of the same width, comparable to the mortice in a mortice-and-tenon joint, and cut either with a dado blade mounted in a table saw or a straight bit in a router. The same term appears in the tank-divider notation on Drawing #8; see [water tank construction — an off-centre tank divider](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#an-off-centre-tank-divider-confirmed-correct-against-the-plans).[41][42][43]

**Bulkheads 3 and 4, and the transom.** Bulkhead 3, at 18 mm ply, needs no side cleats — the side panel glues and nails directly to its edge (see [bulkhead #3 has no side cleats, by design](#bulkhead-3-has-no-side-cleats-by-design) above for the earlier, shorter version of this same answer). Bulkhead 4's sides are beveled despite showing no side curve on the drawing, as is the transom's, which is likewise undimensioned there; all bulkhead sides and bottoms are beveled to match the angle of the plywood panel fastened to them, regardless of whether a curve is separately called out. The deck beam's height where undimensioned works out to about 57 mm, following the curvature shared by all the deck beams — laminate the beams first, then cut plywood to match. The tiller opening's shape is not templated on the drawings; instead, drill pilot holes to locate the baffle cleat during assembly, then cut and carve the opening to clear the tiller once the rudder is hung and can be swung side to side to check clearance.[40]

A 2024 newcomer working only from Drawings #7 and #8 — which show bulkheads 1 through 4 — found no separate transom drawing there and worked out a rough estimate from other sheets instead: Drawing #4 puts the transom at roughly 850-900 mm at its widest point, and Drawing #1 shows it angled slightly outward; absent anything more exact, scaling down bulkhead 4 was floated as a fallback guess.[98] The transom does have its own half-drawing on the plans; it is easy to miss because it sits nested inside bulkhead #3's own drawing on Drawing #8, oriented sideways relative to it, rather than appearing as a separate sheet of its own.[99][100]

**General reassurance.** Precision is not the point: most of the hull goes together by fitting and adjusting until parts sit right — pack a gap with an added piece, or cut back and refit an oversize one — "and always remember that epoxy is stronger than wood, so all those gaps in the joints just make it stronger." One practical trick: nails needing a 2.5 mm pilot hole can be temporarily driven as **dowel pins** (from a spare bag of 2.5 mm wire nails) to locate two parts being joined, then pulled and replaced with the permanent bronze ring nails once the rest of the fasteners are in.[40]

A **carline** — a term that stumped a newcomer — is one of the fore-and-aft horizontal beams supporting the deck between bulkheads 2 and 4, under the side windows (shown on the plans' deck plan, port side).[40]

## Why the bulkhead-side curve exists, and fixing one already cut straight

Having cut bulkhead #3 with straight sides only, missing the 5 mm curve the plans call for (see [bulkhead 1](#bulkhead-construction-details-not-spelled-out-on-the-plans) above for the same dimension), a builder asked why Matt Layden specified it at all — for looks, for locker space, for hydrodynamic efficiency when heeled, or for strength through a compound curve — and how best to recover the missing curvature short of buying new plywood.[71] The curve adds real strength to the bulkhead frame.[72] It also exists because the hull's own side panels are curved in the third dimension rather than flat, so a bulkhead's side edge has to match that shape to fit the panel properly: plywood as thick as a bulkhead's side member can only be bent into a conical or cylindrical section, and the side panel's own curve and twist forces a conic section onto the bulkhead.[73][74] A bulkhead left with straight sides can leave the side panel not touching it at every point along the joint — properly nailing the joint and filling any remaining gaps with thickened epoxy, or simply shimming the bulkhead's sides, keeps the mismatch from mattering in practice.[74] The transom is drawn curved on the plans too, though — consistent with it being undimensioned there, noted above — no actual dimension is given for it.[75] The curve's own structural job is stiffness: it keeps the side panels from flexing under a push from a crew member or a dock.[76] For a bulkhead already cut straight, the fix is to glue cleats onto both sides to build the missing curve back up and fill the resulting gap with epoxy — the cured epoxy ends up stronger than the surrounding plywood, so the repair leaves the bulkhead stronger than the design called for in the first place, and the small extra weight it adds is offset simply by carrying a little less lead ballast.[76] Put into practice, that cleat-and-epoxy repair is best glued onto the bulkhead's aft face rather than the forward one, purely so the filled gap does not sit in view from the helm — a cosmetic precaution that in any case turns out unnecessary once the boat's two-inch foam insulation and flotation goes in against the bulkhead, since it hides the joint completely.[79][80]

## Clarifying the bevel-and-cleat relationship, and a rebuild-it-differently alternative

Where the plans show a curve (say 5 mm) on a bulkhead edge, the straight line on the drawing is merely a reference: the cleat itself runs right to the plywood's edge, sharing the same curve, and both are beveled together to fit the panel each joins.[44]

Curving and beveling an edge in one operation is difficult enough that a rebuild-it-differently alternative is worth considering: cut the plywood to size and let the cleats **overlap the panel edge**, rather than trying to fit them flush. Once the sides are offered up to the bulkheads for trial assembly, the correct bevel angle becomes obvious and can be cut at that stage. Using **dowel pins** (see the temporary-locating-pin trick above) to hold the bulkheads in position, they can then be removed and refitted repeatedly without the whole structure collapsing. Working outward from **bulkhead 3** is the natural starting point, since — per its no-side-cleats construction above — it carries no bevel to begin with.[44]

## What a "scab" is, and a scarphed alternative that eliminates it

A **scab**, in plywood construction, is simply another term for a **butt block** — a reinforcing patch glued over a joint. On the Paradox plans, the one builders most often ask about is the 6 mm ply scab called out on **bulkhead #2** (see the "Bulkhead 2" answer above).[45]

A builder can eliminate that scab entirely by **scarphing bulkhead #2's plywood together** instead of butting and patching it — practical only with plywood of sufficiently high quality that the front and back veneers are properly matched and joined.[45]

Regarding the surrounding bevel work generally: every edge shown beveled on the plans, in both plywood and solid wood, can be measured and marked directly off the drawings before cutting, then cut on a **bandsaw** without particular difficulty, yielding a near-perfect fit — with the exception of bulkhead #2's sides, which carry a **rolling bevel** that is genuinely tough to get exact without full-size lofting. The recommended order is to cut the bulkheads and transom first, then cut and add the cleats and floors. Cutting a bevel takes no more time than cutting square, so there is no need to treat beveling as excessive care — tight joinery is simply preferred over joinery-filled-with-epoxy-and-hoping, though epoxy does forgive a fair amount of looseness if a builder prefers to work faster. The degree of fit and finish achieved is ultimately up to the individual builder.[45]

## Which side of the station line a bulkhead's dimensioned face falls on

A recurring point of confusion, worth stating plainly: on "instant"-boat jigs and most station-mold designs, frames are placed so that beveling is unnecessary in the first place — a forward station mold sits **aft of** its station line. The Paradox plans work the opposite way: a forward bulkhead's **dimensioned face lies forward of its station line** (true for both the bottom and side panels, though not the floors), which is exactly why beveling is necessary rather than optional on this design. So long as the plans' specified edge of the bulkhead falls on the station line, the resulting hull geometry comes out as designed, regardless of which face is treated as the dimensioned one — a bevel can be cut precisely from the plotted lines and glued directly, or cut slightly oversize and backfilled with epoxy filler; both give the same final geometry.[46]

Reading the drawings correctly means checking, sheet by sheet, which face of each bulkhead the plans dimension **from** and where its bevel rolls **toward**: bulkheads 1 and 2 sit forward of their stations, while bulkheads 3 and 4 sit aft. Sheets 7 and 8 label each bulkhead drawing "view from aft" or "view from fwd" — for bulkhead #1, the plans dimension the **after** face, which is the face that lies on the station line, so the bulkhead is cut straight to the plotted lines. Bevels themselves are taken off sheet 2, where some bevels roll from chine to sheer; plotting and marking all of a boat's bevels from sheet 2 in one sitting lets a builder cut cleats and floors afterward just by glancing at the marked reference, rather than recalculating each one in turn.[47]

Bulkhead cleats illustrate why getting this right matters: one builder's cleats measured **35 mm at their widest**, not their narrowest, dimension — camber cut into both the inboard and outboard faces (5-7 mm, per the design spec at each bulkhead) reduces an oversize blank down to its final width against the bulkhead, with the inboard face left at 90 degrees to the bulkhead (parallel to the centerline) and only the outboard face beveled to match the bulkhead's own edge. Cutting cleats to their finished 35 mm width from the outset, skipping the camber step, leaves them too narrow once beveled — a shortfall that shows up as an unsightly gap once the foam board insulation is fitted against the hull. On one build, the resulting **least-width** cleat measurements (after camber and bevel) were: bulkhead 1, 25 mm; bulkhead 2, 30 mm; bulkhead 4, 33 mm; bulkhead 3 needs no cleats at all (see [bulkhead #3 has no side cleats, by design](#bulkhead-3-has-no-side-cleats-by-design) above); the transom's cleats are 45 mm wide as drawn, since the transom carries no bevel. These figures are specific to one builder's cuts and should not be used to derive bevel angles for another hull — the bevels themselves come from sheet 2, not from a cleat's finished width.[48]

Working out this relationship in the abstract is harder than doing it: in practice, studying the plans sheet by sheet answers nearly every question of this kind, though some points genuinely benefit from another builder's confirmation before the first cut. Photographing and uploading in-progress work to a shared photo album is itself a minor technical hurdle worth pushing through — once the knack is found, the resulting photos are "really helpful... for those trying to figure out how things go together."[48]

## What a rolling bevel is, and cutting bulkhead #2's

A **rolling bevel** is an edge whose bevel angle changes — "rolls" — continuously along its length, rather than holding one constant angle from end to end. The design's most conspicuous rolling bevels are on the **chine runners**; the next most noticeable are the **side bevels of bulkheads #2 and #3**. On sheet 2 of the plans, at station #2, the "sheer" and "design WL" reference lines cross the station line at visibly different angles — that difference in angle is the bevel's roll. On one builder's own bulkhead #2, the cut bevel measured **8 degrees at the sheer and 13 degrees at the chine**.[49]

That particular roll is not extreme, and cutting it is straightforward: cut the whole bevel on a **bandsaw** to the shallower angle (8 degrees in this example), then hand-plane the roll down toward the chine to the steeper angle (13 degrees) — easy work, since the parts involved are light and manageable. With a helper available and the bandsaw already set up for bevel cuts, one builder can start the cut while calling out the changing bevel angle for the helper to dial in on the saw as the cut proceeds — a technique that takes some practice and teamwork to do smoothly. A tablesaw is a (considerable overkill) alternative for a well-equipped shop; a carefully guided jigsaw or skilsaw can also do the job, though a bandsaw is preferred over either. Because epoxy and fillers are part of the build regardless, cutting the shallower angle only (8 degrees, in the example above) and simply filling the resulting minor gap on the forward side of the joint is an acceptable shortcut, in place of chasing the full roll exactly.[49]

The **chine runners** are harder: they carry **two** rolling bevels rather than one, demanding constant re-checking to keep the work correct. Installing the runners' filler pieces in **short, non-rolling segments**, or as **long laminations**, are the two alternate approaches — each with its own tradeoffs, and each builder's own situation should decide which is the "right" choice for a given boat.[49] See [chine runner construction](/chine-runners/construction.md) for the fuller build technique.

## Fit at trial assembly rather than pre-cutting a perfect bevel

The chine log's drawn shape on sheet 5 — not flat on the bottom, and its flare far short of the hull's nominal 16-degree figure partway along its length — can look inconsistent against the bulkhead #3 detail on the same sheet.[50] In fact the bevel is drawn correctly at the chine log's **ends** but not at the **middle**, by design rather than error: once the chine logs are in place, they and the bulkheads are meant to be **faired together** so the bottom panel fits, which is easier to do at that later stage than trying to pre-cut every bevel exactly right and assemble the parts hoping they land true. This reflects the design's general build philosophy — rather than the "get every part perfect, then assemble and hope it fits" approach, Paradox is meant to be built by **assembling each stage to what has already been made**, bevel-perfect or not, which guarantees a fit at every step.[51]

A related, separate detail is an extra **triangular section** at the chine log's bottom.[52] This is simply **another rolling bevel**: as drawn, chine logs and sheer clamps alike are cut to a **constant** bevel. Only once the chine logs are glued to the sides and bulkheads are they **dressed off with a straight edge** to accept the flat bottom — the extra triangular portion is removed at that later stage, wherever necessary, exactly as the sheer clamps are separately faired to the deck beams to accept the deck. The reference corner on the chine log — the lower, outboard corner — stays untouched along the log's whole length and always tracks the side panel's edge; the sheer clamp's equivalent untouched corner is its upper, outboard one. Worked out frame by frame from the **lines drawing (sheet 2)**:[53] the outer corner angle is always greater than 90 degrees to fit between the side panel and the bottom, the chine logs attach with that section held constant, and the logs get shaped to let the bottom fit snugly only once the bottom itself is offered up.[54][55] The angle is needed because the plans' 48 mm chine-log measurement is not itself vertical; leaving the bottom perfectly square instead would only shift the problem onto the bulkheads, whose cutouts would then need varying depths to minimize fairing elsewhere — though in practice, given how the rest of the assembly proceeds, the distinction matters little.[56] Sawing off a short length of scrap sheer clamp and chine log to physically handle and visualize the relationship before cutting the real parts is useful groundwork for deciding where to cut the bulkheads, transom, and stem.[54]

Working through the geometry this way shows it is in fact easier to **glue the chine log down flat against the side first**, then cut the rolling bevel into the **bottom face** only — straightforward there because that face is straight across — rather than trying to cut a rolling bevel into the log's side before installation. This matches the design intent exactly, with the same parallel holding for the sheer clamp. Clarity comes with proceeding: the building process is, in practice, fairly intuitive once under way.[57][58]

A 2022–23 build tested the pre-calculating approach on the **bulkhead cleats** and reached the same conclusion the hard way, which is worth recording because the arithmetic itself is sound and only the strategy is wrong. Working from a **scale print of the plans** with the X and Y scales preserved, the builder drew a **tangent** at each cleat-to-side junction and a second line **parallel to the centreline**, and solved the resulting triangle for the bevel — finding every angle fairly close to 90°, the largest excursion about **18°**. Cut to those figures, the cleats all came out **slightly too steep**: shallower angles nearer 90° would have needed less fiddling and less epoxy fill, and the method cannot capture the fact that the angle at the top of the boat is not the angle at the bottom, because of the panels' curvature. Six months on, his verdict was that pre-calculating and pre-cutting the bevels had been the mistake outright, and that the angles are better taken **off the boat as it comes together** — worth calculating, perhaps, as a check that nothing is badly out, but not worth cutting to.[66][67]

## Gluing up the hull: one sitting or several, and locating the bulkheads with temporary pins

A recurring worry at the gluing-up stage is whether the whole hull needs to go together in a single marathon session, or whether it can safely be built up bulkhead by bulkhead over several sittings without the earlier glue joints losing symmetry before the last one is made.

The key preparation happens **before** any epoxy is mixed: get a good dry fit first, with the major nail holes already drilled, and make sure the boat is square at that stage. Temporary cleats or blocks should be screwed to the side panels at each bulkhead station, angled so the bulkheads sit at right angles to the centreline; sheathing them in plastic bin liner stops them sticking to the panels and frames once epoxy is applied. A combination of straps, ropes, clamps, and shaped wooden blocks helps spring the bow and stern sections into place. Arrange every item within easy reach before starting the wet fit, then work from the middle out: fit the side panels to bulkhead 3 first, then 2, then 4, applying epoxy and nailing each in place before moving to the next; do 1 and the transom next, and the stem last. One full hull went together this way in a single session of about two hours; because the symmetry was already established at the dry-fit stage, the wet assembly went into place automatically, confirmed with a line run down the centre of the boat with no adjustment needed.[59][60][61]

A second, independently developed technique achieves the same result with less time pressure, and does not require gluing in one sitting at all: drill the pilot holes needed for the ring nails during trial assembly, and once a bulkhead sits correctly, drill a couple of extra holes each side and push in **temporary wire nails** as locating pins — a size that happens to be a nice push fit in ring-nail pilot holes. This lets the hull be disassembled and reassembled repeatedly in exactly the same position, and lets every other ring-nail hole be pre-drilled before any glue is mixed. At final assembly, start the ring nails into all the side-panel holes except the ones holding wire-nail pins, then glue bulkhead 3 first, positioning it with the wire nails before driving the ring nails home (finishing with the pins' own holes last), and work outward toward the stem, finishing bulkhead 2. Because each joint is nailed as it goes, it does not matter whether an earlier joint has fully cured before the next is made — a nailed joint cannot move regardless. When fitting the stem, sight a line from the centre of the transom across the centre of each bulkhead to the centre of the stem itself, to avoid ending up with a banana-shaped hull. The wire-nail-as-tooling-pin trick is reusable at every later stage of construction, not just this one.[62]

The wire-nail pin technique specifically solves a problem that can otherwise go unrecognized until the first side panel is already glued: using ordinary screws instead of nails as locating pins can let a panel bite into the stringers slightly out of true even when the screw holes do not quite line up on re-driving, producing a first panel that is subtly misaligned — discovered only once the glue had already set, and correctable only because the panel had deliberately been left oversize and could be planed down to fit afterward. Switching to finishing nails as locating pins for the remaining panels avoided the problem entirely, with every subsequent panel fitting perfectly.[63]

A 2022 build reached the same finding from the opposite direction. Trial-assembling and then gluing up his hull with **short drywall screws** as temporary anchors, the builder found they held the parts together perfectly well but **did not index** — they could not be relied on to bring a bulkhead back to exactly the position it had occupied at the dry fit — and concluded afterward that a pin system of the kind Don Elliott's guide describes "would probably be useful to make this a lot less stressful." Two further lessons came out of the same glue-up: **alignment blocks are worth screwing down on both sides** of bulkhead 3 rather than one, since it carries no side cleats to locate it and the slight lean that resulted is permanent; and epoxy needs **thickening considerably more for the vertical joints of a hull glue-up** than for the flat bench work that precedes it, or the fillets slump. An extra pair of hands is worth having, though the helper will spend long stretches idle.[68]

## Bilge and tank divider order, and a dowel-registration technique for the bottom panel

Bilge and tank dividers are best fitted **before** the bottom panel goes on, not after — trying to fit them afterward, with the bottom already in place, does not work.[90][92] Checking the dividers with a straight bar laid crosswise over the chine logs confirms everything between the chine logs sits lower, as it should.[91]

For locating the bottom panel itself, a centerline marked on the bottom and on each bulkhead, together with a pair of dowel holes drilled through the bottom into the centermost bulkhead ahead of time, lets the panel be positioned exactly once glue is applied: lower the bottom onto the dowels first, with one person holding down each end, catching the dowel holes already in the right place with no glue smeared where it shouldn't be, then work the fastening outward toward both ends. This is the same registration principle as the wire-nail tooling pins described above, applied to the bottom panel specifically rather than the bulkheads.[91]

One builder's own bottom-panel installation went bow to stern rather than working from the centre outward: nailing and gluing began at the bow, with a second pair of hands sitting on the panel to pull the last of it down at the stern — a job he found easier overall than the sheer strake and chine logs, the latter suggested as better fitted in several laminations rather than as single solid pieces. A powered mixer kept one batch of epoxy being prepared while the previous batch was still being applied, avoiding a dead stop between batches during the glue-up.[97]

## Epoxy-coating the bulkheads and side panels: before or after the wet assembly

Coating the bulkheads and the inside of the side panels with epoxy can just as easily be done **after** the wet (glued) assembly as before, provided the deck has not yet been fitted — access through the open deck opening is enough. Coating afterward, rather than before committing to the wet build, has a real advantage: the epoxy joints between the side panels and the bulkheads/frames get a good, clean bonding surface to key into, and the chine logs and sheer strakes likewise get a better bonding surface than if the surrounding wood had already been sealed.[64]
