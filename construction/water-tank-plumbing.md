# Water tank plumbing — fill, vent, and suction

The Paradox carries **two separate water tanks** which serve as both drinking-water supply and water ballast. Each is plumbed with two runs the plans name directly on the drawings: a **tank fill and suction** line, and a **tank vent line**.[1] The tanks can be filled from an external source or replenished by rain water, and either way the plumbing has to work in both directions — when filling or pumping out, a tank **must be vented to expel air and to receive it**.[2]

## Everything goes in before the tank top closes

The whole of the fill, suction, and vent installation is a **before-the-tank-top job**.[2] Once the top is glued and filleted down the tank becomes an integral part of the hull structure, and the only access left is through its inspection port. This is the same closing-off logic that governs the [cabin structure](/components/pilothouse.md#it-goes-on-last-because-afterwards-there-is-no-working-room) and the [breast hook](/components/breast-hook.md#the-bow-insulation-must-go-in-before-the-breast-hook): the sequence is fixed by what each step makes unreachable. For the tank top itself, see [water tank construction and lining](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#fixing-the-tank-tops-in-place).

## Vent lines: high, and as near the centreline as possible

The vent lines are run **high on the tanks and as close to the boat's centreline as they can be got**, and the reason is a sailing reason rather than a plumbing one: sited that way they cannot be blocked **at any angle of heel**, so they go on venting overboard in any condition.[2] A vent that submerges when the boat lies over is a vent that stops working exactly when the tank is being relied on as ballast.

Two practical cautions attach to the vent run. The **180-degree bend** the vent pipe has to take is hard to make — the instruction is simply to keep at it — and the finished run must be checked so that **it will not protrude and interfere with the mast**.[2]

In use, the vents double as the tank's own fill gauge: topping up through a deck fill, the tank is full when water is heard **gurgling out of the vents**.[3]

## The suction pipe stands 6 mm off the bottom

When installing the **pumpout (suction) pipe**, the open end is kept **6 mm off the bottom of the tank**, so it cannot pick up any dirt that has settled there.[2] It is a small dimension with a direct consequence for drinking water, and it is the kind of detail that cannot be corrected once the tank top is on.

## Fitting the tubes: rough, glue, and fillet both sides

All the holes are carefully marked and drilled first. Each tube's surface is then **roughened with 60 grit sandpaper** before it goes in — a bonding step, not a cosmetic one — and the tube glued in place. After the putty stiffens, a **finger fillet** is worked around the pipe: glove off, a small amount of thickened epoxy on the fingertip, smoothed out to build a strong, watertight fillet around the pipe **on both sides** of the panel it passes through. Hands are washed well afterwards, and any residue washed off once the epoxy has cured.[2] For the general technique this belongs to, see [epoxy fillets](/components/epoxy-fillets.md) and [epoxy safety and personal protection](/construction/epoxy-safety-and-ppe.md).

## Pipe material and size

The plans draw the fill/suction line in **12 mm copper pipe**.[3] Where a builder prefers plastic, **CPVC is preferred over PVC** for the fill and suction line, being smaller in outside diameter for the same bore.[4] The tanks' own **breather tubes** are likewise made from small-gauge copper pipe.[5]

## In service: filling, drawing, and draining

The installed plumbing is what the day-to-day routines run on, and those routines are covered in full at [transom deck plate — filling the water tank](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#the-fillsuction-copper-pipe-sizing-timing-and-the-fittings-that-connect-to-it): filling through a deck fitting outside the hull with a funnel, roughly 5 to 10 minutes a side from a tap and about twice that using a 12-volt bilge pump in reverse; the hazard of **forcing water in fast enough to blow an inspection hatch off**; and two ways of draining, either by hosing the galley pump's spigot out through the vent opening or by hosing the fill spout and pumping until the tank siphons itself dry.

One point of designer practice is worth recording against all of this: **Matt Layden draws water from his own tanks with a fuel bulb rather than a pump**, which he says goes a long way toward encouraging fresh-water conservation.[4]
