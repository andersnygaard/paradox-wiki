# Water tank plumbing — fill, vent, and suction

The Paradox carries **two separate water tanks** which serve as both drinking-water supply and water ballast. Each is plumbed with two runs the plans name directly on the drawings: a **tank fill and suction** line, and a **tank vent line**.[1] The tanks can be filled from an external source or replenished by rain water, and either way the plumbing has to work in both directions — when filling or pumping out, a tank **must be vented to expel air and to receive it**.[2]

## Everything goes in before the tank top closes

The whole of the fill, suction, and vent installation is a **before-the-tank-top job**.[2] Once the top is glued and filleted down the tank becomes an integral part of the hull structure, and the only access left is through its inspection port. This is the same closing-off logic that governs the [cabin structure](/components/pilothouse.md#it-goes-on-last-because-afterwards-there-is-no-working-room) and the [breast hook](/components/breast-hook.md#the-bow-insulation-must-go-in-before-the-breast-hook): the sequence is fixed by what each step makes unreachable. For the tank top itself, see [water tank construction and lining](/construction/water-tank-lining.md#fixing-the-tank-tops-in-place).

## Vent lines: high, and as near the centreline as possible

The vent lines are run **high on the tanks and as close to the boat's centreline as they can be got**, and the reason is a sailing reason rather than a plumbing one: sited that way they cannot be blocked **at any angle of heel**, so they go on venting overboard in any condition.[2] A vent that submerges when the boat lies over is a vent that stops working exactly when the tank is being relied on as ballast.

Two practical cautions attach to the vent run. The **180-degree bend** the vent pipe has to take is hard to make — the instruction is simply to keep at it — and the finished run must be checked so that **it will not protrude and interfere with the mast**.[2]

In use, the vents double as the tank's own fill gauge: topping up through a deck fill, the tank is full when water is heard **gurgling out of the vents**.[3]

## The suction pipe stands 6 mm off the bottom

When installing the **pumpout (suction) pipe**, the open end is kept **6 mm off the bottom of the tank**, so it cannot pick up any dirt that has settled there.[2] It is a small dimension with a direct consequence for drinking water, and it is the kind of detail that cannot be corrected once the tank top is on.

## Fitting the tubes: rough, glue, and fillet both sides

All the holes are carefully marked and drilled first. Each tube's surface is then **roughened with 60 grit sandpaper** before it goes in — a bonding step, not a cosmetic one — and the tube glued in place. After the putty stiffens, a **finger fillet** is worked around the pipe: glove off, a small amount of thickened epoxy on the fingertip, smoothed out to build a strong, watertight fillet around the pipe **on both sides** of the panel it passes through. Hands are washed well afterwards, and any residue washed off once the epoxy has cured.[2] For the general technique this belongs to, see [epoxy fillets](/components/epoxy-fillets.md) and [epoxy safety and personal protection](/construction/epoxy-safety-and-ppe.md). One builder's own account of this step is blunter about the real difficulty involved: expecting simply to "drill four holes," drilling them through the sides of the water tanks in practice proved not nearly so easy.[15]

## Pipe material and size

The plans draw the fill/suction line in **12 mm copper pipe**.[3] Where a builder prefers plastic, **CPVC is preferred over PVC** for the fill and suction line, being smaller in outside diameter for the same bore.[4] The tanks' own **breather tubes** are likewise made from small-gauge copper pipe.[5]

## In service: filling, drawing, and draining

The installed plumbing is what the day-to-day routines run on, and those routines are covered in full at [transom deck plate — filling the water tank](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#the-fillsuction-copper-pipe-sizing-timing-and-the-fittings-that-connect-to-it): filling through a deck fitting outside the hull with a funnel, roughly 5 to 10 minutes a side from a tap and about twice that using a 12-volt bilge pump in reverse; the hazard of **forcing water in fast enough to blow an inspection hatch off**; and two ways of draining, either by hosing the galley pump's spigot out through the vent opening or by hosing the fill spout and pumping until the tank siphons itself dry.

One point of designer practice is worth recording against all of this: **Matt Layden draws water from his own tanks with a fuel bulb rather than a pump**, which he says goes a long way toward encouraging fresh-water conservation.[4]

## A 2016 alternative: submersible bilge pumps, and a combined fill-and-drain pipe proposal

A builder weighing how to fill and empty his own water-ballast tanks explained his reasoning for choosing built-in tanks over the bottled ballast carried on a previous boat, where filling, emptying, and stowing bottles under the seats had been "a real hassle"; his own plan was to fill by hose and empty with a plumber's siphon once trailered, the tank water doing double duty for washing while bottled water stayed reserved for drinking.[6] Told in reply of a working alternative to siphoning: after trying, and for one season abandoning, lead ballast in place of the water tanks altogether, a **submersible bilge pump fitted to each tank's own filling pipe** empties it in a fountain once the boat is trailered, water having flowed the "wrong way" through the same pipe during filling; filling itself is done with a mobile electric pump hung over the side. A caution followed: **ballast-tank inspection hatches are usually designed to keep water out rather than in**, so fitting a genuinely good one matters more here than the hatch quality question usually implies.[7]

Days later, having concluded from trying it that siphoning the tanks is impractical after all, the same builder floated an alternative combined fill/vent/drain design of his own, offered as a thought experiment rather than something built: a single large-diameter pipe (32 mm), fixed flush to the inside of the tank wall with a gentle curve, with an ordinary garden hose (18 mm) threaded down through it both to fill the tank — overfilling briefly, then withdrawing the hose to let the level drop and cap the wider pipe off — and, fitted to a pump instead, to empty it; the plans' separate breather tubes would be omitted altogether, on the reasoning that the wider pipe alone should pass enough air for both filling and emptying. Two questions were left open: how to cap the large-diameter pipe at deck level without raising it proud of the deck (ruling out plain expanding plugs as liable to work loose), since keeping the opening flush would also allow filling the tanks from rainfall; and whether omitting the breather tubes would create a pressure imbalance not otherwise foreseen.[8]

A further refinement proposed drilling the tank wall on a slight downward angle and running the pipe most of the way across the tank to exit near the inside wall at the bottom, so that once the boat heels past about 90 degrees the opening clears the water and takes the pressure off an expanding plug; reasoned a sufficiently rigid, large-diameter pipe ought to stay on the tank floor unaided over time, though epoxy and tape to secure it outright were kept in reserve.[9] A reply suggested fitting a ball valve where the pipe reaches the underside of the deck, left flush and open when not wanted, and separately floated a hand-operated diaphragm pump to empty the tanks while afloat, doubling as the boat's own bilge pump; it also warned that a large temperature swing after filling can pressurize an otherwise sealed tank, calling for either a little air space left for expansion or a second, very small breather tube.[10] A further reply held that some venting is unavoidable regardless of pipe size, since the tanks cannot be made fully airtight against ordinary changes in air pressure and water temperature.[11]

Weighing the replies, the builder decided to keep the plans' own breather-vent routing into the mast vent trunk rather than deviate from it, then noticed on a closer read that the plans nowhere actually route the fill/vent pipe out onto the deck at all — the idea of filling the tanks with rainwater through a deck fitting must have come from elsewhere, perhaps Don Elliott's own building manual. Dropping the deck-fill idea altogether simplified the whole scheme: a garden-hose snap fitting and manual on/off valve fitted inside the boat, plumbed to a further pair of garden hoses through a 12-volt self-priming pump reversible to fill or drain.[12] A caution followed against relying on rainwater to fill the tanks at all — it would take an impractical amount of rain, or real desperation for water — and against driving water in under hose pressure without vent pipes sized for the full flow: a roughly 2 mm vent pipe once proved unable to vent fast enough, and the inspection cover blew off.[13]

A further reply pointed to a reason to keep a hose connection to hand even after giving up on water ballast altogether: filling or emptying while anchored away from a dock, with no hose available, needs some way of pumping in the surrounding water directly. One builder reported having done exactly that — abandoning water ballast on his own boat entirely for roughly 70 litres of additional dry storage, with less hassle at launch and recovery and no risk of water seeping past the inspection ports to wet a berth, at the cost of an estimated 70 kg added to the trailered weight; the boat's screw-cap deck fillers were left in place but disconnected from any plumbing, in theory able to catch rainwater in a bottle held beneath them, though this was never actually tried.[14]

By June 2016 the builder who had spent the spring working through the fill/vent/drain questions above had a working system built and tested: a trial run of a **self-priming 12-volt diaphragm pump** moved water in both directions simply by changing the valve settings.[16] Asked shortly after what pump other builders were using to move ballast water in and out,[17] he answered with three photographs of the finished installation: a circulation diagram;[18] the hoses and valves stowed in a side bin when not in use, on an inlet/outlet hose long enough — 6 m — that the pump still self-primes over that distance from a bucket set on the ground;[19] and the same plumbing rigged the other way, reversing a set of Y-valves to swap filling for emptying, with a measured flow of 7.6 litres a minute at 2.6 amps putting the time to fill or empty both tanks at roughly ten minutes.[20] A follow-up account gave the same system's own working details in full: a **fresh-water 12-volt self-priming diaphragm pump**, chosen for a flow gentle enough to fill a water bottle without spilling, mounted to the hull bottom under the forward stateroom floor; the light hoses and valves, easily pumped free of water for storage, kept coiled in the forward starboard side bin; and filling done through an intermediary bucket, the same role a header tank plays for a low-pressure hot-water cylinder.[21]

A separate builder's own tanks needed no such plumbing at all: the only fixed pipework running to them is the vents and the deck-mounted fillers shown on the plans, with a **submersible electric bilge pump dropped into each ballast tank** to empty it and a second, roving electric bilge pump used to fill them.[22] Whether that arrangement's filler lines route down through the pumps themselves was raised as a follow-up question and answered the same day: with the standard, cheap, centrifugal, submersible pumps in use, water passes freely through them whenever they are not actually pumping.[23][24]

The rainwater-filling idea's own origin, left an open guess earlier in this exchange, was pinned down in this same June 2016 thread. Challenged over where the plans actually show a deck-mounted filler — no such fitting appears on Matt Layden's own drawings, and filling the ballast tanks with rainwater off a salt-laden deck would do virtually nothing to replenish a boat's drinking-water supply while almost certainly contaminating what fresh water remained — the reply conceded the fitting is not drawn as such, but is "seriously suggested" on sheets 3 and 4, traced its own origin to Don Elliott's build manual, which does show a deck-mounted filler both pictorially and in writing, "a long time ago," and agreed that filling from rainwater is optimistic — it would take a great deal of rain.[25][26]
