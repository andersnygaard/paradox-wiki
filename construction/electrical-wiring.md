# Electrical wiring

A stock Paradox's electrical load is modest — running lights, a GPS, an echo sounder, and a bilge or water-ballast pump — but re-wiring for LED lighting, auxiliary plugs, and a masthead nav light raises real practical questions: what gauge and type of wire to use, how to route it through a hollow, unstepped-for-trailering mast without cutting the mast open to service it, and how to size and wire an LED array. This page collects that wiring and construction detail; see [radio and electronics](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md) for the boat's radio/communications equipment and overall power budget, and [running lights](/operations/running-lights.md) for the COLREGs case for a single masthead light and the choice between LEDs, kerosene, and other light sources.

## 12-volt wiring gauge: choosing conductor size for a low-draw boat

Re-wiring a Paradox for LED lighting, auxiliary plugs, and a handheld GPS raises the ordinary question of what wire gauge and type to use, and there is a genuine difference of approach. One practical, low-cost choice is **22-gauge, color-coded speaker wire**: light enough to disappear under a coat of paint, easy to route around the boat's interior, and simply held in place with clear duct tape while the paint goes on.[1]

A second approach argues instead for using the **thickest wire practically affordable** (10-gauge, for instance) throughout, reasoning that too thin a gauge causes two real problems — the wire heats up, and voltage sags at the load — whereas oversizing a 12-volt conductor has no real downside. Where obtainable, wire with a **light tin coating under the insulation** (to resist a corrosive green crust forming over time) and **aircraft-grade insulation** is preferred, on the reasoning that it produces the least toxic smoke in the event of excess heat or an accidental fire — a margin-of-safety consideration distinct from the LED loads themselves, which draw very little current regardless of wire size.[2]

The counter-argument, raised directly against that blanket thickest-is-always-better advice: for a lighting circuit drawing on the order of **0.08 amp**, heavy-gauge wire is unnecessary — the sound general principle is to **size the conductor to the actual current draw** of the load being wired, not to the thickest wire that can be afforded.[3] A third view reconciles the two: sizing wire strictly to the current draw is the cost-effective approach, but using the largest cross-section of copper that will physically fit is still the ideal, since the goal is always to minimize resistance losses in the conductor. In practice, salvaged multi-strand cable — 240-volt lighting cable and machine-tool cable recovered for free from a skip — offers a low-resistance, large-cross-section conductor at effectively no cost, a practical middle path between buying minimal-gauge wire and buying heavy new stock.[4]

The thickest-wire position ultimately accepts the size-to-the-load principle, while still admitting a personal preference for "easy overkill" over a precisely matched conductor — an aesthetic and risk-tolerance choice on top of the electrical argument, not a rebuttal of it.[5] A **tidy, invisible installation** is itself a design goal to weigh alongside gauge: one habit is to run the smallest wire that will do the job specifically because exposed wiring looks unsightly, routing it through **conduit** where the hull structure allows, and, where conduit is impractical (as when wiring a small production hull's overhead), simply taping the wire up under a run of clear duct tape that all but disappears once painted over to match the surrounding surface.[6]

## A compact LED tricolour/all-round masthead unit

One practical build combines both a **tricolour light** and a **white all-around light** in a single unit **less than 2 in in diameter and 1.5 in tall**:

- Built using **LEDs**, which use roughly **one-tenth the power** of an equivalent filament bulb for the same light output — a meaningful saving on a boat with limited battery capacity (see [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)).
- Only **two wires** run up the mast to power it: the tricolour and all-around LEDs are wired in **opposite polarity**, so reversing the battery connection's polarity switches which light function is lit — a neat way to drive two independent light functions over a single two-conductor run instead of the four-plus wires a conventional separate-fixture setup would need.
- A boat with no engine has no need for a separate steaming light.[7]

## Routing and maintaining masthead-light wiring without cutting the mast open

Electrical wiring is one of the **most frequently replaced items** on any sailboat, due to **corrosion**. Epoxying masthead-light wiring into the interior of the mast would mean cutting the mast open every time it needs replacing — so the wiring is better left **unencapsulated**:

- A **socket at the bottom of the mast** can be **extracted**, leaving a **20 mm hole** to fish wire through.
- At the top, the wire exits through a hole **just big enough for the wire itself**, then is **sealed in place with silicone sealer**.
- The wire itself is **not epoxied in**: it simply **hangs loose inside the mast** (padded to stop it slapping against the inside), so it can be pulled and replaced without cutting into the mast.
- Ordinary **5 amp flex** cable is adequate for the run.[8][9]

This approach — leaving the wire loose rather than encapsulating it — is what lets a compact two-wire LED masthead unit be installed and later serviced without opening up the mast.

## Routing masthead-light wiring outside the mast, below the sheave

A stave-built mast leaves little room past the **masthead sheave and its axle** for wiring routed fully inside, which raises a second routing question distinct from mast-base servicing above: whether to run the wire inside the mast all the way to the top, or exit it 100-150mm below the light and run the last stretch up the outside.[10]

Four different answers have been settled on. The most common is to bring the wire out below the mast's solid top and run it up the outside from there, since the plans' dimensions leave very little clearance past the sheave and axle for wiring kept fully inside[11][12]; a cleaner variant of the same idea, floated in hindsight, is to run copper strips up the outside of the mast near the top and bury them under a wrap of fiberglass cloth rather than leaving bare wire exposed on the surface. Another approach likewise routes the wiring **outside the mast at the top**, exiting near the topping-lift hardware. A third keeps the wiring recessed **flush with the mast's surface**, routed through a shallow groove rather than run proud on the outside or fished through the interior.[13] A fourth builds a small **wooden channel over the mast's surface** to enclose the wire at the masthead entirely, photographed alongside the sheathing visible under the mast — a boxed-in variant of the recessed-groove idea, rather than either a bare exterior run or a true internal one.[14]

At the **bottom** of the mast, one method brings the wire out under deck level and connects it to a plug fitted just under the forward port-side deck — simple to connect and disconnect each time the mast is stepped or unstepped for trailering, and proven reliable in service.[15]

## Clearing a snug deck hole as the mast is stepped and unstepped

Bringing the flex out below deck level, rather than above it, raises an obvious-sounding worry: if the mast fits snugly through its deck hole, how does the flex avoid being sheared or trapped as the mast is lowered into place?[16] The answer is the mast's own **taper**: the mast narrows steadily below deck level, so there is still clearance around the flex while the mast is partway down, well before the fit becomes snug.[17]

The same below-deck destination — a disconnectable plug/socket reachable from inside the boat — has been reached by two different routes. One method brings the wire out just below deck level to a **recessed plug in the end of the mast**, matching the destination (though not the routing) of the forward-port-deck method described above.[18] A second mounts the socket low on the **aft** face of the mast instead, reached from inside the boat by "a bit of a contortionist trick" rather than through any dedicated access hole; the plug/socket is a water-resistant type (so splashing bilge water is not a concern), fitted as low as clearance allows, with the socket itself fixed on from outside and spare wire left coiled inside the mast so the connection can be remade, or the run pulled through and replaced after damage, without having to strip the whole length out.[19][20] Both share the same underlying principle as the interior wire-slap padding already noted: the wire is never epoxied in, only run loose (or, on one build, through sections of pipe-insulation lagging glued to the mast's interior) so it can move slightly with the boat's motion without chafing or making noise, and can be drawn out and replaced without cutting into the mast.[19]

## A 2008 recurrence: resolved above deck, through the vent box

The same above-versus-below-deck connection question, and how waterproof the fitting needs to be, recurred once more in 2008 from a first-time boat-wirer making up a mast for the first time, having sailed only unwired boats before.[21] One answer offered a further below-deck option: a **waterproof panel-mount connector** fitted at the mast's foot, with the lead fed in from inside through the vent.[22] Bringing a wire out through the mast's **side below deck level** was flagged as a real complication, since it makes fitting and removing the mast harder; a builder wanting to exit the mast's side rather than its base is better off doing so **above** deck.[22]

A cheap, weatherproof alternative connector was suggested from outside the Paradox-building community, by a non-Paradox sailor who had faced the identical corroded-connector problem on an older production boat: **molded trailer-light connectors**, sourced from any trailer-supply house — the female half has a wide sealing flange and a spring-loaded weather door to keep water out when unplugged, the male half is injection-molded, and the pair is cheap enough to simply replace outright if it corrodes, with a little silicone grease on the male pin extending its life further. A ground wire, common or separately run, is needed regardless of hull material, wood or fiberglass alike.[23]

The recurrence was closed, in the end, by sidestepping the waterproofing question rather than solving it: bringing the wire out the mast's **aft face**, routing it upward under the **vent baffle** alongside the halyard and reef line, and socketing it at a high point on the **port side of the vent box** — an above-deck, inside-the-vent-box connection needing no waterproofing at all, a solution admired as simple and elegant.[24][25]

A **portable, unwired alternative** sidesteps the whole routing question a different way: rather than run any permanent flex up the mast at all, an LED enclosed in a translucent plastic ball (of the kind sold containing washing-machine powder) can be strung up separately only when a masthead light is actually needed.[18]

## A 2010 exchange: a VHF coax run alongside the lighting wiring, and two further conduit techniques

A further builder, closing in the mast's final side panel, raised the routing question once more in a form the earlier threads had not covered directly: running a length of **RG58 coax** for a VHF antenna feed together with the lighting wiring (sized up to 4-core, to leave spare conductors in hand), needing an entry/exit above deck, below the mast's solid top section, clear of the halyard and yard, with water ingress at both ends still the standing worry.[37]

The reply confirmed the established pattern above holds up over years of use: a **2-core wire** feeding a combined white/tricolour masthead unit is lined the length of the mast with short sections of **pipe insulation** at intervals, letting the wire be pulled through for future replacement without rattling; the wire exits at the mast's **front face** at the top, sealed with ordinary **bath sealant**; at the bottom, a **waterproof connector** is fitted flush with the mast just above the bottom block, its mating half reached and plugged in from inside the vent box only when the light is actually needed. Two further details, drawn from years of service rather than initial installation, refine the demountable-connector case for a mast-base fitting: the fitting itself can be **removed** to reach the screw terminals directly once they work loose over time, and a **spare foot or so of cable** is deliberately left coiled at the mast's bottom, so a length damaged at the top can simply be pulled through rather than the whole run replaced.[38]

A third answer restated an already-documented sealed-for-life alternative — a hard plastic tube glued permanently into the mast at assembly as a conduit, so wiring can be pulled and changed later without any accessible joint at all — though, as of writing, the tube had not yet actually been used to run a wire through.[39] Weighing the three answers together, the original questioner concluded a **conduit of some kind** is the reliably foolproof way to route mast wiring, whatever form it takes.[40]

## A further November 2010 recurrence, and a caution against an exposed mast-base connector

The routing question recurred once more that November, from a builder new to the mast stage and wanting a masthead running light wired down to the cabin.[41] The answer restated the vent-box approach already established above: a waterproof socket embedded in the mast just above its solid base, with the mating plug reached and inserted from inside the cabin through the vent box.[42] A less tidy alternative seen on other builds — the wire simply emerging from the mast at that point with a short lead and an exposed socket — was flagged as a real liability rather than a matter of taste: it can catch and become "a pain" each time the mast is stepped or unstepped for trailering.[42]

## Mast length and masthead-light headroom

A masthead light's height matters beyond wiring: the [mast](/components/rig/mast.md) must still fit stowed inside the hull for trailering, so a tall fixture at the top can push the whole spar past the length the hull allows. One low-profile light, built specifically to solve this, comes in at around 4 in/10 cm long — the archive's only recorded figure for how compact a masthead light needs to be to clear this constraint.[26]

## LED electrical theory, and how to wire several in series

The underlying electrical theory behind an LED masthead unit, for builders wanting to design their own array rather than buy one ready-made:

- **LEDs are current-driven, not voltage-driven**, unlike a filament bulb. A filament bulb simply takes whatever voltage it is fed and regulates its own current draw; an LED instead needs its **current** actively controlled, and must be connected with the correct polarity.
- Every LED has two key ratings: its **forward voltage** and its **operating current**. On one built nav light, the white LEDs had a forward voltage of **3.46V**, the red **1.84V**, and the green **2.2V** — with all three needing about **20mA**, the norm for most LEDs generally.
- Below the forward voltage, no current flows and the LED stays dark; at the forward voltage, it draws its rated current and shines brightly; above it, current rises further (still bright) until, past the rated voltage, the LED is destroyed by excess current.
- **The simplest current control is a resistor.** From a 12V supply (higher if the boat runs 13.6V or more with a charging system), a white LED needing 3.46V leaves 8.54V to drop across a resistor; at the desired 20mA (0.020A), Ohm's law (V=IR) gives roughly **427 ohms** (rounding up to the nearest convenient value), for a power consumption of about **240mW**.
- **Wiring LEDs in series multiplies the light for the same power budget.** Three of the same LEDs in series drop 10.38V between them, leaving only 1.62V for the resistor — still at 20mA, so the resistor drops to about **81 ohms**. Total power draw is unchanged at 240mW, but light output **triples**, since less of the fixed power budget is wasted as heat in the resistor. Maximizing the number of LEDs wired in series to get the most light for the least power draw is the guiding design philosophy — used both for a nav-light array and for strings of three white LEDs lighting the interior and lockers.[27][28][29][30]

One such array uses **six 60-degree white LEDs** arranged as **two parallel rows of three in series** (matching the series-wiring principle above), with a half-watt resistor ample for the 240mW circuit; LEDs and resistors of this kind are available from general electronics suppliers such as RS Components (`rswww.com`) in the UK.

## Building a sector'd LED masthead light: LED counts, wiring, and potting

A combined **tricolour (red/green/white) and all-round anchor light** in a single low-profile housing is worth building rather than buying where a compact **mast-fitting design** — the mast must stow inside the hull for trailering — demands a thinner unit than any off-the-shelf fixture. Working from each LED's datasheet **luminous intensity** (candela) rating against the legal minimum visibility requirement gives the LED count needed per sector:

- **Red sector:** 3 LEDs
- **Green sector:** 5 LEDs (green LEDs run dimmer than red at the same legal threshold, hence the extra LED)
- **White sector:** 4 LEDs
- **Anchor light:** 6 LEDs

Rather than fit opaque baffles between sectors, each LED's own **viewing-angle** datasheet figure can set the sector boundaries directly — accepting a soft, gradual color transition between sectors rather than a hard cutoff, no worse than many commercial lamps.

The array splits into **five circuits**: three tricolour circuits (5 green + 56 ohm resistor; 3 white + 82 ohm resistor; 3 red + 1 white + 150 ohm resistor) wired in parallel for 720mW total, and two identical anchor circuits (each 3 white + 82 ohm resistor) wired in parallel for 480mW total. The tricolour and anchor circuit groups are then wired to each other in **reverse parallel polarity**, so that reversing the battery polarity at the mast base switches between the tricolour and the anchor light — the same two-wire trick noted above, requiring only two conductors to run up the mast for both light functions.

Construction used two purpose-made circular circuit boards about one inch in diameter (one per light function), with LEDs positioned point-outward in their correct sector locations, then the whole assembly **potted in silicone rubber** with only the LED heads protruding. The housing itself is a pair of turned plastic fittings and a short length of perspex tube held together with a central screw, and does double duty retaining the pin for the mast-head sheave. An alternative construction method — LEDs mounted around the outside of a short cylinder with wiring run inside — is a reasonable first idea but is more easily replaced by circuit-board etching once that equipment is available. A light built this way has proved **brighter than a 12W filament bulb**.[31]

See [running lights — why a tricolour light isn't fitted despite the sector-light design](/operations/running-lights.md#why-a-tricolour-light-isnt-fitted-despite-the-sector-light-design) for the COLREGs objection this array design raises.

## A 2010 hindsight verdict: sector overlap, and a case for skipping the tricolour on a boat this size

Revisited six years after it was built, the compact tricolour/all-round unit described above (see [a compact LED tricolour/all-round masthead unit](#a-compact-led-tricolourall-round-masthead-unit)) drew a practical verdict earned from years of actual use rather than bench testing: the red/green/white sector transitions show, in practice, "quite a large overlap of colours" — a real shortcoming rather than a cosmetic one. Building the light again from scratch, the fix would be to fit only the white all-around light — legal under COLREGs for a boat this size (see [running lights](/operations/running-lights.md)) — and skip the tricolour sectors altogether. The array's LEDs are wired in series with their current set by a simple resistor; a proper voltage regulator, rather than a resistor, would probably do the job better.[43][44]

## Running LEDs off a charging battery rather than a steady 12V supply

The forward-voltage/current figures above assume a steady 12V supply, as on a boat with no charging source aboard. A boat that does charge its battery under way should size the current-limiting resistor for the **higher charging voltage** (e.g. 14.4V) rather than the resting 12V; run this way, the LEDs will simply glow slightly dimmer once charging stops and the battery settles back toward its resting voltage (around 12.6V fully charged) — a difference too small to notice by eye. Where the swing between charging and resting voltage is larger, a simple voltage regulator ahead of the array can stabilize it, though this is unnecessary on most installations. Every LED's datasheet gives a **maximum forward current** rating that must never be exceeded, or the LED is destroyed; white LEDs are commonly rated for 30mA and green for 25mA, leaving some headroom to work with as supply voltage varies.

The legal basis for the single all-around white light bears restating in a wiring context: **COLREGS** requires only that small boats show a white light sufficient to avoid collision, and by convention a 360-degree masthead light satisfies this — with most vessels obliged to give way to it. Under power, COLREGS additionally calls for side lights, a steaming light, and a stern light, though the steaming and stern functions may be combined into a single all-around white; side lights, however, may **not** be folded into a combined tricolor unit under those rules — a technical limit on how far the two-wire tricolor/anchor-light trick (above) can be pushed for a boat that is sometimes motored.[32][33]

## A cheaper home-built LED light, without a lens

A cheaper DIY alternative is a home-built all-round LED light for about $18 total ($8 of LEDs plus $10 for a Perko replacement fresnel globe, the LEDs sourced from `whitelightled.com` and the globe from West Marine or Boaters World), documented with photographs at `microcruising.com/Graphics/led2-0004r.jpg` and `led5-0001r.jpg`.[34] A similar light built without a lens, with LEDs mounted in a length of perspex tube instead, gives fine all-round white output, but on a similarly-built red-green-white version the color transition between sectors lacks a sharp cutover.[35]

## Homemade below-deck lighting, and a tube-built anchor/nav light

Below-deck illumination can be built from commercial 12V LED strip lighting mounted on lengths of ordinary wooden yardstick, wired with light-gauge (24-gauge) wire. The same do-it-yourself approach extends outside the cabin: an anchor or navigation light can be built from 12 in lengths of clear plastic tubing capped at each end with standard PVC pipe caps, ground out for a tight fit over the tube, housing individual LEDs and resistors wired in strings following Ohm's law, as above.[45]

## Where to fit interior lamps: a battery-powered stick-on option, and a 2015 wired-LED placement scheme

Not every below-deck light needs to be wired at all: a cheap, battery-operated LED "stick-on" light, of the kind sold for lighting the underside of kitchen cabinets, needs no wiring or switchboard connection and can simply be fitted wherever a dark corner — the forepeak among them — needs illuminating occasionally.[46]

A fuller, permanently wired scheme was planned as: LEDs on the forward face of bulkheads 1 and 2 and the aft face of bulkhead 4, plus one more under the cabin hatch, each separately switched from the switchboard.[47] Experience from an existing installation refined where such lamps are actually worth the wiring: a lamp on bulkhead 2's forward face, mounted high and near the centre, is worthwhile, but a lamp on bulkhead 1 is not, since that compartment can only be reached awkwardly with a fully outstretched arm and is rarely worth lighting; a foam pillow stowed there, with part of it left sticking out toward the stern, can simply be found and pulled out by feel. The same installation mounts its own forward-stateroom lamp on **velcro** rather than fixed permanently, since a cheap AA-cell LED unit of the kind used there is prone to poor connections and needs frequent "fettling" — velcro lets it be brought into the main cabin to be worked on rather than serviced in place. The aft storage area was judged to want more than the single lamp it has.[48]

## Solar-panel cable entrance through the deck or window

For a Paradox with an installed solar panel, wiring the panel's cable into the cabin can be run either through a hole drilled in a window, or through a loop of wire left outside the hull rather than piercing anything at all — the external-loop method used on at least one boat. No builder has recorded routing a solar-panel cable through the deck itself.[36]

## Choosing where to site a solar panel: roof, hatch, foredeck, or aft deck

Four candidate solar-panel locations were weighed against each other on one boat. The **cabin roof, just forward of the sliding hatch**, gets good light with the hatch closed but less through the polycarbonate hatch skylight when the hatch is open, forcing a choice between closing up below to keep charging or sitting in the open air. Moving the panel onto the **sliding hatch** itself would trade away the skylight — valued for watching sail trim and keeping a lookout to leeward while heeled — and would need a longer flexible wire able to survive the hatch's sliding action without chafing or catching. The **foredeck, forward of the mast**, risks being in the way each time the mast is lifted in or out, is prone to damage underfoot, and sits where the anchor rode runs across, bringing mud and wet with it. The **aft deck** was ruled out altogether: it earns its keep as a general work surface at anchor — fuel cans, the anchor bucket, food preparation, washing up — and gets walked on boarding from a pontoon.[49]

A modest electrical budget kept the siting choice simple on that same boat: besides a newly added tiller pilot, only a small netbook computer and occasional camera/phone charging drew power, with no VHF or navigation lights fitted and the anchor light burning paraffin rather than electricity — even so, a 30 W panel was not expected to keep the tiller pilot running as much as wanted.[49]
