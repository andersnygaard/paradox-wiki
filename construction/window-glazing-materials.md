# Window glazing — Lexan/polycarbonate versus Perspex/acrylic

A Paradox's windows can be glazed in either of two clear plastics — **polycarbonate** (trade name Lexan) or **acrylic** (trade names Perspex, Plexiglas, Lucite, Acrylite, and Rhoplex/Oroglas) — and the two have genuinely different working properties, not just different price points.[1][2]

## Toughness versus scratch resistance: the core tradeoff

**Polycarbonate (Lexan)** is far tougher and more impact-resistant than acrylic. It is the material used for jet-aircraft cockpit canopies, bulletproof glazing, shatterproof eyewear, and ice-hockey and football face guards.[1] A demonstration of its resilience: a quarter-inch sheet of Lexan laid over an open barrel and struck repeatedly with a two-pound sledgehammer eventually deformed into the barrel under the hardest blow but did not break — the sheet had to be worked back into a rough cylinder with a Spanish windlass to get it back out.[1][2]

**Acrylic (Perspex/Plexiglas)** is more brittle by comparison — it can crack under impact or under excess stressing (for example, over-tightening a screw through it) — but is harder and more scratch-resistant than polycarbonate, and easier to polish back to clarity if scratched. Acrylic also costs a fraction of what polycarbonate costs, and is the more commonly used term in the US for the same family of sheet plastic.[1][3][2]

Both materials are used in boat windows; acrylic simply cannot carry structural loads the way polycarbonate can. Either should be specified with a **UV coating**, or it will cloud over much sooner in sunlight — a bigger factor in the tropics than in temperate climates.[1] Cost-conscious builders who cannot get factory-fresh sheet can often find **drops or damaged sheets** from a local plastics supplier or fabricator at a considerable discount over a full new sheet.[3]

A 2011 window replacement prompted by a wave breaking hard over a South-Atlantic-sailing Paradox's deck (see [Cachivache — a 2011 refit](/boats/cachivache.md#a-2011-refit-a-new-boom-a-pulpit-and-balcony-and-the-logo-question-asked-again)) drew a question about what material the original and replacement panes used; no answer is on record, but a 1/4 in (about 6 mm) thickness is reported in service on two other boats, one glazed in polycarbonate (Lexan) and the other in acrylic (Plexiglas).[30]

## Working the two materials

The two plastics behave differently under tools and heat, which matters when cutting a window opening:

- **Acrylic** can be edge-polished with a heat source such as a propane torch (practice first) and bends fairly easily with a heat gun.[1]
- **Polycarbonate** chars and burns under high heat rather than softening cleanly, and an amateur will get only marginal results trying to cold-bend or heat-form it — the forming temperature is close to the temperature that damages the material outright.[1]
- **Laser cutting** leaves acrylic with a smooth edge, while polycarbonate chars at the cut and needs the burnt edge scraped, leaving a sanded look.[1]
- **Jigsaw-cutting polycarbonate window openings** works best with a coarse-tooth blade run at a slow speed; a fine blade run fast melts the plastic swarf into a lump that clogs the blade instead of clearing it. See [Faith — cutting the polycarbonate windows](/boats/faith.md#cutting-the-polycarbonate-windows).[4][5]

One glazing choice for a see-through hull-bottom viewing window was Lexan Excel D (UV-coated): it scratches with use, but because polycarbonate's refractive index is close to water's, visibility through the scratched panel stayed serviceable even underwater.[1] See [glass-bottom viewing panel](/design/ideas/glass-bottom-idea.md) for that use case, and [cutting window openings into the cabin sides](/boats/enuf.md#cutting-window-openings-into-the-cabin-sides) for a use of 3 mm Lexan offcuts glazing routed window recesses directly into the plywood cabin sides.

Two further chemical hazards to Lexan/polycarbonate windows worth planning around: **petrol splashed on Lexan ruins it** — a caution most relevant to a boat with an outboard rather than one relying only on a sculling oar for auxiliary power — and some **insect repellents containing DEET** likewise damage polycarbonate on contact. A home-built 23-footer glazed with ordinary Perspex/acrylic instead had no such problems.[6][7]

A protective **Mylar film**, of the kind sold as automotive sun-blocking window tint or household window-darkening film, extends either material's working life: it is inexpensive, peels off cleanly once scratched or dirtied rather than requiring the underlying pane to be replaced, comes in shades from near-clear to near-black, can be reapplied every season or so, and — being a polyester — resists chemical damage in its own right while adding UV protection for the Lexan or acrylic beneath it. This mirrors the same trade already used on NASCAR windshields and helicopter canopies.[8]

A **boat cover** for the cabin and decks while trailered or laid up is a further layer of protection, expected to pay for itself many times over by keeping paint, epoxy, and Lexan/acrylic glazing free of UV and abrasion damage, and as a deterrent against curious or careless passersby — a benefit that applies equally to a boat left on a mooring between trailer-sailing trips.[9][10]

## Window tint color

Cabin window glazing is fitted in either near-clear/water-clear sheet or a black/smoky tint, with no single preference on record: a clear windshield paired with tinted side windows is one builder's choice, while others prefer clear glazing throughout on the reasoning that tinted windows can impair night vision, reserving tint's appeal for use in a marina.[11][12][13] A cheap, reversible way to test tinting without committing to permanent film is to fit a removable frame holding a layer or three of ordinary window-screen mesh — "a poor man's removable window tinting."[14] Separately, sandwiching one thick, expensive polycarbonate sheet between two thin, cheap sacrificial layers has been floated as a way to protect costly glazing from scratching and fogging by replacing only the outer layers as they wear, in principle using an optical gel between the layers to keep the sandwich visually seamless — untested on any Paradox on record.[14]

A firsthand report from a tinted build backs the clear-glazing skeptics' concern only partway: from inside, tinting is barely noticeable even at night, with very good visibility maintained — the difference is mainly apparent when the sun is shining directly on the glazing from outside.[15][16][17] A second, independent tinted-build report agrees: tinted acrylic port lights paired with tinted safety glass in the hard-dodger side windows, against a clear windshield, is a combination its builder would not change if building again.[18][19]

## Rebuilding a cracked window from the original as a template

A cracked acrylic window can be rebuilt using the cracked original itself as a cutting template. To keep the sheet from splintering under a jigsaw, sandwich it between two pieces of plywood with the shape drawn on the upper piece; once cut, swap the upper plywood piece for the old cracked window to mark the bolt-hole positions, avoiding burred edges or a split sheet. A tight, even fit within the window's mount matters as much as the cutting itself — uneven tension from the retaining bolts, rather than any flaw in the material, is a likely cause when a window keeps cracking at the same bolt holes, so fitting a replacement as precisely as possible within its mount is worth the extra care.[20]

## Bedding sealant: a cost comparison

Bedding a window against its hatch or cabin-side mount calls for its own sealant choice, separate from the glazing material itself. When rebedding a hatch window, **General Purpose Sealant by Ever Build** was chosen over the pricier **Geocel Sealant** once Ever Build's own technical data sheet confirmed it adequate for the job, and the same product was used for the boat's cabin windows too. Applied economically, the sealant need only run around a window's outer and inner perimeters plus the nut-and-bolt holes, rather than filling the whole cavity between the two seals, which can be left unsealed but still protected from ingress.[21]

A later rebedding job, reinstalling windows removed for a refit, drew a wider range of sealant options from across the fleet. **Butyl tape**, laid over a coat of one-part poly paint on the surrounding wood (white or black), was praised for its elasticity — it does not break off the surface — giving a leak-free bed that still lets the window be pulled again later; **Sikaflex**, sold in a choice of colors, was reported to work well; **silicone-based sealants** were also endorsed, though they come only in black for UV protection of the silicone itself, with FiX200 and FS200 named as specific products; and plain **latex caulk** — paintable, cheap, and easy to clean up — was reported holding up four years in service when run under the lexan and smoothed into the panel's edge to keep paint sealed over it.[36][37][38][39]

## Window retaining-bolt material, and a leak found on removal

Removing a set of aging cabin windows for inspection took about three and a half hours; they turned out not to have been properly sealed, likely enough to have leaked in continuous heavy weather at sea, and the bolts retaining them had rusted, with rust streaks only likely to worsen.[22] Cheap metal window-retaining bolts corrode quickly in salt water; **marine-standard stainless steel or phosphor bronze** hold up to the job where a cheaper fastener will not.[23]

See [hatch hardware and security](/construction/hatch-hardware.md) for the hatch's own sliding-runner wear surfaces and locking hardware.

## A proven window-fastening screw size (2021)

Asked what fastens a lexan window into its opening — screws or bolts, what size, and what spacing — a builder with the same window installed and holding for 19 years gave a concrete answer: 3 mm diameter by 20 mm long pan-head stainless-steel screws, spaced at 75 mm intervals.[40][41]

## Cabin condensation: a drip rail, a moulded gutter, and window drain holes

A small trim piece specified on sheet 10 — a drip rail, 2 mm x 75 mm, mounted below the cabin-top beam — is meant to manage cabin-window condensation rather than deck-edge drainage; whether it is worth building depends on the local climate, since only a boat wintering somewhere genuinely cold sees enough overnight condensation on the windows to need it.[24] One alternative to the plans' thin wooden strip: judging it too fragile, mould a small guttering from thickened epoxy instead, and separately drill a 4 mm hole through the lower rear corner of each side window to let any condensation that does form drain away rather than pool on the sill.[25] A firsthand report from a boat sailed in genuinely cold conditions — April, cold enough to bring on real condensation — found the plans' own drip tray, built as drawn, worked well, backing building the trim piece to plan over improvising an alternative.[26] Following this advice, one builder committed to building the drip rail as drawn rather than substituting the epoxy-gutter method — see [Ironsides — laminating the cabin and hatch beams](/boats/ironsides.md#laminating-the-cabin-and-hatch-beams-and-salvaged-hardwood-trim) for that boat's own beam-lamination progress at the same stage of the build.[27]

## A 2014 recurrence: cleaning reach, salt-spray technique, and UV durability compared

A later thread revisited both the material choice and the practicalities of keeping windows clear underway. Clear glazing was again preferred over tinted, on the reasoning that a tint had made an existing salt-smearing problem worse on a different (non-Paradox) boat; Paradox's own side and back windows can be reached and cleaned easily while underway, unlike the front.[31] Polycarbonate (Lexan) was restated as stronger than acrylic (Perspex) for rough-weather use; the side windows are reachable from outside for cleaning underway, and the front one too, though "a bit of an arm stretch."[32] In wind and sun, salt spray dries on the windows almost as fast as it can be wiped off, quickly saturating and smearing a dry tissue — calling for a good supply of fresh tissues rather than seawater, which only adds more salt, since there is rarely enough fresh water aboard to rinse the glazing clean; the effect was likened to driving through road salt spray thrown up by the car ahead, needing constant use of the washer fluid and wipers, which then runs dry at the worst moment.[32] A separate, independent account backed polycarbonate's durability specifically against sun exposure: acrylic cracks with age under UV where polycarbonate does not, and one builder's own windows are cut from aircraft-grade polycarbonate.[33] A magnetic aquarium-glass cleaner was floated as a way to wipe salt off the windows from inside without opening the hatch in the rain, sized to match the glazing's own thickness — but a cleaner of this kind is made for glass fish tanks, and the polycarbonate or acrylic used in a Paradox's own windows would scratch immediately under one.[34][35]

## UK sourcing and pricing

In the UK, Lexan sheet (2.05 m x 1.25 m, 6 mm) was sourced from **Master Plastics (S.W.) Ltd** (trading as `fascias.com`) for about £160 in 2003;[28] the same 6 mm sheet size later fell to £99.52 including VAT plus postage.[29] Plexiglass/acrylic sheet, by contrast, is a lower-cost alternative worth checking with local plastics suppliers and fabricators before committing to the pricier polycarbonate.[3]
