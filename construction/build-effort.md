# Build effort and workspace

This page gathers what builders have actually reported it takes to build a [Paradox](/paradox.md): hours invested, money spent, how difficult the build is judged to be, and what kind of workspace it demands. It draws together dozens of individual builders' own figures — from a bare-minimum "slam dunk" to a fully finished cruiser — plus the tool lists, cost breakdowns, and workspace workarounds those builds produced. It also covers what keeps a multi-year build moving, and the one case on record of a builder starting over after losing a boat to disaster.

## Time

Reported build times span roughly **400 hours for a bare-minimum "slam dunk"** up to **1,000 hours or more for a nicely finished example**, with finish quality — not the design itself — the main thing driving how long a build takes.[1d]

- **~500 hours over about two years**, working intermittently as a hobby rather than full-time, is one figure for a stop-start build ("Zoë") that was a "fun project," not a job.[1] A fuller retelling of the same build later put the total higher: **over a thousand hours** across two years of on-again, off-again work, with a final concentrated push of intense effort in the **last three months** — a thousand hours being roughly **six months of full-time (40 hr/week) work**.[1a] The boat "could have been completed in a lot less" time than it actually took, given the stop-start pace; the higher, ~1,000-hour figure is the more complete account, covering the project through to completion and first sailing (see [Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)), and was later confirmed again as the settled answer — **about two years of on-again off-again work and approximately 1,000 hours** — with the same finish-quality caveat: the boat "could be done in less time" since the finish a builder wants ultimately determines the time invested.[1b]
- **~4 months of full-time work** built the original Paradox, by the designer [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) himself.[2] This is the fastest data point on record and reflects working full-time straight through, remarkable given Matt had no car and hauled materials to a remote woods site by bicycle trailer. A later account gives a slightly different figure for the same build — **~3 months full-time**[3] — the two figures (4 months vs. 3 months) are not reconciled, both describing the same original boat.
- **~18 months for an evening/weekend build, down to a couple of months for an experienced full-time professional.** One account — declining to give a precise hour count ("heaven knows") — gave concrete elapsed-time figures: a build of "[little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" took **18 months** of "not-very-hard evening and weekend work"; building again, one would reckon on **about a year**; and an **experienced builder, working full time with all the right gear**, could probably finish a hull in **a couple of months**[4] — a range that brackets the ~500-to-1,000+ hour figures above at the faster end and sits well below Matt Layden's ~3-4-month full-time original build only if that builder is similarly experienced and equipped. See [transom deck plate](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md) for the sealing question answered in the same account. The same 18-month figure was independently matched a few years later by [Small Fry's](/boats/small-fry.md#completion-time-compared-with-another-owners-account-may-2011) own owner, whose build spanned about eighteen months of active work stretched across roughly three calendar years by travel and house moves.
- **Corner-cutting to shorten an 18-month build.** Reacting to the £2,500/18-months figure above as daunting for someone with only a few hours a week free, one prospective builder floated skipping glass sheathing, simplifying the interior finish, using paint instead of varnish and galvanized rather than brass hardware, and even substituting roofing tar for conventional waterproofing — trading finish and possibly durability for build speed, in the spirit of function-over-form.[5] The question drew a direct answer from [Alastair](/people/alastair-law.md), the builder of the original £2,500/18-months figure: "I don't think you could significantly reduce the building time (an experienced builder in a well equipped shop may do it in half the time) by taking shortcuts. If you want a quick boat, you don't want a Paradox"[6] — Paradox is, despite her small size, "really a big boat squeezed into a small package,"[7] and her practical parts count and complexity resist shortcuts the way a genuinely simple design would not. Building from photographs alone, without the plans' refinements, was floated as one way to build an [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) faster than a Paradox, but at the cost of losing the design details a photograph can't show.[6] Separately, Enigma's stitch-and-glue construction — without Paradox's internal hull stringers and floors — was floated as inherently faster to build than Paradox, especially without matching the amount of time Matt Layden spent on exterior finish.[8] A builder wanting Paradox specifically for her hull lines, chine runners, and shallow draft — having already ruled out both a Philsboat (too frumpy) and an Enigma (too small) as compromises he wasn't willing to make[9] — reasoned that owning a boat built with his own hands mattered enough to accept the longer timeline, rather than buy a cheaper, quicker secondhand boat.[10]
- **Comparative build-time data points from similar-sized designs, and the case for buying secondhand instead.** A [Bolger Micro Navigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro_Navigator) — judged similar in size and complexity to Paradox — took about **450 manhours over two years** and roughly **$1,000** (with heavy scrounging) for a self-described "quick and dirty" builder; building it again, with lessons learned, was estimated at **300 hours and $1,500**.[7] A Jim Michalak "Philsboat" — another boat that, like Paradox, can be sailed "from inside" — was estimated at under **45 hours** invested so far partway through the build (bottom glassing and epoxy not yet done), a much faster build than Paradox's greater parts count and complexity would allow, though judged less seaworthy;[11] deciding on a design at all, weighing several options, took **5 months** of research and "mental flip-flopping" for the same builder.[12] A second, completed Philsboat build came in at about **200 hours** total labor, "much simpler than Paradox" — built by a New Zealand owner who went on to start a Paradox anyway, simply wanting something he could take offshore.[143] A further, even more extreme comparison: a Triloboat — an unrelated flat-bottomed scow design — was floated as buildable in as little as **3 to 7 days**, far faster than any Paradox figure above.[13] Set against these faster options, the case for building any boat at all rather than buying a serviceable used fiberglass sailboat — often available "for nearly free" — is weak on pure cost/time grounds: "building a boat is very rarely the cheapest or quickest way to get a boat."[14] The enduring counter-case for building anyway is personal: enjoying the process of building, wanting a boat built by one's own hands, and treating the finished boat as "just a by product of the trip" rather than the point of it.[12] A concrete data point on the secondhand side of that trade-off: an **1989 Hunter 140** with a nearly new roller-furling jib and main, on a galvanized trailer, was picked up secondhand for **$300** — trailerable cruisers in serviceable condition, needing a good cleaning and perhaps a week's touching-up, turn up regularly for under $1,000, against which a week's building effort would buy nowhere near as much boat; the only real reason to build one at all is the enjoyment of building itself, or a set of requirements no secondhand boat happens to meet.[15]
- **A Jim Michalak Paradox knock-off, and a lightweight stitch-and-glue Paradox.** Distinct from the "Philsboat" comparison above, Michalak separately produced a specific **Paradox-derivative design study** he called "Robb's boat," intended to be easier to build than Paradox itself. A separately built, lightly-constructed **stitch-and-glue Paradox** (as opposed to the plans' stock plywood-and-lumber construction) was also reported as a fun, workable boat despite its lighter build — though between the two alternatives, the stitch-and-glue Paradox was judged the better choice, since at least its hull shape is the designer's own proven one rather than a knock-off derivative.[16]
- **A confirmed twenty-two-month span for one fully dated build**, precise at both ends rather than estimated: marine plywood for the hull arrived on 1 August 2005, and the finished boat launched on 10 June 2007 — a span of roughly twenty-two months. The same builder's own later, looser retrospective claims of "a year and a half" (roughly eighteen months) for the identical build understate the true elapsed time; the contemporaneous dates at each end are the more reliable figure.[17][18]
- **A common source of both envy and reassurance during a slow build.** Watching a more advanced builder's boat come together while one's own plywood still sits cut only to the shape of unassembled bulkheads is a recognizable low point of an on-again, off-again build — offset by the reassurance that having started years earlier would already mean being afloat by now, so a slow start still beats no start at all.[19]
- **What a CNC kit would actually save.** A CNC-cut kit covering only the bulkheads, sides, bottom, and deck was judged likely to save only about a week of total build time — those flat panels are "the easy parts of the build," with the many hundreds of smaller parts needing individual fabrication and fitting, plus finishing and painting, the real driver of build time.[120]
- **Two further 2015 data points, at opposite ends of the range.** A first-time boatbuilder with no prior craft experience, sewing his own sails along the way, started construction around **20 December** and had her out for a **first sail by mid-September** the following year — a span of well under a year, faster than the ~18-month figures above and closer to Matt Layden's own ~3-4-month original build, though without his full-time pace.[135] A second builder working **5 hours a day, 5 days a week**, reported after **7 months** that his hull was almost ready to paint, estimating **about 8 months** total to launch — a middle-of-the-range figure consistent with the ~18-month evening/weekend figures above scaled up to something closer to full-time hours.[136]
- **A third 2015 data point, and a cross-builder estimate.** A New Zealand builder, scarfing his hull sides that April with the bulkheads already done and every appendage but the sail already made, gave his own build's full order to date — masthead light, mast, yard, yuloh, boom, lower gudgeon, a stainless tack downhaul, stem, rudder cheek and rudder, three of the four bulkheads, the transom, deck beams, and the mast step — most of the pieces taken only to 90-95% complete, deliberately, so each could still be checked against the assembled hull before final fitting; in his own experience the order rarely matters, with the one clear exception that the mast step depends on bulkhead 2 being finished first (full order and build detail at [boats/mini.md](/boats/mini.md#the-build-2014-2015)). Struck by how widely completion times vary across builders, he guessed the build time for most of them falls somewhere between **900 and 1,100 hours** — consistent with the roughly-1,000-hour finished-boat figure already established above.[140]
- **Adopting the same order, and a pre-taper drilling sequence for the yard.** A prospective New York State builder, thanking the New Zealander above for sharing that order, planned to start the same way — with the yard and its end fittings — reasoning that since the yard tapers along its length, its centerline is easier to find and drill true while the stock is still square, before the taper is shaped into the wood; the hole receives a copper-pipe insert, the same style of pipe-lined bearing already documented for the [boom's own tack-end fitting](/construction/boom-end-tack-line-holes.md#drilling-the-tack-end-for-the-swivel-rod-bearing-and-a-further-tang-bolt-caution). Starting there also doubles as practice with tools, measuring, and reading the plans and build manual before committing to the hull itself.[144]
- **A follow-up on the ~8-month builder above, and general advice on going faster.** Weeks after launching (see [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#build-progress-and-launch)), the same roughly-8-month full-time builder answered a newcomer's hours-to-build question by placing his own build against an accident that struck around the six-month mark; judged a faster build possible given the right tools and trade skills, and offered the general encouragement to start right away and do something toward the boat every day.[153]
- **The wood-quality trade-off, restated by the same builder years later.** Buying the best available wood and plywood is expensive but noticeably quicker to build with; working around poorer-quality material instead teaches more, a lesson carried over from building garden structures and furniture with whatever timber comes to hand.[157]

## Outsourcing the wood-cutting to a professional carpenter

One approach floated for a builder more drawn to sailing than to learning woodworking from scratch: draw the parts on paper and hand them to a professional carpenter to cut, doing the actual assembly and epoxy work personally and sending further batches of parts to the carpenter as the build proceeds — narrowing the skills that need to be learned firsthand to epoxy work and basic assembly, rather than the full range of woodworking technique a from-scratch build otherwise demands. Left open on record whether the approach carries any real drawback.[121] A related idea recurred at group scale in August 2013: an official poll gauging interest in a plywood, CNC-cut kit, with options ranging from full enthusiasm — including a deluxe kit complete with hardware and epoxy, or joining in on the kit's own design and cutting — through preferring to make every piece from scratch, to sourcing parts independently for less.[122] One reply welcomed the idea outright, asking to hear more.[123] The poll closed a week later: **16 votes (55%)** for a kit if priced right, **9 votes (31%)** for a deluxe kit complete with hardware and epoxy, 2 votes (7%) for helping design or cut it, 1 vote (3%) for making every piece from scratch, and 1 vote (3%) for wanting someone else to build the whole boat outright — no vote at all went against the idea of a kit itself.[124] The lone from-scratch vote traced to timing rather than a preference for the work: spreading the purchases out over what was expected to be probably years was preferred to buying all the materials at once, the same pay-as-you-go reasoning already on record above.[125] That vote drew a reply admiring the commitment it implied next to a kit's easier path, and asking who among the design/cut-it-yourself voters had actually come forward.[126] A third reply agreed from separate experience: unrelated hobby projects — a teardrop camper, a 7.5 in gauge locomotive, a cyclekart — had likewise only come together by being paid for and built over time rather than all at once.[127]

## Discovering the design through the Bolducs' site

A recurring path into the Paradox community is a prospective builder finding the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) website while comparing small-boat designs, then committing to a Paradox on the strength of the site's tone and the community's responsiveness as much as the boat's specifications. In one case, after months spent weighing a Glen-L design and a Long Micro against the Paradox, a prospective builder settled on the Paradox after prompt, friendly replies about the "Building the Paradox" article series — deciding factors echoed elsewhere in the archive: an active builders' group, alongside the boat's simplicity, affordability, and easy trailering/storage.[20] New members are consistently welcomed onto the list by multiple established builders within their first days, an informal but consistent pattern across the archive.[21][22][23] Not every newcomer arrives by that route: one lurker traced his own path to the design through [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md)'s website instead, having grown up near where Yrvind built his first "Bris" and carrying since childhood the fascination that a boat could be built and sailed around the world.[118] Asked how long a completed build had taken, the answer restated the standing ~18-month "Little Jim" figure already on record above.[119] A first practical question that recurs for newcomers with plans in hand — exterior versus marine-grade plywood — is generally settled in favor of marine grade; see [plywood grade and foam flotation debate](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-new-builders-exterior-vs-marine-choice-and-why-marine-usually-wins-on-labor) for the full exchange.

The group's accumulated photo-sharing is itself a recurring draw for newcomers sizing up the design before committing tools to wood: one new builder, having ordered plans and the "Building the Paradox" articles, thanked the wider list specifically for the build photos its members had contributed.[24] Early progress can come quickly once building starts — removing a first **deck beam** from its jig and finding it came out "dead on," much easier than expected, is enough to make a builder confident of sailing "in a couple of years of weekend work."[25]

## Cost

- **~$2,000** — what it cost Matt Layden to build the original Paradox (materials), alongside the ~3 months of full-time labor above.[3]
- **$1,600** — what it cost to *buy* [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) secondhand (not a new-build cost); it needed some refitting after 3 years of full-time cruising.[3]
- **$600** — the secondhand asking price of [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md), bought a few years before late 2001.[3]
- See also [secondhand Paradox market](/operations/secondhand-market.md) for a $500 secondhand Paradox asking price and the $35 cost of the building plans themselves ([building plans](/references/building-plans.md)).
- **$AUS790 for plywood alone** — the cost for the full **ten-sheet** plywood order for "Walden" (Pacific Maple marine ply, guaranteed void-free), Brisbane, Australia.[26] This is a materials-only figure (plywood, not epoxy/hardware/lead) but gives a non-US cost data point distinct from Matt's ~$2,000 total build cost above. See [hull scantlings](/design/hull-scantlings.md) for the ten-sheet bill of materials this corresponds to.
- **$2,000-8,000 US** is a working range for a finished boat's value, reported by a builder sewing his own sail for an in-progress stock-wood build. A Paradox is "not really a first timer's first project" unless the builder already has woodworking/building experience, though it is workable for an experienced amateur. Finished-boat value depends on plywood grade, degree of finish, quality of workmanship, and hardware quality — "an item is worth what someone will pay for it."
- **The same figure restated a third time, six years on.** Asked in August 2010 whether anyone had kept track of rough Paradox building costs,[116] the "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" builder gave his own figure again — about £2,500, this time dated to his 2003 finish and noted as including a few tools — the same total already on record twice from 2004 below.[117]
- **A full itemized UK build cost.** The fullest single line-item cost breakdown on record, for "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" (see also the [£2,751.16 total figure above](#time)): **£600 timber**, **£375 plywood**, **£630 epoxy and related consumables** (with a large surplus left over), **£30 polystyrene insulation/buoyancy foam**, **£170 Lexan sheet** (also with plenty left over), and **£350 for the sail** — plus bronze nails, stainless screws and bolts, numerous new tools, cleats, fairleads, inspection hatches, an anchor, rope, LEDs for the lights, a lifejacket, and a cooker, all adding to the total; the £1,502.12 custom trailer was **not** included in the £2,500-plus boat figure. Building more cheaply is possible by using exterior-grade plywood, per Matt Layden's own suggestion, rather than marine-grade; precise dimensions on pre-planed timber also rarely matter once building is under way, so planing to exact size in advance is an avoidable expense.[27]
- **A second full itemized UK build cost, and a later, looser estimate.** A separate, independently itemized 2007 UK cost record for a finished, sailing Paradox breaks down by category: timber £996.92; plywood £400.00; epoxy, hardener, filleting and fairing blend £575.97; chandlery, paint and rope £738.70; woven roving (fibreglass cloth) £105.45; nails and bronze fittings £285.49; polycarbonate £123.83; anchors and chain £24.99; nuts and bolts £29.80; a secondhand sail including US import duty £188.35; lead £70.00; and other items including a secondhand depth sounder £270.88 — a **boat total of £3,810.38** — plus tools and clamps £99.56, a secondhand road trailer and lock £528.99, and a tow ball and fitting to the car £176.25, for a further £804.80, bringing the **grand total to £4,615.18**, explicitly stated as excluding hidden costs such as car trips to suppliers, phone calls, and correspondence.[28] The same builder's later, rounder verdict put the true cost of building and fitting out a Paradox at "well over £4000 — more likely in the region of £4,500" without a trailer, over about 18 months of spare-time evening/weekend labour — and separately priced a **new** road trailer at £1,092, nearly double the £528.99 the secondhand trailer and lock had cost in the 2007 breakdown, consistent with costs having "wildly escalated" since then, especially epoxy resin and plywood.[29]
- **Plywood, cloth, and epoxy quantities, itemized by thickness.** A Paradox build's typical bill of materials: **2 sheets of 18mm, 4 sheets of 12mm, 3 sheets of 9mm, and 1 sheet of 6mm** plywood (marine ply costs more than the plans' suggested exterior-grade alternative, trading cost against longevity); **no glass cloth is strictly required**, but covering the deck and sides with **6 oz cloth** (about **14 yards**) and protecting the bottom with **4mm of woven roving** is common practice (also optional if heavy groundings are not expected — though one builder was glad he had not skipped it, after scraping the bottom on rocks and finding only cosmetic paint scratches beneath); and **10 gallons of epoxy** total (5 gallons bought initially, another 5 gallons later) is typically enough with plenty left over.[30]
- The same recurring build-time and estimated-cost questions come up repeatedly from newcomers introducing themselves to the community; see [Paradox as a first boat — storage, beachability, and who buys the beer](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#storage-beachability-and-who-buys-the-beer) for one such introduction and the answers it drew on storage and beachability, without adding new time/cost figures beyond those already on record above.
- **Paying as you go, rather than saving up first.** Faced with the same total-cost figures above, one prospective builder chose to buy materials and components incrementally as funds allowed rather than save toward the full amount before starting — accepting a longer project and a higher total cost in exchange for being able to begin construction right away.[31]
- **A 2008 restatement: build to rough tolerance, not to the millimetre.** A prospective builder, sidelined by recent surgery, asked for a full list of the non-plywood lumber and the amount of fiberglass cloth needed to complete a Paradox.[32] Though not a direct answer, the reply restated the same practical lesson already established above: don't fuss over the exact sizing of small parts like bulkhead cleats — building to the millimetre is unnecessary when anywhere within about 5 mm is close enough — and, building again, the better approach is to buy a lot of large stock and cut it to size as required with a table saw rather than pre-planing pieces to exact dimensions in advance.[33]
- **A fiberglass-cloth answer to the same question.** Dave Bolduc, who has fiberglassed both Little Cruiser and Swamp Thing himself, gave the cloth side of the same lumber-and-glass question a fuller, comparative answer: **4 oz cloth on the topsides, 6 oz on the sides, and several layers of 10 oz on the bottom** is one well-tried combination — 10 oz judged easier to finish than woven roving. Little Cruiser's own bottom took 5 layers of 10 oz, more than he would use again (3-4 layers, with hindsight); Swamp Thing needed only 2 layers of 10 oz, the same amount Matt Layden used on Enigma. For a Paradox, a working minimum is **15 yards of 10 oz, 8 yards of 6 oz, and 8 yards of 4 oz**, buying extra to also cover the rudder/blade and mast.[34][35]
- **A 2015 budget bracket, and how cheap the electronics side can actually be.** A prospective builder, not yet begun cutting wood, floated a pre-build working range: roughly **$2,000** for a bare-bones build in lumberyard materials, cheap epoxy, and porch paint, against roughly **$12,000** for a build in marine plywood, West System epoxy, two-part marine paint, state-of-the-art electronics, and a new outboard.[145] The $12,000 figure drew a sharp real-world corrective on the electronics side alone: a GPS for under £100, a handheld VHF for under £80, a compass received as a Christmas present, and a nav/anchor light improvised from a converted garden solar lamp — a working navigation suite for well under £200 total.[146] Restating the top end as a deliberately "gold-plated" figure — marine plywood throughout, multiple batteries, solar panels, a new outboard, two-part epoxy marine paint, a full suite of electronic toys, and a new trailer — $12,000 was confirmed as an outside/upper number, with the builder's own expected total closer to the $2,000 end.[147] Asked in turn where the boat was planned to sail, the reply named the Bahamas and the Gulf of California in Mexico.[148]
- **The same thread drew three builders' own running costs, and a dissent on epoxy brand.** A prospective New Zealand builder put his own likely total at about **NZD8,000 (USD5,500)**, nearly half of that for a bespoke trailer alone, and argued for marine plywood and West System epoxy throughout even under a rough "work boat" paint finish, reasoning that a Paradox is meant to last and that the materials under the paint matter to resale value; full text and citation also at [boats/mini.md](/boats/mini.md#the-build-2014-2015).[149] An Australian builder gave his own materials spend as **A$2,000 for wood and the same again for epoxy**, all marine-grade throughout; full text and citation also at [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#materials-cost-and-a-modified-jet-ski-trailer).[150] A UK builder seconded the case for a proper bespoke trailer over an off-the-shelf one, having designed and built his own economically after a "normal" trailer proved "disastrous"; full text and citation also at [operations/trailering.md](/operations/trailering.md#designing-and-building-your-own-trailer-economically-rather-than-buying-bespoke-2015).[151] A dissenting voice on brand: **West System** is "outdated and expensive" next to "excellent non-blushing" alternatives — **MAS** throughout one UK build, at a total epoxy bill of about **£400**; full text and citation also at [construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md](/construction/epoxy-brands-and-chemistry.md#brands-used-in-practice) and [people/pete-martin.md](/people/pete-martin.md#a-voice-among-fellow-builders).[152]
- **A UK builder's own cloth-weave follow-up.** Having already bought 450 g/m² biaxial cloth for the bottom, one builder found suitable 4 oz (135 g/m²) cloth only in a plain weave too narrow to use as bought, and that stepping up to a 200 g/m² (roughly 6 oz) weight was only available as twill or crow's-foot weave; a 260 g/m² (7.5 oz) woven roving, wide enough to cover the whole boat, cost less than a narrower run of the lighter plain weave — raising the question of whether 7.5 oz woven roving overall was too heavy. The answer: overlap a too-narrow cloth rather than special-order a wider roll; a heavier woven roving will need more epoxy to fill its weave; 6 oz was the preferred weight among the options on the table; and, with time short, buying small samples of each candidate weave to try firsthand beats guessing from the numbers alone.[36][37] A supply of 4 and 6 oz cloth in the widths wanted duly turned up and arrived; the cold weather it had been a race against had by then already caught up regardless — down to about 4°C at night, barely 13°C by day even when dry and sunny — so the cabin top became the winter's job instead, with plenty to keep the builder occupied until spring.[38]
- **A 2021 update, as prices began to rise.** Answering the same recurring build-or-buy cost question, [Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md) gave an itemized total for "[Thor](/boats/thor.md)" — A$2,000 for wood and resin, A$1,000 for a professionally made sail, A$900 for a jet-ski trailer, and A$1,200 for a Honda 2.3 hp outboard — and, pressed further, an all-up total of **A$7,000**, up from the A$2,000-wood/A$2,000-epoxy figure he had given the same boat in 2015 (see [Thor — materials cost](/boats/thor.md#materials-cost-and-a-modified-jet-ski-trailer)).[159] A separate reply warned that quality timber prices might already have doubled from what earlier builders had paid, owing to a supply-and-demand imbalance, and judged a well-built secondhand Paradox in rated marine plywood and epoxy likely cheaper, over the next year or two, than building one from scratch — advising against lesser materials regardless of price.[160] The same exchange doubled as Bell's own for-sale listing for "Thor," citing declining health; the boat and her full specification and price history are at [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#sold-across-the-country-and-renamed-pamela-b-2022), and she sold in February 2022 to [Mark Brothers](/people/mark-brothers.md) of New South Wales.

## Difficulty

- Building a Paradox is "both challenging and rewarding," with "some tricky details to attend to" — even for a professional.[39] Community support and documents like the ["Building the Paradox" articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) (e.g. the coach-roof jig) are recommended to help builders finish.

## Practicing epoxy and glassing on a simpler boat first

A builder confident in general woodworking but new to epoxy and fiberglassing, having started construction three months ahead of his own stated schedule, weighed practicing those two specific skills on a simple secondary boat before risking mistakes directly on the Paradox itself — reasoning that the Paradox's own large parts count leaves plenty of non-epoxy tasks (the yuloh, the mast) to make progress on in parallel while epoxy technique is still being learned.[40] Two practice-boat candidates were considered: a Puddle Duck Racer (see [Gorfnik](/similar-boat-designs/gorfnik-paraduck.md) for the design's own Paradox-adjacent history) and an unnamed plywood single-sheet 8-footer, the latter advised against as reportedly not a good sailor.[41][42] The PDR was favored as the safer bet for a beginner — its roughly 600 lb displacement was framed as keeping a new sailor "high and dry" through the early weeks of sailing, and building one at all was framed as serving a boatbuilding apprenticeship on a simple "Duck" rather than risking those same early mistakes on the Paradox.[41][43][42]

Weighing the idea further against his own coastal conditions and stated impatience to finish the actual boat, the same builder judged a practice boat would likely be sailed no more than once, and leaned instead toward simply testing epoxy technique on scrap plywood offcuts and applying the lesson directly to the Paradox build rather than completing a separate practice boat at all.[44][45] The next day this builder — [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md), whose boat a later census would identify as "[Wizard Spawn](/boats/wizard-spawn.md)" — moved on to the deck and cabin beams; see [boats/wizard-spawn.md](/boats/wizard-spawn.md) for the full continuing account.

## Tools

Photos showing 15 or more clamps in use for laminating deck beams can look alarming, but the clamp count is a function of lamination thickness rather than a sign of an unusually demanding build: laminating with **thinner strips than necessary** takes more clamps than a thicker lamination would.[46][47] A builder unsure how thick to laminate can cut a few **test pieces at different thicknesses** and try a dry fit around the beam-laminating jig without steaming — using the thickest stock that will still bend to shape minimizes both the clamp count and the number of glue lines.[47]

Tool needs and quality generally creep up as a build proceeds — a first boat can be built with mostly a coping saw and a block plane, though few builders who have since acquired a fuller kit could imagine going back to so little.[47][48] A long tool list should not intimidate a first-time builder: the practical approach is to amass tools the same way the whole build itself is best tackled — eat the elephant "one bite at a time."[47]

A recurring newcomer question — whether a table saw is truly essential as one build manual suggests, or a bandsaw more useful, and whether "Skilsaw" simply means an ordinary handheld circular saw — was answered from three separate builders' own experience; see [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#a-uk-builders-workspace-and-questions-on-tools-and-plywood-sourcing).[49] If only one saw could be afforded, an **orbital jigsaw** with good-quality blades does an amazing amount of cutting; a **Skilsaw** (handheld circular saw) is also very useful, especially for ripping long boards into strips for framing, provided the work is clamped to a stable surface first — a small shop will often rip strips to order cheaply and more accurately than a home setup can manage. A **"panel saw"** — a high-revving handheld circular saw taking only a 5 1/2 in blade — is well suited to sawing plywood and follows curves easily if the blade is set to just clear the cut's underside, and is light to handle. With tools like these, neither a table saw nor a bandsaw is strictly necessary.[50] One UK builder confirmed the same conclusion from his own completed Paradox: he never used a table saw or bandsaw, instead shaping all his plywood with an ordinary handheld electric jigsaw and cutting, planing, and hand-sanding his mast, boom, and yuloh with a plain rip saw. Good tools make the job easier and quicker, but surprisingly few are actually necessary to build a Paradox — the one tool that cannot be done without is the **clamp**: more than 30 were needed just for the sheer strakes and chine logs, with two 5 ft bar clamps and real physical effort needed to pull the chine logs into place at full dimension, unlaminated, a job made easier by doing it in hot sunshine to soften the wood. His own compiled tools list (see [Tools](#tools) above) was, in hindsight, longer than it needed to be, and he planned to revise it down.[51] A specific jigsaw recommendation followed: a sale-priced kit bundling a jigsaw with a 1/4 in-pad finishing sander, around $80 CDN; for hand-sawing, a Japanese pull-stroke saw such as a ryoba, with hardened teeth, was also suggested as a worthwhile alternative.[52]

A further builder confirmed the same "no table saw needed" conclusion from his own completed build: bulkheads were cut by hand, or with a circular saw, or a combination of both, with no table saw at any stage. Fitting cleats went the same way — a rough bevel cut from the plan first, then finished with a hand plane during dry assembly once the actual angle could be checked against the parts it joins, rather than trying to cut it exactly beforehand.[161] A further builder who did buy a table saw ended up barely using it — worth having only for ripping one's own lumber from rough stock, since lumber bought already rough-cut by the supplier needed no more than cleaning up with a hand plane.[162]

**A full annotated tool list**, working from the recommended "Building the Paradox" list with a builder's field notes added against each item. The **most-used power tools** were the drill, jig saw, and angle grinder. Beyond those: acid brushes (cheap market-bought ones, about 10p each, washed in vinegar and soap for reuse or simply discarded); bar clamps for the hull sides; a flexible batten for fairing curves; bread pans for casting lead ballast pigs; screw clamps (2 at 3 in, 4 at 9 in, and 2 at 15 in is "enough, but more would be better"); large spring clamps, bought as a mixed-size market pack, for temporarily joining side panels to the stem; a wide Stanley-knife-type cutter for fibreglass; a battery drill with keyless chuck (usable in place of a separate electric screwdriver); a long drill bit for the vent trunk drain; drinking straws for flicking dirt out of wet epoxy; a low-angle Stanley No. 118 plane for shaping the bilge runners and cabin parts; a borrowed power planer (only needed for shaping the mast); bin liners as cheap plastic sheeting when glassing the bottom; a Stanley No. 21-115 Surform; a table saw (a purchase one builder, in hindsight, could have done without); and yoghurt pots for mixing small batches of epoxy — with about 1.5 gallons of the cheaper brown vinegar, rather than white, consumed along the way.[53]

Lead ballast pigs can be cast in either of two shapes, with different trade-offs. The **original wedge shape** a bread pan produces makes pigs light enough (about 12 kg each) to lift with one hand; painting them with Hammerite while still warm from casting settles the handling question fast — "in a couple of minutes your worries about handling lead are over."[54] A later refinement moved to **square, elongated forms** (roughly 3 x 3 x 12 in, molded in plywood boxes), melted down and re-poured from the original wedge shape;[55] see [the full ballast pig layout](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#the-ballast-pig-layout-in-full-don-elliott) for the fully worked-out account of that square-pig approach.

A **Nicholson pattern maker's rasp** — sold in a couple of sizes, with one flat and one convex face and a random (non-grooving) tooth pattern — drew a strong recommendation as a superior alternative to an ordinary rasp or the Surform mentioned above: with firm pressure it cuts aggressively, and with light pressure it leaves a finish equivalent to about 50-grit sandpaper with very little cleanup needed, making it well suited to complex shapes such as the Paradox's chine runners.[56] It is not cheap (around $20, a price paid some 25 years earlier) and can be hard to find, but is available from **Lee Valley**.[57] An angle grinder is a workable, if "not very delicate," alternative for shaping chine runners.[58]

**Sourcing one outside North America.** A UK builder unable to find a Nicholson rasp locally was pointed to several mail-order options that ship internationally — sculpt.com, Nolan Supply, Lee Valley, and Garrett Wade (the priciest, but with the fullest product description) — plus a German-made half-round pattern maker's rasp (fine-tools.com) that looks similar though not identical to the Nicholson, untested but suggested as worth trying in person at a local tool shop first, since "a good tool is a tactile thing."[59][60] Lee Valley's price, once tracked down, ran to more than double what was originally paid for the tool 25 years earlier — still judged worth it over time, including for shaping paddles and oars.[57][61] Only the single **coarse** rasp is worth buying, though — a second, finer one is redundant next to ordinary small sanding files, which are considerably cheaper.[62]

**Authorship of the tools list, and a separate materials-list copyright question.** The tools list circulated on [the Paradox UK website](/references/paradox-uk-website.md) is an independent compilation of the tools used to build one Paradox — not the builder's own list, and not attributable to any single individual — offered as a useful starting point for prospective builders, and posted freely at `paradox-uk.co.uk` with no copyright claim.[63][64] A separate **Building Materials list** is a different document: it came as part of a bought plans package and may genuinely be under copyright, so adding it to the group's FAQ file needed confirmation from [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) or the design's distributor that doing so would not breach it.[64] Neither list can really be copyrighted in the stricter sense, since each is only a set of suggestions rather than anything "carved in stone" — but crediting the builder as the source of the tools he used is proper attribution regardless.[65]

## The Build Manual's introduction

A community-written **Build Manual** (see [proposed a group-written Paradox build manual](/people/david-c-smith.md#contributions-and-stories)) began with a draft introduction open to additions, omissions, and edits, with sections on particular tasks — spars, rudder, yuloh, vent trunk, and others — contributed by volunteers from the builders' group;[66] a volunteer took the [yuloh section](/people/david-beard.md#community-and-mentorship).[67] The manual drew early encouragement from the group as "an invaluable asset to future builders."[68]

The introduction sets the manual's scope and philosophy: it exists to guide builders of [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s Paradox design, referencing the design's own specifications throughout, and while a builder may prefer to alter details to their own taste, following the design as drawn is generally recommended — the same designer-fidelity credo argued for elsewhere (see [a designer-fidelity credo](/people/david-c-smith.md#a-designer-fidelity-credo)). It points builders with design questions to the [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) website, and to the wider Paradoxbuilders group's own accumulated photos, files, and archived messages. It describes the Paradox as a heavy-displacement sharpie type, proven through empirical testing of scale models and preceding vessels of the same lineage.[66]

Practical guidance in the introduction includes: the plans use metric measurements throughout, and while this may unsettle North American builders at first, it is easily mastered with a metric tape measure and an appropriate scale rule (1:10 and 1:20); additional copies of the plans may be made for the original purchaser's own use, and having a copy at the build location, at a computer, and even in the head is worthwhile, since time spent studying the plans is time well spent; and a build journal or notes, plus a camera, are recommended as construction proceeds. Material choice is left to the individual builder — the designer has suggested many options, from ACX to marine-grade plywood and Douglas Fir to Mahogany — so the manual generally references plywood and lumber ambiguously except where a specific species matters. A materials list should be worked out by each builder individually, both to ensure adequate study of the plans and because a list compiled by one builder may not suit another builder's tools and techniques; a chronological task list is recommended to aid organization. Materials need not all be purchased at once, though bulk purchase (5 gallons of epoxy, 10 sheets of plywood, a spool of line) can realize savings. The manual itself is arranged in a logical build order, but a builder may undertake smaller tasks — rudder, spars, sail, yuloh — in whatever order suits poor weather, economics, or curing times, so long as task dependencies are respected.[66]

## The Build Manual's "Preparations" installment: build site and a full tool inventory

Continuing the Build Manual (see [The Build Manual's introduction](#the-build-manuals-introduction)), a **"Preparations"** installment covers build site and tools — the manual's fullest single tool inventory.[69]

**Build site.** Beyond the builder's own physical comfort, a Paradox can be built anywhere shelter can be arranged against dew, rain, and airborne debris (dust, pollen, spores) — at minimum, a tarp strung between trees suffices. A build site with no heating or cooling must be factored into material choices, particularly epoxy and paint products, which have their own working-temperature ranges. A garage is the optimal setting but is not strictly necessary; a dolly or rolling cradle helps move the boat around a tight space as needed (compare the roller-skate solution to the same problem, below).[69]

**Tools, as a suggested rather than fixed list.** Tool recommendations are inherently personal — builders vary in experience and tool availability, some collecting the finest available tools and others making do with hardware-store basics, some assembling a complete inventory before starting and others acquiring tools only as needed, and some (not recommended) building an entire boat without ever using a power tool.[69] A comprehensive list by category:

- **Basic tools:** 16-20 oz claw hammer; 2-3 lb deadblow mallet; punches and nailsets; screwdrivers; vicegrips; wrenches; ratchet/sockets; putty knives; plastic spreaders; scissors; utility knife; halogen lamps; hot glue gun; at least two sawhorses; two 4x4x8 ft sleepers; a higher-quality Workmate; and a stationary bench or dedicated sawhorses topped with a sheet of heavy ply.
- **Measuring tools:** a triangular metric scale (1:5, 1:10, 1:20); a combination metric/imperial tape measure; a calculator; dividers; a tri square; a bevel gauge and bevel board; a 4 ft straight edge; a carpenter's square and a framing square; seine twine for a string line; marking chalk; pens, pencils, and assorted battens.
- **Edge tools:** a block plane; assorted chisels; a 1 in scraper and a cabinet scraper; a power planer.
- **Abrasive tools:** assorted files and rasps; a rubber sanding block; an orbital palm sander; an autobody longboard; a shop-made sanding board (36 grit, a 3 in x 24 in belt tightly fitted to plywood); a 4 in angle grinder; an 8 in grinder (light, vertical motor, fitted with a soft pad and Stikit disks); and assorted barrel sander sleeves for a drill motor.
- **Saws:** hacksaw; dovetail saw; jigsaw; 7 1/4 in skilsaw.
- **Rotary tools:** electric drills in 3/8 in and 1/2 in chuck sizes; twist bits to 1/2 in; assorted spade and Forstner bits; fuller taper countersinks; a 5/8 in metal countersink; hole saws as needed; a router/laminate trimmer with assorted roundover bits.
- **Clamps:** pipe clamps in 3, 4, and 5 ft lengths (as many as possible); 2 ft bar clamps; roughly twenty 6 in C-clamps; and around six 3 in metal spring clamps — the rule of thumb throughout: "more the better."
- **Stationary power tools:** table saw; bandsaw; surface planer; drill press.
- **Safety:** dust masks; a chemical respirator with cartridges; latex gloves; pigskin MIG-welding gloves; safety glasses; a face shield; hearing protection; medical scrubs or Tyvek coveralls; a box fan for ventilation.
- **Consumable supplies:** 1 in blue masking tape; masking paper; waxed paper; paste car wax; sheet plastic; blue poly tarp; plastic cups (yogurt, cottage cheese, whipped-topping tubs); unwaxed paper paint pots; cardboard; doorskin; assorted sandpaper; cotton terry rags; denatured alcohol; lacquer thinner; acetone; assorted throwaway brushes; acid brushes; waterless hand cleaner.[69][70]

This inventory complements, rather than replaces, the earlier annotated tool list above (see [Tools](#tools)) — the two lists overlap heavily, but that one carries per-item field notes from an actual build, while this categorized inventory serves as a fuller planning checklist for a builder starting from scratch. See [deck and cabin beam lamination — the Build Manual's "Deck Beam Template" and "Laminating Jigs" installment](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#the-build-manuals-deck-beam-template-and-laminating-jigs-installment) for the manual's next installment, on deck beams.[71]

A comprehensive list like this is a useful planning reference, but it can also read as intimidating: one builder, seeing the complete list only after finishing his own boat, judged that he owned barely a tenth of the tools it names — a newcomer weighing whether to start a Paradox at all should bear in mind that the boat has, in practice, been built with a small fraction of it.[72]

**A "workshop or a boat?" pushback, and the case for a tiered list.** A comprehensive list like this one can be judged "wildly excessive" — none of the stationary power tools are strictly needed, a potential saving of "well over $5,000." A telling counter-example: the designer of a boat far fiddlier than a Paradox (its stem alone "much more architectural") built the prototype, with his wife, using only an electric drill and an electric planer as power tools, "hiring" a circular saw just once to trim the mast's edges.[73] One proposed fix is not to drop the comprehensive list but to **tier it** — an absolute-minimum list first, then a supplementary "these make it easier" list, and only then, if wanted, a "luxury, easy living" list — reserving the full inventory for builders chasing a "gentleman woodcrafter" market rather than simply a finished boat.[74]

The case for the list's comprehensiveness, on the other side, is that it was compiled as suggested rather than minimal, since what is minimal for one builder is luxuriously expansive for another — a full set of stationary power tools can be assembled for as little as **$1,200 new**, with an additional drill press (**$300** more) reasonably deferred until an actual need arises, "maybe next week, perhaps next year." Buying tools prized for rosewood handles, brass fittings, Japanese forging, laser engraving, or "even vented bass speakers" — ornamental qualities aimed at a "modern outlook" that admires tools rather than uses them — is one way spending can run needlessly high; plain, functional tools bought from old-time boatbuilders serve just as well as anything from a "coffee table catalog." A builder dissatisfied with any published list is free to compile a personal one, the same way a materials list is worked out individually — but the more important discipline either way is to start building before either list is complete.[75] The deterrent effect of a long tool list on likely first-time builders is nonetheless real, reinforcing the case for leading with a bare minimum and layering in "nice to have" and "luxury" tiers only afterward.[74]

**Old tools over new: a case for buying secondhand.** Against the "modern outlook" that prizes ornamental, over-marketed new tools, the fuller case for fine antique tools runs as follows: tool steel, rosewood, and brass handles are not a modern marketing invention but a genuinely old tradition, and the way to own such tools cheaply is patience rather than a full-price purchase — accumulating them over years from estate and farm sales, through trade, or as gifts from older tradesmen glad to see good tools passed to a new generation of craftsmen, rather than paying anything like list price for them new. The practical advice for a builder weighing whether an old tool is worth buying: strike when the opportunity arises rather than waiting until a current project justifies the purchase, since a fine tool at a good price will always find appreciative hands and repay its cost through use or trade — accepting that a "new" find may need a day or more of derusting, cleaning, sharpening, and tuning before it can be used. Tools of genuine age — Victorian-era examples that have passed through five or more generations of craftsmen's hands are on record — could not have survived that much use if inferior, and while such age can make a tool valuable to collectors, that is beside the point of actually owning and using it: well-made old tools are simply more enjoyable and more accurate to work with, and are sometimes better suited to a job than a modern equivalent (planes made for pitch-laden wood are one example).[76]

A builder's-eye distinction cuts the debate a different way: a tradesman or a hobbyist planning to keep building benefits from investing in good tools over time, but a one-boat builder building from necessity should simply start with whatever tools are on hand and buy more only as a real need for them appears. A minimal starting kit — three saws, three chisels, a brace, a hand drill, a No. 4 plane, a combination square, and assorted pencils and rules, with an ordinary drill mounted in a stand as an improvised pillar drill for a first power tool — is enough to get going. Fine tools are a pleasure to own but are not necessary to build one plywood boat; power tools mainly save time, at some cost in finger-safety margin; and a builder unsure where to start should buy the timber first, then acquire tools to suit the specific job at hand — "the price of ply is daunting enough" on its own.[77]

**Builders vs. boaters, and essential vs. desirable tools.** Prospective Paradox owners split into two types: those who enjoy building boats (the majority) and those who mainly want to go boating and see building as the price of admission. For the latter — never having wanted to build a boat before Paradox, and with no interest in building another once finished — splitting the tool list into **essential** and **desirable** categories makes more sense than treating it as one comprehensive whole: buying only the essential tools, cheaply, sufficient to last through one build (a cordless drill, an angle grinder, and a jigsaw, bought specifically for the project and cheap enough that their survival to the end of the build was all that mattered) keeps costs down, even allowing for a table saw purchase some builders come to regret.[78] It should be possible to complete a Paradox spending well under about £50 on tools, and the wider tool-list debate should not be read as implying otherwise, for fear of discouraging exactly this kind of builder from starting at all.[78] A **6 in orbital sander**, rated excellent for aggressive sanding work, is one specific addition worth making to an essentials-first kit.[79]

**A working builder's practical middle ground.** Between a comprehensive list and the minimal-tools camp sits a simple practical answer: a table saw becomes something a builder "cannot imagine working without" once owned (one is on record at $75 secondhand), yet a Paradox could genuinely be built with nothing more than a hatchet and hand saw — it just "would not be fun." A builder does not need every tool on a published list, but having the full list in front of you, or at least handy, gives a **point of departure**: start building, and when a real need for a particular tool actually arises, decide then whether it is worth adding to the tool chest.[80]

**An outside professional boat designer's endorsement of a minimal tool kit.** A New Zealand small-boat designer and author added independent, professional-builder weight to the minimalist side of the debate. Tool "need," in this framing, is as much a perception shaped by advertisers convincing buyers they "have to have" particular tools as it is a real requirement — to be weighed against the time and money a given builder actually has. Despite owning a comparatively well-stocked shop, it was judged no real inconvenience to build a Paradox with far less: the book *The New Zealand Backyard Boatbuilder* pictures on page 33 a single carry-box of tools, liftable and carried in one hand, sufficient to build most small boats. One first big boat, a 21-footer, was built with only a single power tool — a 3/8 in-chuck, two-speed Hitachi drill — carried entirely in one toolbox; the only real cost was extra sweat hand-ripsawing long stringers and the hardwood keelson. Everything beyond a minimal kit falls into a "nice to have" category, and it is easy to overspend on tools to the point of merely enriching the tool-shop owner rather than finishing a boat — Nelson's *Victory*, among other historic ships, was built entirely with hand tools, and a full stationary-power-tool shop only makes sense for a builder setting up production runs. That designer had built **19 boats in about 18 years** and was about to start a 12-ton cruiser.[81] A dry coda on the hand-tools point: if Nelson's *Victory* and her contemporaries were built without power tools, it was not for want of trying — the Romans had already worked out how to drive a saw with water power.[82]

## Workspace

- An **all-weather shelter** to build under — allowing work to continue regardless of conditions rather than waiting out rain or cold — is worth having on a second build.[158]
- The boat can be built in a **very small space**: one builder's own boat went together in a **garage only about 2 ft longer than the finished boat**, doing as much as possible before assembling the hull.[83]
- A build is even practical for an **apartment dweller with no garage**: one Los Angeles builder planned to build in the space of a single outdoor parking spot (roughly 8 ft by 15 ft), with additional temporary room available just to its front, timing the build for the winter months to avoid the local summer heat.[84]
- Moving a part-built hull between a workshop and the outdoors is itself worth planning for. One builder, working on "Enuf" in a workshop separate from the driveway, built a **trolley** from a recycled, welded-on wheel set (freed with an angle grinder) bolted onto a new wooden frame, then raised the hull with **levers, wood blocks, trolley jacks, and axle stands** to slide the trolley underneath. The trolley worked well moving the boat back and forth once assembled — but on one attempt to push the hull back into the workshop, its front wheels caught on the workshop floor's half-inch door-sill lip, stopped dead, and tore loose from the wooden frame, leaving the hull to skid off its trolley and back onto its original resting blocks.[85]
- **Moving a finished boat solo, without a trolley mishap.** One builder built an alternative for turning [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) over: **"the big wheel,"** which lets one move the boat around **solo** using it together with a **block and tackle** and a jack, rather than depend on a neighbor or a helper, as before — a helper-dependent method found unpredictable and always feared to damage the boat. The solo method is slower but avoids the risk of working with others who may handle a heavy hull less carefully.[86] A **simpler wheeled cradle** is another workable option — a spare set of dinghy-trailer wheels and an axle strapped to a wooden baulk positioned under the hull's point of balance lets the boat slide easily along timber and concrete once set up this way.[87]
- **Getting a raised hull's side vertical for glassing, without cutting wedges.** With the hull up on baulks of timber roughly 10 in off the floor ahead of glassing one side, a simpler alternative to cutting long wedges to correct a side sloping inward and away from the builder is to nail a pair of short stops to one end of the baulks, stopping the boat from sliding away, and to lift the opposite side on a trolley jack instead.[88]
- **How heavy the bare hull is, for manhandling past an obstruction.** The bare hull structure runs to roughly **400 lb** before ballast, tanks, or gear are added (see [weight, ballast, and displacement — loaded-weight breakdown](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#loaded-weight-breakdown-don-elliotts-boat)), making her light enough for the trolley- and cradle-based moving methods above rather than requiring a full crew to lift her clear.[89] Two further independent guesses converge on the same range: **450–500 lb**,[90] and, drawing on the "Zoë" build, **350–400 lb before cabinets and ballast**[91] — both consistent with the ~400 lb design figure above. At that weight, no single build stage stands out as especially time-consuming, but **scarf joints in the panels were consistently irritating** for at least one builder (who switched to a lapped butt joint for the floor instead), and **chine logs were troublesome** for the same builder (narrowing them made them easier to work, and leaving the vent box out until after the bulkheads are fitted is worth avoiding, having proved a mistake in practice). Pre-shaped kit panels would not have detracted from the pleasure of building.[92][93] At the hull-only stage, a bare hull can be lifted easily by hand by three people; further along, with decks going on and some ballast aboard, sliding the hull around solo on wooden blocks becomes possible, and 5–6 fit people could lift her a foot or so into the air at that stage.[93]
- **A bare hull can be safely set down or left mid-move without damage.** Even if a moving crew tires out partway through, the hull itself is in no danger from being propped up on rocks or other rough support — there is "no danger of putting a hole" in the Paradox's 18 mm bottom.[94] Manhandling labor scales with the size of the moving crew rather than its members' individual strength.[95][96]
- **Moving a full 8x4 sheet of plywood alone**, before it is cut down into panels, is a recurring nuisance solvable a few ways: a rope sling — a length of rope a bit over 8 ft long with a loop at each end, one loop over each bottom corner of the sheet stood on edge, held together in one hand at the sheet's center while the other hand steadies the top edge — still takes effort but noticeably less than an unaided carry; alternatively, a board fitted with four castors makes a simple all-purpose trolley for wheeling sheets around a workspace.[97][98] A similarly effective handle for dragging or carrying a sheet solo is a 4 in C-clamp fastened to one edge (sometimes two, one at each end); see [JoHanna — bulkhead #3's deck-area curve, and the rudder-stock cutting order](/boats/johanna.md#bulkhead-3s-deck-area-curve-and-the-rudder-stock-cutting-order) for the exchange this answered.[99] Short lengths of plastic pipe laid under a sheet standing on edge work very well as rollers, moving it with very little effort — a fourth, low-cost alternative to the sling, castor trolley, and C-clamp handle.[100]
- **Kit-cut panels versus building from scratch.** A precut kit would only save time, not skill or accomplishment — one of Paradox's own appeals being that a builder can complete her from start to finish. Even an experienced builder using no kit can find the build, in spite of mistakes made along the way, thoroughly enjoyable, and "a bit more complicated than an Ikea flat pack" — many such mistakes coming down to not having read the instructions closely enough.[95][96] The design's real case rests on its practical **track record** rather than on paper: Paradox has "done so much in reality, not just on paper," and essentially all serious negative criticism of the design on record comes from theoreticians who have never owned or sailed one, not from actual owners.[96]
- **A wheeled dolly for a tight garage.** Mounting the whole hull on a wheeled dolly through the build lets it be pushed outside for easier access rather than working entirely inside a cramped bay — one builder's own garage-based build (a UK garage, typically smaller than an American one) used exactly this method.[101]
- **Doing all scarfing and spar/rudder work before assembling the hull.** For a builder with no outdoor space to fall back on, the recommended sequence is to finish every task possible — scarfing, and building the spars and rudder — before joining the hull together: practically, this keeps room free for that work rather than losing it to a finished hull; psychologically, it means being ready to launch the moment the hull itself is done rather than waiting on the pieces still outstanding. The same approach was independently reached by [one small-workspace builder](/people/rodney-cannon.md) planning his own build around a move.[102] A further advantage of the same sequence surfaced from a builder expecting to relocate every few years over the course of a long build: an unassembled pile of frames, plywood, and chines is simply much easier to move house with than a part-built hull.[103] The same sequencing advice — finish the small stuff while the workspace is still clear, so the day the hull itself is done there is nothing left outstanding but to launch — has since been given independently by a second builder answering the same newcomer's question.[104] A 2015 exchange restated the same practice with a further rationale, and drew a genuine counter-view. One builder, with the bulkheads done, the hull sides scarfed and glued, and every appendage but the sail already made, argued the sequence matters because a small workspace leaves no room to build the appendages once the hull is assembled, and because the temptation to shortcut or skip the appendages entirely grows once a boat-shaped hull already exists — better to spend the initial burst of enthusiasm on the fiddly parts first.[137] Against that, a fellow prospective builder — having already built two smaller boats without ever finishing either one's sailing rig — argued the opposite: save the mast, booms, and other sailing parts for last, since a boat can be used without her sails but the sails cannot be used without the boat, and getting something that at least looks like a finished boat first sustains motivation better than fiddly appendage work with nothing to show for it.[138]
- **Building an entire Paradox from a city apartment, with no garage or yard at all.** One builder stored a completed bulkhead in every closet of the apartment, planned to hang the finished spar from hooks screwed into the ceiling, hand-sanded small parts in the bathtub or bathroom at night, and did power-sanding and cutting outdoors by day. His plan for final hull assembly was to transfer the hull onto a dolly and occupy his own car's parking space with it, parking the car on the street instead and keeping the hull under a tarp between sessions. See [Wizard Spawn — still building in Venice Beach](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#still-building-in-venice-beach-over-a-year-on-october-2009).[105]
- **A garage bay shared with a car, and a dolly for outside storage.** One workable arrangement moves the boat in and out of a shared garage bay as needed, keeping it inside for a few days at a stretch for particular operations, then storing it outside in a covered area measuring roughly **14 ft by 5 ft by 4 ft** — tight, but workable — on a purpose-built dolly, which can later double as a loader for transferring the finished boat onto a tilt-away utility trailer.[106] A photograph from the "Aussie Paradox" photo album, showing the hull afloat with her ground plate visible, and further photos of people standing beside their own finished boats, were enough to confirm by eye that a Paradox would indeed fit such a covered space.[107]

## Building Paradox as a high-school class project

A Rhode Island charter high school took on a Paradox build as a hands-on educational project, growing out of a volunteer sailing-mentorship program already using science and physics projects to teach students aboard a private boat. The school's own liberal off-campus-work policy, comprehensive insurance, and background-checking already covered mentoring students at forges, auto garages, and a water-treatment plant, and extended readily to boatbuilding; materials donated by local boat yards would be tax-deductible for the donor. The envisioned build order starts with structural members and accessories — rig, rudder assembly — before the hull itself is assembled, partly to keep the option of moving the project open; a solar/wind 12V electrical system paired with electric propulsion was floated as a further draw for students specifically interested in "green energy," alongside the boatbuilding itself. Left open on record: which tools and materials are appropriate to hand a crew of high-school-age students, and which are better held back until the more dangerous or expensive stages are done outside their presence.[108]

## Staying motivated through a long build

Given how long a build can run (see the hundreds-of-hours figures above), maintaining enthusiasm during the slow winter stretch of a project is its own recognized challenge, with a standard set of countermeasures: keep a build log (progress, costs, gear wishlist, photos, eventually a simple website); develop useful skills in advance (weather prediction, navigation, boating-safety courses, a first-aid course, practicing simple cooking); buy the occasional piece of gear as a motivational reward (without overspending); and research cruising destinations via library/web rather than buying guides or charts that will age.[109] The essential discipline underlying all of it: **build on the boat at least every week**, on a regular, persistent schedule. See [the full list of motivation suggestions](/people/david-leblanc.md) for more.

A further, simpler motivational framing: one prospective builder facing a realistic **5-7 year** build, given other commitments and no plan to retire soon, aimed instead to have the boat finished in time for his own retirement, treating it as a retirement gift to himself — "a great way to stay motivated."[139]

A concrete instance of the "keep a build log" suggestion above: one builder started a public build blog explicitly to document the build for his own record and to pay forward the help he had drawn from earlier builders' own posts and websites, hoping the record — mistakes included — would help whoever came after him. Rather than working to a fixed weekly schedule, he let the blog update only as fast as the boat itself progressed, finding the absence of a deadline part of what made both building and sailing enjoyable — a different temperament from the "build every week" discipline above, but the same underlying practice of keeping a running public record.[115]

A **1/10-scale cardboard mock-up**, built ahead of starting construction, is one concrete way of keeping a stalled project moving and refreshing a builder's own sense of the interior arrangement: floppy and rough, but enough to check dimensions and layout by eye, alongside photographing a finished Paradox ("Little Jim") in person for the same purpose. Fastenings can be sourced from **Robbins Timber of Bristol**, about half the price of Classic Marine for UK builders.[110] The boat was provisionally named "So-So" at this stage — a nod to the builder's own woodworking skill and to a personalized logbook his children had given him — before later settling on "Enuf" as its final name.[111] Coming back to progress photos after a gap of weeks can reveal a build has moved on considerably further than remembered, even when the interior and close-up work still looks too rough to show publicly.[112] On the Yahoo Groups platform the archive predates, there was no way to rename a photo folder directly to match a boat's new name — only to delete it and upload a fresh one — making an early, settled choice of boat name worth having before photos accumulate.

## Starting over after total loss

A distinct, more severe kind of setback than a slow winter stretch: one builder's **Paradox-in-progress and boat shed were both destroyed** by large trees knocked down in a violent windstorm one New Year's Eve.[113] This is *possibly*, but not confirmed to be, the same builder (Sebastopol, CA) who had revealed his own from-scratch Paradox build in September 2002 — see [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md) — a plausible match given the six-month-old, early-stage build described here would trace back to roughly that same period, but the archive gives no explicit cross-reference (no repeated location, no "that's me" link between the two), so the two are kept separate per the project's identity-merge caution. The devastation killed his enthusiasm for a time, but did not end the build: he went back to cutting out pieces again, and asked what, if anything, about the early building stages had changed in the roughly six months since.[113]

The one concrete lesson-learned for a builder returning to the very earliest stage of construction: **don't bother getting cleat sizes precisely accurate** — wood planed exactly to the plans' specified dimensions "really wasn't necessary," since extra wood left on would have been fine regardless.[114] The one firm piece of advice, by contrast: **do get the plywood bulkheads the right shape**, since "everything else will follow" from a good start there.[114] This is the only recorded case of a builder losing an in-progress hull entirely to an external disaster (as opposed to abandoning a build by choice) and restarting from scratch.

## Weathering a hailstorm while stored outdoors, unfinished

A far less severe brush with the same kind of weather risk left a hull almost untouched. One builder's hull, still under construction near Dallas, Texas, was stored outdoors beneath nothing more than a plastic tarp when a hailstorm severe enough to total both of the household's parked cars, and to put softball-sized holes through neighbourhood roofs and windows, hit directly. Seeing the tarp itself shredded with holes, the builder expected the worst and put off looking for a couple of days; on finally pulling the tarp back, only a single spot on the hull needed repair.[154]

The repair itself drew two independent technique accounts. One held that a break like this, under constant bending load, needs something opposing that load once epoxy is injected into it: newsprint over the crack on both sides, then two squares of thin plywood scrap screwed right through the hull side into each other to clamp it while the epoxy cures, afterward removed, the paper sanded away, screw holes filled, and the whole area — inside and out — covered with a layer of fiberglass fabric.[155] A second account agreed the approach was sound but refined one detail — use plastic rather than newsprint, since glue otherwise seeps through the newsprint and bonds the temporary plywood to the hull — and, judging the damage minor from the photographs alone, proposed simply filling the hole with thickened epoxy over a wetting-out coat of neat epoxy, holding plastic and a board against it while it cured — clamped either by strapping the whole hull with wooden wedges driven in under the straps, or with a car jack braced between the hull and a fixed object such as a tree.[156]

## Building versus sailing, and juggling more than one project at once

Building from scratch a little at a time can spread the cost of materials out over what may end up being years, with no guarantee of ever finishing — judged, by one builder who had so far completed two much smaller boats of his own (one to an existing free design, one his own single-sheet-plywood design) but had not yet sailed either, a big jump in complexity and cost from what he already knew, though one he felt confident tackling if the cost could be spread out over time; a smaller design such as the Enigma or Elusion remained a fallback under consideration if plans for either could be found.[128]

The standing advice for a builder already partway into one project is to get it out sailing before committing further resources to a new one, since there is no way to know whether sailing itself will be enjoyed until it is actually tried.[129] Against that, there is no rule that a builder must sail the boat they build, or must have built the boat they sail: keeping more than one project going, including one that may never be finished or sailed at all, is common enough not to be a fault.[130]

One builder working his own design from a single sheet of plywood, alongside a second small hull already built but not yet sailed, put the case for building purely for its own sake plainly: not the best materials or methods, and no guarantee the design would even work, but fun and learning either way — a self-described "lifelong student at the school of hard knocks," with the build documented in an outside forum thread and a second test sail then under construction.[131]

Simple enjoyment of the process, whatever the outcome, was judged reason enough on its own.[132] The earlier sail-first advice was itself softened a day later: keeping a variety of projects going at once, rather than finishing one before starting the next, is common enough among builders generally to be unremarkable — a habit its own giver admitted sharing.[133]

The same single-sheet-plywood builder reported testing the new hull on a pond too small and sheltered to judge her properly — some breeze moved her but never let him actually sail — with a river trial still to come; lacking a rudder so far, steering was to be tried first with a paddle, and the leeboard left simply clamped in place until testing settled whether it worked and where it belonged, reasoning against fitting a permanent one until it was proven worth doing, since the hull already worked fine as a canoe or kayak if sailing failed. Should sailing itself prove disagreeable, an outboard on the Paradox would still make her worth building, since long ocean voyages were never the goal — the underlying ambition was simply a small boat to camp aboard, for which the Paradox, Enigma, or even the Elusion would all serve; two further homemade wooden boats and a plastic kayak covered the gap on the water in the meantime.[134]

Returning to the same question in April 2015, the same builder reported starting no further boats since the two already mentioned, with no immediate plans to — both had been self-described "quick and dirty" builds, and he had since deleted the build threads he once kept on the Wooden Boat Forum after enough criticism that they encouraged newcomers toward imperfect methods and materials. His own boating stays confined to ponds, rivers, and a small lake, needing neither electric nor gas propulsion — sailing is fun to play with, but a paddle serves him better there. His underlying goal remains a boat to camp in; Paradox was the only one of Matt Layden's designs with plans actually available to him, though the Enigma, Elusion, or even Sand Flea would suit his own needs better on weight and ease of build alone, and he expects to end up combining pieces of several designs rather than build any one of them exactly as drawn, to fit his own low budget — content with the two homemade boats that have already gotten him out on the water cheaply, without pushing his own crude building methods on anyone else.[141]

A week later, still weighing Jim Michalak's smaller "birdwatcher"-style boats as a stopgap, he wondered whether they could all be fitted with the same tent-style covering already used over Michalak's "AF4Breve" design's own center opening — unanswered on record — while restating that what first drew him to Paradox was being able to operate her entirely from inside the cabin, a feature he noted several of Michalak's designs share too; not yet ready to start another boat, and having raised, then set aside without elaborating, one further Matt Layden design, "Dockbox."[142]
