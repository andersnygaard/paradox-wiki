# Scarf joints vs. butt blocks, and scarf orientation

Practical joinery guidance for lengthening plywood panels on the [Paradox](/paradox.md), covering the choice between a true scarf and a butt block, and the correct orientation of a scarf joint.

## Butt block vs. true scarf

Building drawing #6 specifies a **scarf joint** just forward of bulkhead #3. A butt block *will* work as a substitute — and would in any case be hidden, behind the flotation/insulation foam on a side panel, or inside a storage compartment or the water tank on the bottom — but it will not give as **fair a curve** as a true scarf: a butt block leaves a slight discontinuity in the panel's fairness that a tapered scarf avoids.[1][2]

A further variant, raised independently by a newcomer about to start his own build, asks whether a **plain butt joint** — no backing block, no router-cut lap, no scarf taper, just two panel edges butted square and taped over with fiberglass cloth and epoxy on both faces — is strong enough for joining hull panels to length. This sits close to the "Payson joint" described above, which sands and tapes only one face; whether taping both faces is necessary, or how the strength compares to either the Payson joint or a true scarf, is left unanswered on record.[3]

Scarfing is "easier than you think." In the era before modern epoxies, a scarf's strength depended on a tight, precisely-fitted joint; **with today's epoxies the glue itself is stronger than the surrounding wood**, so the joint no longer has to be geometrically perfect to hold. A practical tip: **practice scarfing on scrap** left over from cutting the bulkheads before cutting into the actual panel stock.[2] This matches the "epoxy is stronger than the wood" framing already recorded for other joints — see [fastening and gluing](/construction/fastening-and-gluing.md).

## Scarf orientation: forward panel overlays the aft

Once the hull is glassed over, a scarf joint's orientation "really doesn't matter" **structurally** — but from a **hydrodynamic** standpoint, the **forward panel should overlay the aft panel**, so water flowing aft along the hull does not catch an exposed edge.[4] On a hull that isn't glassed above the waterline, this orientation matters more, since there is no glass to smooth over an exposed edge.[5]

A practical **cutting caution** applies for builders who stack two panels and cut both scarfs in one pass: because the "**bad side**" (the less attractive face veneer) should end up on the **outside**, to be hidden under glass, stacking the panels for a single cut means **one of the two scarf cuts inevitably falls on the good side** of its panel — worth planning for before cutting, rather than discovering afterward.[4]

## Scarfing methods that shorten the panel, and the "Payson joint" alternative

A published step-by-step scarfing tutorial with photographs is a useful way to demystify the process for a first-time builder.[6] The simplest such methods, however, **shorten the overall panel length**: joining two 8 ft sheets end to end this way will not yield a full 16 ft panel. Two further methods avoid that shortfall. The first butts the two sheets together, then removes a 4 in x 1/4 in layer from the join and inserts a matching 1/4 in butt strap embedded flush with the panel surface. The second — the method preferred over the first — takes off only about 1/8 in with a belt sander and epoxies two 4 in-wide strips of cloth, with enough filler to make the join smooth, over the seam; this is often called a **"Payson joint,"** after Harold "Dynamite" Payson, who used to grind out a couple of plies at the join, fill the resulting hollow with fiberglass mat, then tape over it and grind the whole repair smooth.[7]

## An angle-grinder method, ganging multiple panels for one cut

Lacking a power plane, an angle grinder is a workable substitute for scarfing by hand: clamp together every piece that needs the same scarf — four side-panel pieces and two bottom-panel pieces, in one build's case — and cut the slope through the whole clamped stack in a single pass rather than shaping each piece on its own. A first-time scarfer using this method, having never scarfed anything before, found it "relatively painless."[8]

## "Scarf" vs. "scarph"

Both spellings are used in the archive interchangeably, depending on the builder's preference (British builders tend to favor "scarph"). A Merriam-Webster dictionary entry for "scarf" (noun, 15th century, Scandinavian etymology via Old Norse *skarfr*) confirms that "scarf joint" and "scarf" (verb) are standard, dictionary-sanctioned English alongside "scarph."[9]

## A lap butt joint as a scarf alternative

For lengthening **bottom-panel plywood** specifically, a **lap butt joint** offers a router-cut alternative to a full scarf: a shallow lap is routed into each panel end to roughly half the panel's thickness, and a filler strip of plywood — cut a shade narrower than the routed recess so epoxy fills the gap — bridges the two panels.[10] Because the recess is cut only slightly wider than the filler strip, the joint is largely **self-aligning** if measured out carefully.[11]

Fastening the joint calls for a lighter touch than a chine log: mechanical fasteners should not be relied on to hold the joint under clamping pressure, since squeezing epoxy out of the joint during nailing starves it of glue.[11] Wire nails, or fasteners that can be removed or clenched over once the epoxy sets, are preferable to ring nails, which cannot be backed out; on an 18 mm bottom panel, a lap taken to a little over 9 mm depth with a 9 mm plywood filler piece, fastened with 25 mm bronze ring nails driven just short of passing through, is one workable scantling.[12] A strip of fiberglass tape, about 4 in wide, laid over the joint on the face opposite the 1/4 in filler ply — with that filler-ply face itself already covered by the boat's own bottom glass layup — reinforces the joint enough that, done this way, it can end up **stronger than the surrounding plywood**, with no mechanical fasteners needed at all. A wider strip of tape can also be run over the joint on the inside of the hull for a doubled reinforcement.[11]

This lap butt joint is distinct from the **butt block** discussed above: a butt block adds a backing piece behind the seam, while the lap butt joint recesses and blends the two panel ends into one another before taping over the seam.

## Glue-line thickness and clamping for a lap butt joint

A **25 mm nail** driven through an 18 mm bottom panel and a 9 mm filler piece would pass straight through both — a builder converting between Imperial and metric can end up specifying a nail length as though it were half the actual figure (an easy trap after decades of thinking in inches despite the underlying units having been metric for years).[13] The joint does not need nails at all to be strong: **epoxy alone, reinforced by the outer glass skin, should already be more than adequate**, and the correct approach is a **test joint** to check strength directly, given enough faith that well-executed epoxy is stronger than the surrounding plywood provided the joint is never left starved of glue in any one spot.[14] Where fasteners are used regardless, **screws make better clamps than nails**, since screw pressure can be fine-tuned across the joint and backed off again once the epoxy has cured, whereas stainless nails left in place ("belt and braces") simply add insurance.[14][13]

For the **glue-line thickness** itself, the aim is a line just thick enough that the filler piece nearly floats in the epoxy, flush with the surrounding plywood, with a little squeeze-out at the edges — roughly the consistency of thickened epoxy at "pancake syrup." Most epoxies bridge gaps of about 1/16 in or more once thickened, though some of that will still soak into the plywood itself.[15] A practical routing method is to cut the recess to 9 mm depth while leaving a few shallow ridges proud at that same 9 mm, so the glue line can't be squeezed thinner than intended once weight is applied.[13][15] The two panel faces of the joint need not be identical: the better plan is a **double (taped) joint on the inside** of the hull and a **single, flush seam on the outside**, which — done well — disappears entirely once the boat is finished. While the joint sets, poly sheet or wax paper on both sides keeps it from bonding to whatever it rests on, with enough weight to hold it flat and a few temporary screws to keep the panels aligned. A router leaves the recess smooth enough to work this way, though good dust protection is worth using, since the phenolic glue already present in the plywood is unpleasant to breathe alongside the wood dust itself.[15]

## A dovetail-shaped lap joint after a bottom-panel measuring error

Fitting the bottom panel is itself a common place for a measuring error, since the forward bottom section's lap joint must be cut before the aft panel is offered up for marking, leaving no direct way to check the two against each other until that final step. One builder, having trimmed the forward bottom-panel piece and cut its lap joint, then discovered — only once the last full sheet was lined up against it — a 6 in gap where the two should have met, the result of having shifted the forward piece too far forward earlier while fitting its joint under a bulkhead.[16] The fix did not require starting over: the front joint was reshaped into a **dovetail-shaped lap joint**, and a spare offcut was used to fill most of the resulting shortfall at the rear, with any small remainder there closed as a plain **butt joint** just forward of the transom.[17] This illustrates the same general lesson recorded elsewhere on this page — a router-cut lap or dovetail joint, backed by epoxy and glass tape, tolerates this kind of fitting correction far more forgivingly than a full scarf would.

## Choosing between a scarf, a butt block, and a taped butt joint

Given the total time and money invested in building a Paradox, a plain butt joint should at minimum be backed with a butt block rather than relying on tape and epoxy alone; a fixed jig or method for planing an accurate scarf, beyond skill and a practiced eye, was for a time an open question. It was eventually answered only with further published pointers rather than a physical jig: a browser-based stepped-scarf-joint layout tool, and two further illustrated scarfing guides.[18][19][20] *Gougeon Brothers on Boat Construction* is recommended as the most comprehensive book on epoxy plywood boatbuilding available, with a detailed scarfing description of its own; the individual plywood veneers themselves are a useful guide to how flat and straight a scarf joint is turning out, and a first-time builder need not fear early mistakes — practicing on cheap construction-grade plywood before committing to costlier finished stock (oukoume, for instance) is the practical way to learn.[21]

A dissenting view favors the taped butt joint — the "Payson joint," already described [above](#scarfing-methods-that-shorten-the-panel-and-the-payson-joint-alternative) — over a true scarf outright, holding it not just easier but actually the stronger of the two, and judges a plain butt joint to take real time to execute well without necessarily being the strongest method available either.[22][23] Set against that is firsthand building experience running the other way: scarfing feels, before starting, like "impending doom" waiting to happen with a hand plane on the side panels, but proves in practice much less difficult than feared — once the side panels' scarfs are done, a bottom panel presents no further challenge beyond simply being tough to plane through. Matt Layden's own stated preference, recorded on his Enigma build pages, comes down firmly on the side of the scarf: "After doing these taped butt joints that everybody says are real easy, I confirm my personal belief that glued scarfs are faster, easier, cheaper and bend fairer." The dissenting view conceded only the "bend fairer" point, disputing the rest.[24][25] The exchange closed with lighthearted banter about scarfing indoors, at home, on the good carpet.[26][27][28] The joke ran one further round: one builder confirmed having done his own scarfing and panel-joining at home while his wife was away — side panels in the hallway, the bottom in the lounge, the mast in the kitchen, with carpets protected throughout under plastic sheeting (see [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md#winter-hibernation-a-birdwatcher-temptation-and-naming-her-faith) for the fuller build account) — before a last reply joked about sending a partner off on a cruise instead.[29][30][31]

## A 2009 scarf-length question against the 12-to-1 rule of thumb

A newcomer just starting his own build, having read that a scarf should generally run some **12 times the panel's thickness** (roughly 144 mm, against the plans' own 110 mm), asked whether to lengthen the scarf and the rear side panels to compensate.[32] Two replies converged on leaving the plans as drawn: extending the scarf would cost the two rear side panels their fit on a single sheet of ply, and building to the plans' own 110 mm figure works fine in practice — some builders have even gotten by with a plain butt joint backed by a single piece, without trouble;[33] more generally, a rule like 12-to-1 is best read as a guide for when nothing else is available, not a fixed requirement, since the plans' own dimensions already make a good boat.[34] Satisfied that all three questions had been answered, the newcomer went on to buy more plywood and continue the build.[35]

## A further 2009 question: a hybrid inside-butt/outside-Payson joint, and reusing the trim as a backing block

A month later, the same newcomer — by then with bulkheads made and the mast and rudder under way — proposed joining the already-cut hull panels with a plain butt join on the inside and a Payson-style taped joint on the outside, a hybrid of the two methods described above, and asked whether the roughly 110 mm of extra length this would add would affect the finished boat's sailing performance, or whether the excess should instead be trimmed back off one panel to match the plans' stock length.[36] The answer favored trimming back to stock length rather than sailing an untested, slightly longer boat — and reusing the trimmed-off offcut as the joint's own backing block rather than discarding it.[37]
