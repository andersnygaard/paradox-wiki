# Fastening and gluing

Plywood joints on the [Paradox](/paradox.md) are epoxy-glued and mechanically fastened. There are two documented approaches, plus shared epoxy technique.

## The plans' method — permanent bronze ring nails

Matt Layden's plans (**Sheet 1**) call for **bronze ring nails** left in permanently:

> "Space the nails 40–70 mm according to the ply thickness. Fastenings are intended as a permanent backup to the glue bonds, to limit splitting and delaminating or age."

Per the plans notes, the nails are a **permanent backup to the glue joint**, resisting the working (movement) of the boat in a heavy seaway or from hard crashes. Building with epoxy plus bronze ring nails is long-standing common practice, and builders are best served following the plans to the letter and clearing any deviation with [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) first.[1]

**Matt considers the ring nails structural**, so builders should use them wherever the plans specify. Even though epoxy is an excellent adhesive and the glued parts may not fail *at the glue line*, the **joint can still fail elsewhere — e.g. at the next lamination**. This shows up when tearing up an old glued-and-nailed floor: where there were no ring nails the plywood pries up with a crowbar, but **wherever a nail was present, each nail had to be removed before the plywood would come up**.[2]

**Nail spacing** tracks how hard the ply must be pulled down: ~**70 mm** where the panel is nearly flat, **tighter** (toward 40 mm) on high-curvature areas like the bottom, whichever ply thickness is being fastened at the time.[3]

**Nail sizing** tracks the 40/70 mm range to **nail size**, which in turn tracks lumber size:

- **Big nails** go into **big/thick stuff** and are spaced **further apart (~70 mm)** — they grip strongly. Spacing them too close will **split** the wood.[4]
- **Small nails** go into **little/thin stuff** and must be spaced **closer (~40 mm)** because each nail is weaker, so more of them are needed for strength.[4]

**Assembly tip:** **pre-install the nails in the panel before it is put in place.** This speeds assembly — there is no time for fumbling while the glue is going off.[4]

## Why nails, not stitch-and-glue: a weight trade-off Paradox doesn't need

Nailing is markedly faster than stitch-and-glue's ties-and-fillets approach — banging in a nail beats fiddling with wire stitches and epoxy fillets at every joint. Stitch-and-glue's real advantage is the weight it saves, valuable on a boat where every added pound counts; but Paradox is built to a fixed cruising displacement regardless, made up with lead ballast if the structure itself comes in light, so any weight saved by switching to stitch-and-glue simply has to be bought back as ballast. The nailed method's greater speed is accordingly the more sensible default for Paradox specifically, even though stitch-and-glue is a perfectly workable alternative construction method — see [Jill — construction](/boats/jill.md#construction) for a stitch-and-glue build carried through in full.[5]

## A builder's alternative — removable screws

An alternative approach uses **temporary screws** as clamps, removed after cure, on the reasoning that:

- **No nails while gluing** — hammering drives epoxy out of the joint.[6]
- **No fasteners left in** — metal attracts moisture as it heats and cools with the surrounding wood, becoming a source of rot.[6]
- After removing a screw, the hole is **clear-coated with epoxy using a pipe cleaner**, then **filled with thickened epoxy via a syringe**.[6]

This is "more than one way to skin a cat" rather than a case against following the plans — "it's hard to go wrong following instructions" — and a boat built this way has never broken up in a seaway.[7] See also the removable-screw workflow in [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md).

## Controlling epoxy squeeze-out

- With nails spaced far enough apart, epoxy is **not** squeezed out; heavy clamping with screws or tools *will* squeeze it out.
- Mix the correct amount of **silica thickener (Cab-O-Sil)** into the epoxy to resist squeeze-out under compression.[1]

## Gluing the sheer clamps and chine logs: dry-fit first, then glue

Bending the sheer strakes (sheer clamps) and chine logs into their curved position can take enough time that spreading epoxy **before** the wood is offered up risks the glue going off (curing) while the piece is still only half-seated; gluing only **after** the wood is in place, on the other hand, leaves no obvious way to work the glue into the joint.[8]

Gluing "Zoë"'s sheer clamps and chine logs during the summer months, using a **slow hardener** to extend working time, worked fine done **one piece at a time**, since the glued surface area for each is **under 2 sq ft** — small enough that a slow hardener gives ample time to complete the joint.[9] The method is **dry-fit first, glue second**: fit the wood dry, using **sheet-rock screws** to hold it in a trial assembly so pieces can be installed and removed repeatedly until the fit is correct; only once satisfied, remove the wood, apply the glue, and replace the piece for good.[10] This is the same **temporary-screw, trial-fit-before-final-bonding** workflow used elsewhere in the build (see the "removable screws" section above and [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md)), applied here to the sheer clamps and chine logs specifically — because the piece is only glued once its fit is already proven, there is no risk of the epoxy going off mid-bend.

## Hidden voids in a secondary lamination pose little risk

A hidden **void** (air pocket) can go undetected when laminating an extra layer of plywood onto an existing panel — for example, adding an extra 1/4 in of plywood to a hull side, coating both surfaces with straight epoxy then Cabosil-thickened epoxy, and fastening with a mix of nails into bulkheads/logs and drywall screws elsewhere.[11] Such a void would likely be only a **small pocket of air**, fully surrounded by epoxy and epoxy-saturated plywood, with no path for rot spores to reach it — rot needs food, oxygen, *and* moisture, and the void supplies no moisture route unless the epoxy skin around it is later breached. The pocket is also **too small** for thermal expansion/contraction to cause damage, and too small to let the new lamination flex differently from the rest of the panel.[12]

A practical way to **locate** voids after the fact: watch for spots of **condensation that recur in the same place** on the painted hull's exterior on cold mornings after the boat has sat outside overnight — a telltale sign of a thermal difference under the paint (the same effect can appear over bulkheads or cleats for the same reason). The overall verdict: the less one worries about it, the better.[12]

This is a data point specific to a **secondary/repair lamination** (adding thickness to an existing panel) rather than the original hull-panel lamination sequence documented elsewhere on this page, but the same general reasoning — small, fully encapsulated voids are a cosmetic/diagnostic curiosity rather than a structural or rot risk, so long as the epoxy skin around them stays intact — follows naturally from the squeeze-out and nail-vs-screw material above.

## Armoring small drilled holes against rot

The same Cabosil-thickened epoxy protects small **drilled holes** (such as the boom-end tack-line holes) from rot: straight epoxy first to saturate the end grain, then a Cabosil-thickened coat as an "armor coating." The full method, along with an unresolved practical question about coating narrow, deep holes without closing them up, is documented in [boom-end tack line holes and drilled-hole rot protection](/construction/boom-end-tack-line-holes.md).

## Hardware bonding: epoxy-set fasteners in place of thru-bolting

**Hardware bonding** — setting a fastener into a pre-filled, epoxy-lined hole rather than through-bolting it — is superior to thru-bolting for attaching deck hardware such as pad-eyes and U-bolts; boats have been lifted by hardware-bonded fittings alone.[13] A test procedure lets a builder verify the technique for themselves:

1. Drill a **1/2 in hole, about 1 in deep**, into a piece of 2x stock.
2. Fill the hole with a **high-density epoxy mixture**.
3. Once cured, drill a **3/16 in hole** in the center of the plug.
4. Dip a **1/4 in stainless steel machine fastener** in epoxy and drive it into the hole; let cure.
5. Pull the fastener in tension until it is destroyed or the holding strength is proven.[13]

Consult **WEST System's** own technique literature for the method. Its advantages over thru-bolting: no drilled hole all the way through the hull/deck for water to find, and once cured, **moisture cannot reach the surrounding wood** to start rot — the epoxy plug fully encapsulates the fastener.[13]

The technique's structural logic holds up given a thick enough structure to set the plug in, with one caution: hardware exposed to **direct sunlight**, especially black-colored fittings, could get hot enough to **weaken the epoxy bond** through heat alone. The mitigation is to choose an **epoxy formulation less sensitive to heat** for such an application — West System, once fully cured, is favored for this reason over faster (e.g. 1:1-ratio) epoxy mixes.[14] See [chine runner construction — casting lead ballast in a chine-runner mold](/chine-runners/construction.md#casting-lead-ballast-into-the-runners) for the specific application (bonding cast-lead ballast to the chine log) that prompted this exchange.

## Why the plans show clenched (not simply bent) nails, and how to clench one

The plans show the cabin construction with nails **bent over** — not a temporary clamping step sacrificed once construction is finished, but a permanent, deliberate detail.[15]

The nails as drawn are not simply bent over — they are **clenched**, a distinct and permanent traditional fastening technique long used in **lapstrake boats and canoes**, including canvas-and-cedar canoes built as early as the 1900s.[16][17] Screws, by contrast, hold poorly in plywood over the long term even where the epoxy bond itself stays sound, since **the glue in the plywood's own laminations can give way** before the epoxy joint does — whereas clenched nails, correctly done, are **stronger than screws and easier to execute than rivets**.[16]

Clenching is a specific technique, not just bending a nail flat at 90 degrees: the protruding end is **curved back into the wood** rather than simply folded over, which is what gives a clenched nail its strength as a permanent fastener (see [http://www.faeringdesigninc.com/instruct2.html](http://www.faeringdesigninc.com/instruct2.html) for illustrated instructions).[16] Clenched nails finish **flush on both sides**, so a light fill of epoxy and microballoons fairs the surface without the time-consuming trim work an exposed screw head or nut would need. For a builder who would rather avoid nails and washers regardless, alternatives include flathead machine screws and nuts, rivets and roves, or — if using solid wood rather than plywood — fastening from below with flathead wood screws, or simply trusting a well-made epoxy joint alone, provided the structure is thick enough to set the fastener in. Reference works on the various fastening methods include **"Gougeon Brothers on Boat Construction"** and Robert Steward's **"Boatbuilding Manual."**[17]

Where wood quality or glue-bond longevity is a concern — a lesser grade of plywood, or finishing with ordinary paint rather than a full epoxy seal over all wood surfaces — clenched nails' role as a mechanical backup to the glue bond matters more, not less, since nails moving slightly over time can break an epoxy seal and let water in if the surrounding wood is not otherwise protected.[18] This is the same "permanent backup to the glue bond" logic documented above for the plans' bronze ring nails generally, applied here specifically to the clench-nailed cabin construction.

## Bronze ring nails bending over: pilot-hole sizing

Sheer strakes need not match the plans' nominal dimension exactly: fitting them at **20 mm rather than 25 mm** is workable, and can simply be a matter of getting both strakes out of an available sapele offcut left over from cutting the chine logs.[19]

Bronze ring nails driven into sheer strakes (or other solid-wood members already bedded on epoxy) can bend over in their last third of length rather than driving home cleanly, especially where the nail's point reaches the glue line itself — even into a pre-drilled pilot hole.[19] This is a **pilot-hole sizing problem**, not a flaw in the fastener: enlarging the pilot hole, even to what feels like a generously large diameter, generally cures the bending without weakening the nail's hold — even a noticeably larger pilot hole still leaves the nail effectively impossible to withdraw again once driven, evidence the fastener still bites hard despite the larger hole. Test pieces are worth cutting first to dial in a working pilot-hole size before committing to the actual sheer strakes or chine logs.[20]

A workable rule of thumb, arrived at from a UK chandlery's own published nail/pilot-hole size tables: take the tabulated pilot-hole diameter and add about **1/64 in**. Fasteners sized this way go in cleanly through both the plywood and into solid sapele beneath it, with no further bending.[21]

## Bronze ring nail cost, and nails versus wood screws

A 2008 UK retail data point for the plans' bronze ring nails: about **£2.75 per 100 g**, pricing the plans' full recommended quantity at roughly **£137.50** — steep, but judged reasonable given generally high metal prices at the time and bronze's standing as one of the most historically expensive metals used in construction.[22] Why the plans specify barbed (ring) nails rather than bronze wood screws as the permanent fastener was raised as an open question, distinct from the removable-screw clamping technique documented above[23] — later restated specifically as a question of **mechanical merit rather than corrosion resistance**: a screw can be withdrawn again without damaging the surrounding wood, where pulling out a barbed nail risks exactly that.[24] Screws are, on balance, judged the mechanically superior fastener: a ring-shank nail's only real advantage is its lower cost, and brass fasteners specifically are weaker than bronze and prone to **de-zincify in saltwater** over time — workable only where a boat will see fresh water exclusively.[25]

A different reply to the same mechanical-merits question judged the distinction largely academic in practice: since every joint is already well glued (and, on a finished hull, sheathed in glass cloth that would itself have to come off first), removing screws causes just as much collateral damage as pulling a barbed nail — with the added step, for a ring nail specifically, of grinding off the head, separating the joint, and cutting the nail off flush if it must come out at all. On balance, nails were judged **cheaper, quicker, and more satisfying to drive home**.[26] The thread closed on a lighter note from a further correspondent who does "not do DIY" at all — owning some five hammers of assorted sizes yet never able to find one on the rare occasion a nail actually needs driving.[27]

## Actual bronze-nail quantities used, well under the plans' recommended amount

The plans' own recommended bronze-nail quantities have, in practice, been found to run well over what a finished Paradox actually needs. Surplus stock bought cheaply from a fellow builder — sold off because his own completed boat had used markedly less than the plans called for — supplied one builder's full nail requirement at the following quantities, judged a substantial saving against the plans' quoted amount: **25 mm #14 = 250 g; 32 mm #12 = 500 g; 38 mm #10 = 500 g; 50 mm #10 = 750 g; 65 mm #8 = 250 g**. The cheapest UK retail source found for bronze annular ring nails otherwise was Robbins of Bristol (`www.robbins.co.uk/mar_fixings.htm`).[28] One further personal preference recorded on the same UK hardware-sourcing thread: hammering a nail home is, for some builders, simply more satisfying than driving a screw — an admission even from a builder who otherwise recommends the economical bronze-below/brass-above-the-waterline split documented above.[29]

## Ply thickness and fastener choice for topside panels

Matt Layden's plans call for 1/2 in plywood on the topside panels because exterior-grade plywood — weaker than marine ply — needs the extra thickness; where marine ply is used instead, 3/8 in is an adequate substitute. For fastening those panels to the gunwale, brass screws need not be a second thought: the epoxy bond is what actually holds the joint, with the screws (like the plans' bronze ring nails elsewhere) serving only as backup. A thickened-epoxy filler coat doubles as a sealer, and the whole panel is in any case covered in glass cloth. A 2008 UK retail data point: good-quality brass screws bought from Screwfix cost less than half the price of the same screws bought locally, which also stocks a stainless steel option.[30]

## Fastener material by era and application: bronze, galvanized, and copper

Fastener-material choice has tracked how a boat is actually kept rather than tradition for its own sake. In the pre-fiberglass era, when a hull's wood stayed damp from sitting in the water year-round, bronze earned its cost as the most corrosion-resistant option available. Once trailerable boats — wetted only occasionally — became common, hot-dip galvanized screws and nails proved the more practical, economical choice for that use; with epoxy now able to seal a metal fastener away from moisture entirely, galvanized steel holds up well and is no longer a false economy even over the long run. Bronze remains the right choice for an antique-style boat, or one kept afloat year-round. Ring-shank (annular) nails are cheaper than screws generally: a hot-dip-galvanized nail's own rough, zinc-roughened surface grips the wood fibers so well that, once driven, it is next to impossible to remove — a plain bronze nail, being comparatively slippery, needs the same annular grooves rolled onto it during manufacture to match that grip. The great majority of ordinary working boats have always used galvanized or plain-steel fastenings rather than bronze; bronze's reputation as the standard boatbuilding fastener owes more to yachting literature's romance with it than to how most boats were actually built, and true wrought iron is no longer made at all. A rule of thumb sums up the choice: "nail where you can, screw where you should, and bolt when you must."[31]

## Sourcing fasteners without big-box markup

A 2010 US data point on ordinary wood-screw pricing: brass and stainless-steel screws were found priced sharply higher than expected at a national home-improvement chain, prompting a search for a cheaper source and a question about whether phosphate-coated deck screws might serve instead.[46] Brass, bronze, and 316 stainless steel run expensive everywhere, but two mail-order suppliers undercut both the big chains and an out-of-the-way local hardware store: **Duckworks** sells hardware in the small quantities a home builder actually needs, avoiding the waste of buying a large lot of something only a dozen pieces are needed of, at prices judged reasonable against other suppliers; **Jamestown Distributors** runs a bit cheaper than West Marine, with a paper catalog worth requesting for its mail-order forms, though it carries only about half of the same supplier's full online selection.[47]

## Fastening laminated ply panels: a delamination caution from experience

A laminated plywood panel can delaminate within the plywood itself, independent of how sound its epoxy joints are — a case for driving nails or screws through any laminated ply panel rather than trusting its own internal glue lines alone to hold it together over time. [Faith](/boats/faith.md#a-laminated-hatch-beam-that-delaminated-in-the-ply-itself)'s forward hatch beam, laminated from plywood rather than solid wood, delaminated in service; on inspection, it was the plywood's own lamination that had failed, not the epoxy bonding it to the hatch structure. See [deck and cabin beam lamination](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md) for the general hatch- and cabin-beam lamination technique this qualifies.[32] The same caution was repeated, months later, to a newcomer just starting his own beam-laminating jigs — probably not a problem for a beam bonded directly to a bulkhead, but a real risk for a free-standing one. See [boats/wizard-spawn.md](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#starting-on-the-beams-jigs-sizing-confusion-and-a-lesson-in-plywood).[33]

## Copper nails for the forward cabin's ply doublers

Copper nails have one specific, limited use on the Paradox: about a dozen are needed where plywood doublers, fixed to the deck, form the bottom of the forward end of the cabin and take the bottom edge of the front window. Wherever there is no structure below the deck at that point, the copper nails are simply riveted through.[34] Secondhand copper nails — a potful from a car-boot sale, say — work as well as new ones for this; Nelson's own ships were built with copper rivets, a long pedigree for the material in wooden boatbuilding.[35]

## A below/above-waterline fastening split, and a 28-year steel-screw data point

Fastening a laminated ply panel need not use bronze throughout: one way to economize while still using bronze where corrosion risk is greatest fits bronze fastenings only below the waterline and brass screws everywhere above it. A further 2008 UK retail data point: brass screws ran about £6.26 per 200, stainless steel A2 screws about £5.36 per 200 — cheaper, though without the same practice record.[36] NOLI's own laminated parts — the coachroof's forward section, the windscreen's front (each three layers), and the hatch top (two layers) — were fastened this way throughout, with brass screws and glue rather than bronze ring nails; see [boats/noli.md](/boats/noli.md#laminated-parts-and-a-belowabove-waterline-fastening-idea) for the fuller detail.[36]

A production-boat data point argues elaborate fastenings are not strictly necessary at all: a 21 ft boat, built to the same specification as its production sisters, used ordinary steel-plated screws — the only concession was painting the screw heads with polyester resin before fitting the rubbing strip — and has held up under year-round fishing use, hauled out only about two weeks a year for cleaning and bottom paint, for some 28 years and counting.[36]

## A resorcinol alternative to epoxy

The plans list resorcinol glue as an acceptable alternative to epoxy for wood-to-wood bonding, alongside Douglas fir, cypress, luan, mahogany, and spruce as acceptable solid-wood species (see [Building the Paradox series](/references/building-paradox-series.md)). One builder ordered a gallon of resorcinol from a specialist adhesives supplier to glue the hull-bottom scarph joint on the 3/4 in (18 mm) panels, intending — if it went well — to use resorcinol for most of his own wood-to-wood bonding in place of epoxy.[40]

Interest in an alternative to epoxy is itself grounded in practical experience with epoxy's own limits: easy to use and generally reliable for gluing, but carrying a high price tag, and its strength when used for glassing (rather than plain wood-to-wood bonding) is less assured — a glassed surface can peel more readily than expected.[41]

A first trial of resorcinol on the hull-bottom scarph joint surfaced a real clamping-pressure mismatch with epoxy practice: the manufacturer's recommended clamping pressure, some 25-75 lb per sq in, is achievable on a small glued area but scales to several thousand pounds of force over a joint of any size — for a joint just over 300 sq in, roughly 7,500 lb — so the pressure actually achievable in practice (stacked water-filled containers, working out to around 1 lb per sq in) fell far short of the figure, prompting an extra-cautious full six-day cure with the joint left completely undisturbed throughout. Resorcinol also proved markedly more heat-dependent than epoxy: a batch mixed cold, straight from refrigerated storage, produced none of the exothermic heat ("flash") epoxy is known for, leaving it unclear whether the cure itself was affected, while the ambient heat of the cure period (highs near 100°F/38°C) was treated as a help rather than a hindrance, on the understanding that resorcinol favors heat and pressure. Working properties differ from epoxy too: resorcinol goes on as a thin liquid before thickening to a tacky consistency, needs comparatively little volume to cover a given area, and wipes away cleanly with a damp cloth before it sets, while its powdered hardener component looks and smells like fine sawdust rather than the liquids used with epoxy hardeners. On strength, resorcinol was judged, provisionally and pending a planned boil-and-strength test on scrap material, a promising candidate for structural wood-to-wood joints, but not a substitute for epoxy where fiberglass is being bonded, since resorcinol is not used to bond fiberglass at all; for non-structural wood joints, a different, non-resorcinol adhesive was the preferred fallback instead.[42]

A further reply floated a workaround for the clamping-pressure mismatch — temporary bolts through the joint, with the resulting holes filled with epoxy once the glue has cured — and noted that a build paced only on weekends already leaves natural multi-day gaps between glue sessions, so a demanding cure time is less of an imposition than it might first appear. The same reply revisited the glassing-peels-too-easily impression above with a firsthand data point: after some breakages, and while removing a piece of fiberglass from a hull bottom, a started sheet of glass came away more readily than expected — while still judging epoxy the strongest resin available for glassing, and putting the earlier impression down to his own expectations rather than any flaw in the epoxy itself.[43]

A follow-up dropped the planned boil-and-strength test: the cured hull-bottom scarph joint was judged strong enough on its own that the test no longer seemed necessary; further, smaller 1/2 in side-panel scarphs went ahead with the same resorcinol in the meantime.[44]

A further, independently run comparison arrived that December, testing resorcinol directly against epoxy on a scarf joint under controlled conditions: a 3 m length of clear, straight, seasoned Douglas fir was ripped lengthwise into two identical 70 x 60 mm pieces, each given an 8:1 diagonal scarf cut, then bonded — one with resorcinol, the other with unthickened epoxy — at the manufacturers' stated 14-15°C minimum application temperature, curing over 7 days as the ambient temperature fell to 10°C. Loaded afterward with lead weight across the joint area, the epoxied piece failed at about 65 kg, the wood fiber giving way beyond the glue line rather than the joint itself — a typical epoxy failure mode; the resorcinol piece cracked audibly at about 75 kg but did not fail outright, and testing was abandoned around 85 kg for lack of a way to weight it further accurately. The conclusion drawn: resorcinol stays more flexible than epoxy under stress at low temperature, spreading load more evenly into the surrounding wood, at the cost of needing considerably more clamping pressure to cure well. The same builder went on to glue all of his own spars and the yuloh with resorcinol, finding it no harder to apply or clean up — soluble in water while uncured — than epoxy.[45] A follow-up gave the correspondent's own build location for the first time — France's Tarn-et-Garonne department, the source of the "82" in the rkg_82 handle.[48]

Continuing the same comparison from his own build, the builder who first proposed the resorcinol trial confirmed both adhesives appear plenty strong; his own region's hot summers, plus plans for future trips to still hotter climates, made resorcinol's greater heat tolerance the more useful property going forward, and he remained pleased with the results overall — an initial joint, less tightly fitted from imperfect scarf technique, cracked audibly for a time after gluing before the noise stopped as the loose fragments settled, and none of the glued boards can be pried apart again.[49]

## Fasteners and adhesives in the Bolduc rebuilds (microcruising.com)

The [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) and [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) rebuilds corroborate the "ring nails are structural" argument above with direct practice: during Swamp Thing's rot repair, **bronze ring nails were added to back up the old ones** — "All rotted wood is removed, and bronze ring nails are added to back up the old ones."[37] Later, Swamp Thing's entirely new bottom panel was fastened the same way: **bronze ring nails** driven through **pre-drilled holes**, with the panel bedded on **epoxy thickened with cabosil**.[38] See [hull planking](/construction/hull-planking.md) for the fuller bottom-panel sequence this confirms.

A different adhesive pairing appears on the [folding dinghy](/construction/folding-dinghy.md): its canvas hinges are bonded with **3M 5200** and tacked with **¼" stainless steel staples**, rather than nails or screws — a fastening choice suited to a flexible cloth hinge rather than a rigid plywood joint.[39]

[31] [Paradox builders mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md) message 5199 (yahns / "Stephen Yahn", `paradoxbuilders`, 2008-06-21) — "Pricy bronze.": full text and citation also at [people/stephen-yahn.md](/people/stephen-yahn.md); sets out fastener-material choice as tracking how a boat is kept, not tradition — bronze earning its cost in the pre-fiberglass era when hulls stayed damp year-round, hot-dip galvanized the more practical choice once trailerable boats spent most of their time dry, and, with epoxy now sealing metal fasteners from moisture, galvanized steel no longer a false economy even long-term; bronze still right for an antique-style boat or one kept afloat year-round; ring-shank nails cheaper than screws, a galvanized nail's rough zinc surface gripping wood fibers so well it is nearly impossible to remove once driven, a bronze nail needing the same annular grooves rolled on to match that grip since it is otherwise comparatively slippery; judges bronze's boatbuilding reputation owed more to yachting literature's romance with it than to how most boats were actually built, and that true wrought iron is no longer made.
