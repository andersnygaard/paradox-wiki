# Lower rudder fitting — bearing material options

The Paradox's **lower rudder fitting** (a gudgeon-type bearing) can be made either from bronze or machined from plastic stock; this page covers the plastic-material tradeoffs and, separately, sourced bronze suppliers for the tack, sail, and yuloh fittings. It follows on from the bulkhead-#3 damp-plywood finding covered in [checking a used Paradox for rot](/construction/rot-inspection-ice-pick.md).

## Material choice: UHMW over nylon, Delrin, or Tufnol

Four plastics are the usual candidates for machining a lower rudder fitting. CNC access makes the machining itself cheap or free for a builder with the right shop connections — the setup cost is paid once, so running three or four parts from the same program costs little more than running one; a "high-tech" CNC shop can hold tolerances around **.00005 in** and machine nylon stock up to **6 in thick**, closely to a copy of Matt Layden's print.[1][2]

**Don't use nylon** — it will likely **elongate under stress**. **UHMW** (Ultra High Molecular Weight polyethylene) is the recommended material instead: readily available and a plastic that "will work fine."[3] Ranking the alternatives directly: **Delrin is better than nylon, but still not as good as UHMW**, and **Tufnol** — a cloth-and-resin composite laminate, obtainable in the US though regarded there as an import — is **very expensive**.[4]

Tufnol is well known in the UK for industrial models: easy to machine, dimensionally stable, and resistant to most conditions,[5] though suitable plastic offcuts run expensive there too (an offcuts bin is usually the way to find them cheaply).[6] As a secondhand data point on Delrin, a published cruising/boatbuilding account describes a gudgeon bearing surface/liner made out of **Delrin**.[7]

Nylon's long-term suitability in this application remains an open question in principle — whether it matches glassed epoxy for strength, and how it holds up to salt water, marine organisms, freezing, and UV exposure over a winter outdoors — but is answered in practice by the UHMW recommendation, which sidesteps the issue entirely.[8]

**UHMW** stands as the clear, repeated recommendation over nylon, Delrin, and Tufnol alike for the lower rudder fitting.

At least one build used **Tufnol combined with stainless steel** for the rudder fittings generally (both bearing and metal hardware) as well as for the boom axle, reporting "no problems so far" — a firsthand data point for Tufnol's practical durability in this application.[9]

## Casting a glass lower rudder gudgeon: technique

Separately from the plastic-vs-bronze bearing-material question, the lower rudder fitting can also be built as a **glassed epoxy casting** directly around a print of the rudder gudgeon shape, rather than machined from plastic or bronze stock. The plans call for **6 layers of 24 oz roving**, working out to between **1/4 and 1/2 in** of laid-up roving — a thickness that can strike a first-time builder as excessive for the part, but is correct: it produces "a very heavy casting" by design.[10][11]

Getting the heavy roving to conform to the mold is the real difficulty — a first layer laid up square tends not to bend or lie down cleanly.[12] The fix is to **cut the roving cloth on the bias (45 degrees)** rather than square, which lets it drape and lie down far more easily. The casting method: build a **mold of the gudgeon's inside shape**, lay up the glass roving in it on the bias, fill the finished casting with a **high-density epoxy filler mixture**, and then **bore the bolt hole** on a drill press once the casting has cured. A published article on the technique lays the roving up over a **dowel rod** instead, an alternative worth checking if a builder has a copy of it.[13]

This casting technique has an independent real-world counterpart: on "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," the lower rudder fitting was built from a **block of Tufnol** rather than a glass casting — confirming Tufnol as more than a paper candidate for the part. The same boat also carries an **LED masthead light** (see [running lights](/operations/running-lights.md)), a detail worth a layman's write-up in its own right.[14]

A **bronze-cast lower gudgeon**, in place of the plans' glass-filled casting, is a workable substitute for any builder with the means to cast bronze — endorsed as worth attempting outright wherever that capability exists, with no reason on record why a bronze casting couldn't or shouldn't be used instead of the glass-fibre version.[15][16][17]

A full molding technique exists for the glass-cast version, using cheap, improvised materials rather than a purpose-built mold: shape the **bendy curved part** of the mold from the sides of a plastic ice-cream carton, and the **base and straight side** from wood covered in plastic bin liner, sealing the joints between the pieces with ordinary cellotape. The casting runs **very hot** while curing. Once cured, line the curved side with **5 layers of GRP in a tape layup**, then drill the bolt holes for the transom attachment **in situ**, directly against the transom, to guarantee they line up correctly. A bronze pintle rotating within a fitting built this way turns very smoothly; whether that fit **tightens or loosens once immersed in water** is an open question — swelling could tighten it, or water could instead act as a lubricant and loosen it — that can only really be answered by time in use.[18]

Weighing bronze against the plans' glass/epoxy casting more generally, the choice comes down to cost and available tooling rather than any flaw in either material: **wood and epoxy works and is cost-effective**, and represents a considered compromise rather than a lesser option; bronze or stainless steel is worth pursuing instead wherever a builder's skill and budget allow, since Paradox's own design brief favored common, affordable materials and tools over costly ones. A steel gudgeon, welded up and then epoxy-coated after abrading, is a workable alternative to either.[19]

A second, independent gudgeon-casting account uses a different way of forming the pintle hole: rather than drilling it into the cured casting afterward, a length of copper tube wrapped in sticky tape is set into the mould before pouring, to be withdrawn once the epoxy — poured with plenty of chopped strand matting — has cured. The cure can run hot and fast enough from the exothermic reaction to catch a builder by surprise; freeing the tube afterward is a matter of gripping it firmly and tapping the casting sharply, or, failing that, heating the tube to soften the surrounding epoxy enough to let it slide free.[20]

A related lamination approach was used to build the fitting itself on a composite-hulled Paradox: a thick fiberglass-strip build-up in a simple mold, finished with a Delrin bushing for wear. See [the "BRIS Paradox" — a rudder-fitting rework, and a fiberglass tiller](/boats/bris-paradox.md#a-rudder-fitting-rework-and-a-fiberglass-tiller).

A rule of thumb for sizing a cast gudgeon's own bushing, offered to a builder 3D-printing one for accuracy: keep the distance from the bushing to the gudgeon's own edge under half the width of the rudder stock at any position through its travel, and expect the rudder to need roughly 35 degrees of swing to each side.[21]

## A 2016 recurrence: whether the lower gudgeon needs a metal liner

A builder fitting his own rudder, years after the casting techniques above were last discussed, asked a related but distinct question: not how to cast the lower gudgeon, but whether the finished casting needs a metal sleeve at all — a short length of flared pipe fitted so the rudder-cheek pintle would not chew into bare epoxy as it rotated inside it.[22] Told in reply that a lower gudgeon left completely unlined since 2008 — plain epoxy, no metal sleeve of any kind — had shown no noticeable wear under hard use, decided against lining his own; reasoned that correct alignment alone is enough for the halyard hole and the mast-vent-box sheeting-off holes as well, leaving only the furling-line hole and the transom sheet-entry point as places genuinely needing a flared sleeve.[23][24] See [boats/mini.md — ballast and trim](/boats/mini.md#ballast-and-trim) for the fuller exchange, including the same builder's water-ballast plumbing and battery questions raised in the same thread, and [furling — a tack-rope chafe point where it crosses the deck](/components/rig/furling.md#a-tack-rope-chafe-point-where-it-crosses-the-deck) for the matching furling-line sleeving technique.

## A 2019 recurrence: gluing generally, and a chopped-strand/cloth gudgeon build

A further builder asked, separately, whether epoxy mixed with cotton flox is the right mix for gluing rudder plywood, and whether to follow Don Elliott's own build-manual recipe for the gudgeon — epoxy, cotton flox, and chopped-strand glass fibres worked into a putty.[25] The reply offered a different, proven approach rather than confirming Don Elliott's own: **West System epoxy with its own microbead filler** for all general gluing, and, for the gudgeon specifically, **chopped strand mixed with epoxy alone (no filler) for the core**, wrapped in **about four layers of 200 gsm cloth** around the outside — built this way, "that gudgeon is so strong, it leaves any metal gudgeon for dead."[26]

Three weeks later the same builder's own attempt at a gudgeon casting ran into the exotherm trouble already documented above: mixing about **250 ml** of epoxy thickened with micro glass fibres and chopped glass matting in a single container, the mix heated up and bubbled its way out of the mold before it cured, at a moderate **21°C** outside temperature — a further, firsthand case for keeping such a thickened batch small rather than mixing a gudgeon's whole fill at once, echoing the earlier account above of a gudgeon casting that "ran very hot" curing.[27]

## Controlling exotherm in a small epoxy casting (April 2019)

The bubbled-out casting above drew a cluster of replies converging on the same underlying cause. Epoxy's cure is an exothermic reaction: it sets slower and cooler spread thin in an open tray, but heats up fast in a bulk, well-insulated pour — hot enough in a metal tin to reach melting point for plastic and to burn bare skin — and a fast-set hardener runs hotter and faster still, compounding the same risk.[28][29] Glass **matting** is itself part of the problem in an epoxy casting: it is held together at the factory with a binder meant to dissolve in polyester or vinylester resin, which does not happen with epoxy, so the mat never releases the way it is designed to — woven cloth or chopped-strand roving, not matting, is the correct glass reinforcement for an epoxy casting.[30]

Two fixes followed, both aimed at giving the heat somewhere to go rather than letting it build up in one mass: mix a smaller batch — closer to 100 ml than the 250 ml that failed — in a shallow, open tray, or in a mold of bent tin screwed to a plywood backing, either of which lets the heat escape as it cures;[31] or, alternatively, pour the epoxy in small stages, letting each pour pass its own peak heat before adding the next small patch, building the casting up incrementally rather than filling the mold at once.[32]

The next attempt, mixed smaller and poured incrementally in a flatter tray, went well; a further question then followed about the top pintle's diameter and matching Wicox part numbers.[33][34] The commercial part's own diameter was never confirmed, but a proven alternative was already on record: a top pintle made from Tufnol blocks and an M8 stainless-steel bolt, the same material already used for other rudder hardware (see [material choice](#material-choice-uhmw-over-nylon-delrin-or-tufnol) above), trouble-free after sixteen years.[35] Tufnol itself was clarified as an engineering composite material, not a form of epoxy, believed to use a polyimide resin.[36] A further, independent account of the same gudgeon-casting technique exists as a builder's own contemporaneous blog post.[37]

## A May 2019 recurrence: fastening the finished gudgeon, not casting it

A further exchange, weeks after the casting question above, turned from how to cast the lower gudgeon to how to fasten the finished part to the transom: whether to follow Don Elliott's own build guide, which recommends a sealant with adhesive qualities used together with bolts, or to epoxy the gudgeon directly to the transom instead.[38] The reply weighing the two favoured the sealant-and-bolts route: epoxy is not the preferred method, since it makes the fitting difficult to remove again later, where a sealant-adhesive combined with bolts already holds sufficiently — corroborated by Don Elliott's own boat, "The Edge," whose gudgeon was never epoxied in the first place.[39] Practice on record diverges from that recommendation, though: one builder reported having epoxied his own gudgeon in place with no problem;[40] a second, arguing from the loads a grounding under helm can put through the rudder, held that only bolts should be trusted at the lower gudgeon — enough force can come through a hard-over helm striking bottom to break a tiller or a rudder blade, and a tiller is the easier of the two to improvise a replacement for underway, so it should be the part that gives first.[41] Weighing that safety case against his own stated practice, the first builder settled on both together — epoxying the gudgeon in place and backing it with bolts.[42] No single method has settled as the standard: a gudgeon has been built with epoxy alone, with sealant and bolts alone, and with epoxy and bolts combined, each on record with a builder content with the result. A builder working through the same question on his own boat confirmed he had never meant to drop the bolts at all — sealant for the bolts, plus epoxy bonding the gudgeon itself to the transom, on the reasoning that a bonded gudgeon could still be cut free later if it ever needed replacing.[43]

## Bronze sourcing for the tack, sail, and yuloh fittings

Separately from the bearing-material question, bronze stock for the boat's other metal hardware has been sourced and priced, with surplus made available to other builders.

**1/4 in schedule-40 brass pipe (actually bronze)**, used for the **tack fitting** and the **sail and yuloh fittings**, is available from **Quality Pipe Products**, 13201 Hancock Dr., Taylor, MI 48180, ph. 734-374-5100. Two 4 ft sections cost **$23.65** including COD shipping (priced January 2001; the supplier did not accept Visa at that time). Order the fittings **about 3 in longer** than plans-length and trim to fit afterward — built exactly to print, they come out too short. The extra length in a 4 ft section also gives enough leverage to **bend the pipe in a 1/2 in conduit bender** for the sail and yuloh fittings; the **forward yuloh fitting** additionally needs **heat** to bend around a piece of steel pipe. The material is "incredibly strong."[44]

**Bronze rod and bar stock**, used for the **lighting switch plate**, is available from **Atlas Metal Sales**, 1401 Umatilla St., Denver, CO 82204-2432, ph. 800-662-0143, with a **6 ft minimum of each** required — a full order runs **$79.86**, though a single boat needs only 8 in of bar and 18 in of round, leaving **64 in of bar and 54 in of round** surplus (enough for roughly 8 more boats' worth of bar stock and 3 more boats' worth of round).[44]

That surplus bar/round stock has circulated among other builders at cost plus shipping (**$4.53** for the 8 in of bar and **$9.77** for the 18 in of round needed per boat) — a **buyers' co-op** splitting a bulk order is the efficient way to avoid each builder separately paying a 6 ft minimum for a few inches of material.[44] In practice this played out informally: three builders, including one redoing all the metal parts on [Whisper](/boats/whisper.md),[45] bought into a stock of surplus bronze pipe at a firm price of **$14.30** plus tube and postage each, cut and shipped in a mailing tube against payment by check on receipt.[46] All of the advertised surplus bronze rod stock was spoken for within about a week of the offer, with receipt confirmed within days of shipping.[47]

The same bending question recurred in August 2008 from a builder nearly finished with his own roller-furling system, asking directly whether bronze rod needs heating to bend — left unanswered on record, though the established technique above (cold-bending in a 1/2 in conduit bender, with heat needed only for the tighter forward yuloh fitting) already answers it.[48]

## Bottom roving and epoxy cost

Refitting a Paradox's bottom with **roving** is weather-dependent work best done in suitable conditions. On epoxy cost: a 3-gallon purchase of **West System** runs high enough to be called "too expensive,"[49] against a **Raka 3-gallon kit** (including both slow and fast hardener) at about **$118**, roughly **1/3 of West System's cost** for an equivalent quantity[50] — the same Raka/West price gap that shows up in the Kevlar-vs-fiberglass cost comparison (see [epoxy and fiberglass materials sourcing](/references/materials-sourcing-links.md)).
