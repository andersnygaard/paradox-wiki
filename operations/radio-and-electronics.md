# Radio and electronics

Communications and weather-data equipment aboard a [Paradox](/paradox.md) range from portable ham-radio antennas to simply relying on a cell phone or shortwave receiver coupled to a laptop — with the boat's own mast offering an unusually convenient, if untested, option for a permanent HF installation.

## A permanent HF antenna using the mast's lightning-protection wire

Portable ham antennas — an **Outbacker**, later a **Buddipole** (`buddipole.com`) — have served aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), both stowed when not needed and requiring the antenna to be lowered underway.[1][2]

A more permanent alternative follows directly from the Paradox's [mast lightning-protection wiring](/boats/whisper.md): since the mast already carries a conductor down its length for lightning protection, with a grounding **counterpoise** already in place, that same wire could in principle serve double duty as the **antenna** for a multiband HF radio fitted with an **automatic antenna tuner** — disabled for lightning protection and switched back on for radio use as needed.[1] This mirrors a technique some competitive sailors use aboard carbon masts, running a wire laminated into the spar for the same purpose; there is no evident reason it would not work equally well in a wood or wood/composite mast. A **longwire antenna run up a Technora single-braid backstay** aboard an earlier boat gave good results at minimal cost, using the same principle.[3]

An automatic tuner's main practical advantage is that it can find an efficient match for a **random-length antenna** automatically, which matters less for casual, scheduled-net use but becomes more important for **Pactor** (radio e-mail), where fast, reliable tuning is a real convenience.[3] A properly sealed auto tuner mounted high on the forward face of bulkhead #2 could also serve as a potential source of additional reserve buoyancy.[1]

Weighed against a **portable** antenna (an Outbacker- or Buddipole-style unit, better suited to occasional or mountaintop use), a **permanent longwire-and-auto-tuner** setup has the advantage of needing nothing to stow, aim, or clean, and being ready at a moment's notice — though it commits the boat's lightning-protection system to double duty and requires a manual switch-over between the two functions.[3]

## Cell phone and shortwave alternatives to a full ham rig

Despite the mast's ready-made antenna option, a full HF ham radio installation may not be necessary for weather data and basic communications: a **cell phone coupled to a laptop** is an increasingly viable substitute for traditional ham-radio weather routing, at least for coastal U.S. sailing, and an **all-band shortwave receiver** coupled to a laptop is a simpler, receive-only alternative for pulling down weather faxes/maps without needing a full transmitting ham station at all.[3]

## Portable-antenna experience: Outbacker vs. Buddipole in practice

Season-over-season cruising of [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) extensively in the Bahamas yielded a firsthand comparison of the two portable antennas: the original **Outbacker** vertical antenna worked "okay" and proved durable, but a later switch to a less durable, **home-made $35 "Buddipole"** (`qsl.net/w3ff`) produced markedly better transmissions. Lacking a tall mast, a long-wire backstay antenna was not an option for that boat. Underway, a regular morning check-in to the **Waterway Radio and Cruising Club** net on 7.268 MHz (`jstorm.com/wrcc`) covered float plans, position reports, general traffic, and the NOAA forecast; a trial of **Winlink 200/Airmail** (`winlink.org/airmail.htm`) — free HF e-mail and weatherfax, using a computer and modem — aboard a friend's boat proved "pretty neat."[4]

## SailMail vs. Winlink/Airmail, and the cost/legality tradeoffs of ham gear

Radio e-mail over HF — SailMail's paid-subscription Pactor I/II/III service versus Winlink/Airmail's free but non-commercial-only equivalent over ham radio, each with its own TNC-modem cost and baud-rate tradeoffs — was a period-specific, now-obsolete solution; cellular and satellite data have superseded it.[5] Weatherfax reception remains comparatively cheap and simple: a demodulator or TNC (or even a laptop sound card with freeware) plugged into a cheap shortwave receiver's headphone jack is enough to pull down weatherfax without needing a full transmitting station.[6]

## A wind generator and radar: a scale-mockup exercise, and how little power a Paradox actually needs

A small wind generator and radar unit can plausibly be fitted to a Paradox at all, as a **scale mockup** demonstrated: Southwest Windpower's **Air-X Marine** wind generator (13 lb) paired with JRC's **Radar 1000 Mk II** (under 9 lb) — both compact and light enough, on paper, to imagine mounted on so small a boat.[7] The mockup was refined once after its initial posting: the wind generator and radar outlines were re-scaled to within about **3 mm** of actual size, correcting an earlier version that had rendered both units slightly too large, and the wind generator's **"sphere of operation" / keep-clear zone** marking was corrected to its proper size as well.[8]

How little power a Paradox actually needs is illustrated by a boat sailed without either accessory: a single **12V wet NiCad battery**, salvaged in 1977 from a coal-industry routine-maintenance replacement, charged once each spring runs a GPS, echo sounder, running lights, and water-ballast/bilge pump for an entire season with no further attention. A radar installation specifically would change that power calculus enough to make a wind generator's added generating capacity worth having.[9]

Mounting a roughly 9 lb wind-generator unit at the masthead raises a stability question distinct from simply confirming the units fit: whether the added weight aloft calls for any righting-moment correction.[10] A masthead radar's added weight could plausibly be offset by building the mast itself from a lighter material (Sitka spruce or even carbon fiber) and omitting the lightning rod, though a proper calculation would still be needed to confirm the trade balances out.[11]

The record does contain a genuine mast-weight-and-roll data point, traced back to a January 2004 mast-construction thread: a heavier carbon-fiber/wood composite mast was said to **"provide a more comfortable [roll] motion,"** against which the reply was that such a composite mast would run **"about half the weight and triple the strength"** of a comparable wood mast — a lighter, stronger mast rather than a heavier one added for roll comfort.[12][13] This is not a report of anyone deliberately weighting a mast to damp rolling, only of a heavier composite option being credited with a side benefit of smoother motion, set against the case for building light and strong instead.[13] Reading it this way resolves what had for a time seemed to be an unrecovered separate report: **keeping weight aloft down is considered "always a good thing"** for a Paradox, consistent with the light-composite-mast side of that exchange and with no owner reporting rolling problems severe enough to want to correct with mast ballast.[14]

Any radar fit to so low a boat needs a proper **radar reflector** alongside it — being seen matters as much as seeing, given how little warning a fast-moving ship gives a small craft in its path, as illustrated by a catamaran capsize at night in a main shipping channel outside Los Angeles Harbor, after which a tanker's first mate remarked that a boat that size would barely have registered before being run down.[10] **Active radar is preferable to a passive reflector** whenever the choice is affordable, since a reflector still depends on another vessel having its own radar on and someone actually watching it, whereas the worst-case scenario — a small boat run down without a trace by a larger vessel running no radar or an unattended watch — is exactly the case a reflector cannot help with.[11] Where a reflector is the only option, stuffing a **hollow mast (and hollow spars generally) with aluminum foil** improves its radar return, best paired with a dedicated active radar-reflector unit for anyone who can afford one.[11]

A **full-timing liveaboard's** power budget differs sharply from a weekend sailor's: laptop and internet use, films with surround sound, music and satellite radio, digital photography and photo editing, and e-books are all loads that can justify a wind generator stacked with solar panels. Surplus generating capacity can also run **12V electric cabin heaters** — keeping the boat dry and warm in cold weather without burning through propane or adding the moisture that propane combustion produces — and power a small **12V watermaker** built to run from the boat's own excess generation at anchor or in fine weather. For anyone only weekend sailing, by contrast, a wind generator is a wasted expense.[15] On reliability, the Air-X wind generator's main reported weakness is **radio-frequency interference** rather than any mechanical shortcoming; the units are sold as marinized, but how successfully remains unconfirmed on record.[16]

For 12-volt wiring-gauge choice — speaker wire versus thickest-affordable versus sized-to-the-load, and the general low-voltage wiring practice for a re-wire — see [electrical wiring](/construction/electrical-wiring.md#12-volt-wiring-gauge-choosing-conductor-size-for-a-low-draw-boat), which also covers masthead-light wiring routing and LED nav-light array wiring in full.

A firsthand account of the Air-X's behavior in strong wind confirms the units work reliably, with one real objection: **noise**. Once wind speed passes about 20 knots, the blades unload themselves in a "horrible racket," tolerable only if the unit can be tethered down. An electric brake gives partial control, working by reversing the generator into a motor so the wind itself fights the blades to a stop.[17] Southwest Windpower's own account of the current **Air-X** model attributes the improvement directly to abandoning the older AIR 403's **"aero-elastic" blade design**, which caused a loud flutter above 35 mph (16 m/s); the Air-X instead monitors wind speed electronically and slows the blades as rated output is reached, holding output at a reduced level in high wind until it eases. Wind generators remain louder than solar panels in any case, but also cheaper.[18]

## A minimalist LED-and-battery fit-out, and off-the-shelf light costs

A minimalist electrical philosophy, stated directly by one owner: **"The less electric gizmos there are the better, because less wiring is required and less electricity is taken from the battery."** In practice this meant a solar panel and a 12V deep-cycle battery (86 Ah) linked through a control box to regulate charging, feeding a cigarette-socket adaptor for GPS units, an Autohelm 800, and a single all-round white navigation-cum-anchor light — kept to that short list on purpose rather than expanded further.[19]

Two small LED lights inherited with a secondhand boat illustrate the range of cheap off-the-shelf options available: a battery-powered 3-LED push light and a solar-powered 3-LED light, the latter no longer working once its specific product had been discontinued, with no replacement source found. A package of three 3-LED battery push lights plus a full set of replacement batteries cost **£7.36** all in from a general online retailer, each light rated for about 100 hours' use before its batteries need replacing, with rechargeable batteries and on-board charging kept as a backup.[20][21] Fitted in place, three such lights lit a main cabin, a fore cabin, and a lazarette from that one small purchase, in 40 minutes' work.[22]

A more permanent, built-in alternative to a stick-on unit: single LED bulbs salvaged from cheap solar garden lights, fixed directly into a small drilled hole with a dab of clear epoxy or silicone rather than housed in a fixture. The draw is low enough that thin, easily hidden wire suffices, and a single bulb this way still throws a surprising amount of light into a dark space.[26]

## Equipping a boat from nothing: a first-time checklist

A builder starting from a blank sheet needs more than a battery. A full working list, drawn from a well-equipped, fully instrumented boat: an anchor light at the masthead (a boat under 7 m needs no separate side/stern navigation lights, a single all-round masthead light being sufficient under the rules where sailed); cabin and locker lighting made from an LED strip pushed into a length of clear hose and sealed at both ends with silicone; a fuse box, sometimes built into the switch panel itself; a switch block combined with a voltmeter, two USB ports, and a cigarette-style socket; a phone (or two, as a backup) for navigation rather than a dedicated chartplotter; a fish/depth finder; a ventilator; a handheld VHF radio, chargeable from a car socket or from AA batteries; a deep-cycle battery, a charger, and an extension cable for topping up power at a marina; and solar panels with a charge controller — two 50 W panels, one on the foredeck and one on the cabin top, sited so a panel on the hatch itself keeps working whether the hatch is open or closed. An electric outboard, used only for maneuvering in and out of a marina, rounds the list out.[27]

A small, self-contained solar power station is a simpler alternative to building a system from parts: a portable unit weighing under 4 lb, with outlets and charging ports already built in, charged either from a wall adapter or from its own solar panel, and light enough to be lifted out of the boat entirely when not needed.[28]

A switch panel is commonly organized around a handful of circuits — a cabin fan, locker lights, running lights, one or more accessory cigarette-style outlets (for an autopilot or a water pump), and the autopilot itself — with each switch protected by its own fuse and a single positive and negative distribution bus in the battery locker feeding the whole panel; a solar controller with Bluetooth lets the charge state be checked from a phone rather than a separate display.[29]

## Battery type: car versus deep-cycle, and real-world endurance

A builder sizing a battery for a modest planned load — a few LEDs, a low-power (4W) VHF radio, and an occasional laptop, all constantly trickle-charged by a solar panel — asked whether an expensive deep-cycle marine battery is really worth it over a plain 12V car battery, already anticipating that such loads "have a habit of multiplying."[23] A **sealed 80 Ah AGM battery**, so well sealed it can be mounted upside down, run with **no on-board charging at all**, was reported running lights, a VHF radio, a GPS, a ballast pump, a phone, and a computer for a full week's boating; using an **electric outboard** brought that down to about **2 hours**.[24]

The construction difference between the two battery types explains why: a **car battery** is built with many thin plates to deliver a brief, high **cranking current** for starting an engine, while a **deep-cycle battery** uses fewer, thicker plates for a steady current draw over time. A car battery is **damaged if discharged past about 50% of its rated capacity**, and its working life shortens further if habitually run down that far, whereas a deep-cycle battery tolerates the same treatment for longer — though any battery lasts longer kept close to fully charged. As a rule of thumb, **only around half of a battery's rated capacity is realistically usable** regardless of type, once ordinary inefficiencies are accounted for — an 80 Ah battery, in practice, is closer to a 40 Ah one.[25]
