# Sculling and yuloh technique

Where [yuloh and stowed-rig retaining loops](/components/yuloh.md) documents how the yuloh is carried aboard a Paradox-family boat, this page documents the technique of actually using one — drawn almost entirely from a first-person account of learning to scull [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) and [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md), published on microcruising.com as "Sculling With a Yuloh" within the site's Watertribe "Lessons Learned" material. It is engine-free auxiliary propulsion in practice: how a 1,600-lb-plus micro-cruiser gets moved by one person, by hand, in calms, foul tides, shoals, and — under real time pressure — mid-race.

## Origin: a tow that convinced them to carry a yuloh

The habit did not start as a purist's choice. During a prolonged calm far from shore, Little Cruiser's outboard failed, and its crew discovered they could not move the **1,600-pound boat** easily or very far using only a dinghy paddle — they had to accept a **tow** to the nearest harbor.[1] That failure was the direct trigger for carrying a yuloh at all afterward, on the reasoning that a yuloh sculls far faster than a paddle can.[2] A paddle, on a boat this heavy, simply is not auxiliary power; a yuloh is.

A later spoken account of the same episode supplies the specifics the written one leaves out: what failed was the outboard's **drive shaft**, and it failed **45 miles offshore**, leaving the dinghy paddle as the only way of moving the boat at all. The yuloh had existed the whole time — it was simply kept at home and never carried on the big trips. From the following year it went along, and it has been standard equipment aboard ever since.[71]

## Technique: a whole-body, one-handed stroke

The yuloh is a long, curved sculling oar worked over the stern, and its central practical advantage over a conventional oar is that it can be worked **facing forward, with one hand**, leaving the other hand free — used in practice to hold a **water bottle**, work the **tiller**, read a **GPS**, or even talk on a **phone** while navigating twisty channels at night (done during the 2010 Everglades Challenge working out of Chokoloskee Bay).[3]

The stroke itself is a full-body motion, not an arm exercise: feet spread, knees bent, push with the arms, and **drive forward with the back leg**, leaning into the oar. To reverse, the blade is **re-pitched** and pulled back using the legs and body rather than the arms alone. In waves or rough water the technique is simply to **keep the blade down deep**. Over long sculling stretches the stroke can be refined into a more rhythmic **body-lean**, using body weight rather than pure arm-and-leg force to conserve energy — it produces more roll but does not cost speed, and makes multi-hour sessions sustainable. Off-season training with kayak paddling and cycling a few times a week builds the fitness for it.[4]

Beyond straight propulsion, the yuloh's other advantage over regular oars is in the **shallows**: it converts instantly between sculling oar and push-pole without changing gear, useful for poling across skinny shoals, fending off groundings, or pushing off obstacles at a moment's notice — a dual role regular oars do not offer.[3]

Practicing outside of easy conditions makes the technique reliable rather than a fair-weather trick: sculling in **small breaking surf**, against **strong foul tides** in inlets, and at **night** around anchorages — the last useful for moving a boat silently away from noisy neighboring boats without starting the engine, one-handed with a cup of coffee in the other on calm mornings when sculling is preferable to motoring at all.[5]

The 2006 spoken account is the earliest dated statement of that same regimen, and it gives the practice a stated target: first the creeks, then the inlets on a flood tide deliberately fighting the current, then the surf itself — kept up until the crew were **proficient enough to scull the boat through breaking surf and up an inlet into safety** if the engine failed or the wind died. The often-repeated claim that anything can be sculled — "you could scull the Queen Mary" — holds only in flat calm with no current; what the practice actually bought was the more useful finding that Little Cruiser could be sculled into *any* inlet, given the willingness to put in the effort.[72]

Night use went beyond propulsion. Sculling into an anchorage after dark in about **a foot of water**, running aground repeatedly while looking for a deep spot, the crew used the yuloh itself to **feel for the deeper channel** — the push-pole role above put to work as a sounding instrument.[73]

Steering with the yuloh itself, independent of any rudder, comes from where the pressure is put in the stroke rather than from any change of angle: pushing harder on the back of the stroke (pulling the blade toward the sculler) turns the boat to starboard, while pushing the blade away turns it to port. A filmed demonstration sculling out of a creek off Pamlico Sound measured the resulting speed directly: about **1.8 kt** sustained, worked up to **2.5 kt** over a longer stretch and just under **3 kt** in short bursts.[92]

## Why the technique has to be second nature

The case for practicing when nothing is wrong is made most sharply by the example of **Dave Gatan** — the Florida Keys liveaboard who later owned [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md), a lifetime sailor who spent years aboard small boats (mostly a 23 ft Glander Cay, later a Rob Roy) and **carried no engine at all**. He kept a sculling oar and used it constantly, going out to scull around the anchorage in the middle of the night when it was calm and he could not sleep. That is the standard the technique has to reach: a sailor who reaches for the oar only in an emergency is fooling himself, because it is exactly then that the skill will not be there.[75]

It paid off in the way that matters. Sailing his 23-footer in the Gulf Stream, Gatan lost his wind with a **freighter bearing down on him**; because he had been practicing for years, getting the oar out and moving the boat a hundred yards clear "wasn't a big deal" — a hundred yards being, as his listeners noted, still uncomfortably close to a large ship.[76]

The same reasoning gives the oar priority over the outboard for small maneuvers even where an engine is carried: outboards have a way of refusing to start at the moment they are actually needed — nerves, a mishandled choke, or nothing identifiable at all — while starting first pull whenever there is no hurry. Reaching for the oar first is therefore the safer habit and not merely the purer one.[77]

Whether a yuloh can be reversed at all is disputed. In principle the stroke above runs backward as readily as forward: reversing is simply a matter of re-pitching the blade and pulling back with the legs and body, the same as any other stroke direction. In practice, however, a sculling oar has also been described as having no reverse and no brake[6] — not necessarily a contradiction, since a maneuver worked out deliberately over several strokes is a different matter from one needed in the seconds before a collision, when there is no time to set one up. Either way the consequence is absorbed structurally rather than avoided: the [stem post](/components/stem.md) is 100 mm (4") thick, so no damage is done to the boat itself when something is hit.[6]

## A figure-eight stroke does not work; a horizontal twist does

A first attempt at yulohing a Paradox, sculling in the punt-style figure-of-eight motion the hands make sculling a small boat with a single blade, did not work. What did work: moving the shaft **horizontally** and **twisting it at the end of each stroke** — a distinct motion from punt-sculling that generates power on a yuloh specifically, confirmed over two days learning the technique from scratch.[7]

The same learning curve shows up from the other direction, in whole-day-sail practice rather than a dedicated trial: a first yuloh attempt on one occasion failed outright, but a second attempt the same session succeeded, making about a quarter of a knot — among the earliest quantified yuloh speeds on record, and consistent with how slowly the horizontal-twist stroke becomes automatic.[8] A first attempt caught on video was later judged by its own sculler to have been quite poor; he moved past the disappointment but chose to equip that boat with an outboard motor rather than persist with the oar, one clear case of a difficult early experience pushing a builder toward the engine instead.[9] A further account of the same early frustration offers a practical fix: when a session goes nowhere after half an hour, stopping for a rest and picking the oar back up a couple of hours later is often enough for the technique to suddenly click.[10]

## A four-action yuloh-teaching method, and where to practice

A repeatable way to teach the stroke to a newcomer: move the handle in a horizontal line at shoulder height and perform four clear actions — twist, push, twist, pull — which, once the hang of it is caught, morphs into a much more fluid motion.[80] Reaching an adequate performance took months of practice; practicing near the shore is easier than doing so out at sea, where there is no stationary reference and it can feel like no progress is being made at all.[80] Relocating the pivot hole itself about 100 mm nearer the handle — the same handle-ward gearing change already on record (see [Yuloh — pivot socket location](/components/yuloh.md#ball-joint-versus-oarlock-and-pivot-socket-wear-over-time)) — made it easier to feel the effort going into each stroke, and easier to control the boat.[80]

## A canoeist's account: alternating 45-degree strokes, and a beginner's tie-down

A further first-hand account, from an owner with a canoeing background, describes the stroke from the blade's own path rather than the sculler's hand motion: the blade pushes water backward at roughly a **45-degree angle**, alternating from one side to the other, tracing a **figure-eight** — a description of the resulting motion, not the punt-style hand motion already ruled out above (see [A figure-eight stroke does not work; a horizontal twist does](#a-figure-eight-stroke-does-not-work-a-horizontal-twist-does)). For a beginner, the main difficulty is keeping enough back pressure against the water; without it the blade tends to float up and pop off its pivot, for which tying the yuloh down with an elastic helps at first, though it becomes unnecessary once the technique is learned. The motion came naturally from a canoeing background: to a canoeist, working a yuloh is much the same as moving a canoe sideways with push-pull figure-eight strokes. The same account records teaching himself to scull left-handed as well as right-handed — workable, though harder, since the position on that side is more awkward.[81]

## Poling: a canoe-poling technique for shallow water, groundings, and going upwind without sail

Distinct from sculling, the yuloh can also be worked as a canoe-style push-pole in shallow water. Carried over directly from years as a canoe-poling instructor in whitewater, the technique works despite the yuloh being shorter than a dedicated 12 ft canoe pole and carrying a bend a canoe pole lacks: pole with the curve's convex face turned outward, so the blade runs partly under the boat, parallel to the hull. The technique is powerful enough to move the boat upwind without the sail in shallow water, and — since poling backward works too — to free the boat from a grounding as well; the recommended way to learn it is practising along a beach in controlled conditions, working up to turning the boat left and right with the rudder fully up, ideally after first trying the same push-pull motion in a canoe.[89]

An owner who had already learned to pole this way at first hand — coached through it in the same correspondent's own canoes, during a visit to Canada — confirmed the technique independently on his own boat: tried on a calm day sailing close to shore with the sail up only for shade, the yuloh (slightly longer than the plans call for) poled the boat without real difficulty, moving her rather than merely holding position, though after a few pokes with the pole he found himself reverting to ordinary yulohing, his own preference. The same account recalled watching his teacher pole his own boat along a lake shoreline at a very fast clip.[90]

## Keeping the yuloh on its ball: a captive-line retainer

A yuloh pivoting on a **bronze ball** can work its way off the ball in use, and doing so is one of the first things a new builder is likely to fight. On the December 2001 [Cedar Key to Honeymoon Island sea trial](/boats/zoe.md#sea-trials-route-cedar-key-to-clearwater) some twenty minutes were lost to this at the very outset, to the point that replacing the ball with a **sculling notch** on returning home was seriously contemplated. The fix arrived instead within the hour and needed no hardware: a line was secured to the **base of the pivot** so as to leave a long tail on each side, the yuloh mounted, and the line tied around its shaft so the oar was held **captive to the ball while still free to move in every direction**. Referred to thereafter as a training device, it was proven within two days, when an anchorage had to be shifted under scull in a rising chop **without** the shaft being tied down and the oar stayed put regardless.[11]

A separate, hardware-free way of avoiding the same lost time: rather than waiting for the boat to come to a complete stop before getting the yuloh re-seated, flipping the blade so it re-enters the water sideways lets sculling resume without that wait.[87]

The same trip supplies the clearest single demonstration of **why** sculling proficiency is worth acquiring on a boat with no engine. On the final morning, two tacks were needed to fetch the centre span of the Honeymoon Island causeway bridge, and all wind was lost on passing under it while a foul tide ran at its strongest. Sculling as hard as possible from a standing start, the boat **stemmed the current and cleared the bridge**, then fell off far enough to gather way under sail again — an outcome credited directly to the week's accumulated yuloh practice.[11]

## When wind overpowers the yuloh: rudder position, and a failure that led to a fall overboard

The yuloh cannot always correct a boat's heading against wind pressure on the bow, and the rudder's position matters more than it might seem. With the rudder down while sculling, there is a tendency for the bow to blow away from the wind; left to drift with no sail set, a boat settles downwind with her quarter angled into the wind, and pulling hard on the windward-side yuloh has little effect against it — a lesson learned the hard way by at least one sailor.[12] The fix is to keep the rudder raised while working the yuloh, except with the wind from astern, so the stern can be shunted from side to side to change course; a small bungee to hold the yuloh on its pin is a companion piece of advice from the same source.[12][13] A further owner reports learning the same lesson through his own trial and error: putting the rudder down on his first few attempts, expecting it to help him steer, left him totally out of control and unable to swing the stern around; once learned, keeping the rudder up "becomes second nature."[78] One account puts the rudder-up requirement even more strongly for a beginner specifically: learning the stroke at all requires the rudder to be up, and a sculler knows the technique has really been mastered once a tight circle can be completed both clockwise and counterclockwise with the rudder still raised.[88]

Keeping the rudder up is not always practical: in wind, when sailing is not an option — inside a narrow channel, for instance — the rudder has to come back down to hold a course, which means reaching down to the steering ropes to steer while sculling.[82] A **small tiller extension** fixed to the rudder head answers the problem without giving up the yuloh's rudder-up efficiency the rest of the time: it lets the rudder be steered with the legs on the rare occasions it must stay down, and works well enough below decks that it sometimes replaces the steering lines entirely.[83] A permanently mounted tiller above the back deck was considered and rejected for a different reason — it would give up the flat aft-deck surface used for filleting fish, washing dishes, drying out the anchor, and showering.[82]

The consequence of not heeding this can be serious. Sailing toward a beach near a slipway on an ebbing tide to anchor, wind and current drifted one boat off her intended spot; an attempt to correct with the yuloh failed outright — the wind caught the bow and set the boat toward a walled bank regardless — forcing a switch to paddling from the foredeck, during which the sculler went overboard entirely and had to haul himself back aboard over the stern.[14] It shows the rudder-raised technique is more than a refinement: without it, a yuloh correction against wind pressure alone can fail outright, with serious consequences.

Even with the rudder raised, the fix is only partial: a Paradox yuloh is genuinely useful only in near-calm conditions, since working it in a force 2 or better still takes real effort just to hold a course, with the bow fighting to be blown off to one side or the other regardless. Sven Yrvind's own boats have been suggested to fare better sculled in a breeze, on the theory that a forward drop board gives the sculler something to brace against — a feature Paradox's stock design lacks. Full account at [Faith — a tide-and-wind case for the outboard, on the Blackwater](/boats/faith.md#a-tide-and-wind-case-for-the-outboard-on-the-blackwater).[15] A further, independent account from different waters gives the same verdict again: a Yuloh that serves well on flat, sheltered water proves useless in a chop and against a strong tidal current alike, and went essentially unused as a result over years of ownership. Full account at [boats/thor.md — sold across the country, and renamed "Pamela B"](/boats/thor.md#sold-across-the-country-and-renamed-pamela-b-2022).[91]

## Performance

Using the full-body technique, Enigma could be driven at **3 knots flat out** in calm conditions; ordinary cruising speed, sustained, was closer to **2 knots**.[16] The yuloh itself is no faster over the water than a regular oar would be on a similarly sized small boat — its advantages are in one-handed forward-facing control and its shallow-water versatility, not raw speed.

Gear evolution cut the other way on at least one occasion: a **new fir yuloh for Enigma with more curve** was later built, modeled partly on the self-feathering properties of [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own oar — but GPS-measured top speed with the new, more-curved oar was **somewhat reduced** compared with the old yuloh, a data point against assuming more curve is simply better.[17]

A UK Paradox owner's experience matches the same ~2-knot cruising figure on a different Layden boat: "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" will push at a comfortable **2 knots all day** under the yuloh. On the Dinghy Cruising Association's annual **Thames raid** it sculled roughly **50 of the ~55 miles**, arriving without the blisters that afflicted the crews of the conventional rowing boats — because sculling lets the body keep shifting position rather than repeat one fixed rowing stroke (see [AYRS meeting talk](/references/ayrs-meeting-video.md)).[18]

A separate firsthand account gives a further sustained-effort data point on the same boat: a 3-mile session averaged just under 2 knots, and the longest continuous stretch run at 4 hours with room to keep going — confirming that sculling a Paradox is a genuinely low-energy exercise even for a self-described non-athlete, though pushing for speed tires a rower out quickly.[19] A further firsthand account, from a different Paradox, corroborates the same figure again: about 2 knots sustained "in a pace I can keep up for a very long time" — consistent with the mass-and-momentum case below that a heavy hull, once moving, takes little further effort to keep going.[20]

A further account, from an owner who had just moved his own pivot point back (see [above](#a-canoeists-account-alternating-45-degree-strokes-and-a-beginners-tie-down)), gives a lower sustained figure still: about **2.5 km/h (1.3 kn)** kept up for long stretches, and over **4 km/h (2.2 kn)** for short spurts — noticeably below the ~2 kn range reported elsewhere on this page, recorded here as a further independent data point rather than reconciled against it.[81]

Those figures sit well above what an unmodified or newly built yuloh can manage. On a fully loaded, provisions-laden cruise, one yuloh whose side-to-side articulation had not yet been corrected could only be worked as a paddle rather than sculled properly; once the articulation was fixed, the same yuloh produced only **about half a knot in smooth water** — a fraction of the 2-3 knot range above, and a reminder that most of a yuloh's output depends on getting the pivot and articulation right before technique or fitness can matter.[21]

A further data point comes from a genuine multi-day cruise rather than a single trial or trained-up passage: **22 nights aboard** on a river cruise covering **140 miles**, of which **90 were made under yuloh** and all but 4 of the remainder under outboard motor rather than sail — comparable in proportion to the Thames raid's own roughly 50-of-55-mile yulohed distance above, and a further demonstration that the oar, not the motor, can carry the bulk of a cruise's actual mileage.[86]

## Watertribe evidence: a micro-cruiser can be cruised motor-free under race pressure

The clearest demonstration that this is a viable primary propulsion method, not just a calm-weather party trick, comes from results across several **Watertribe** events, entered partly out of curiosity and partly to test micro-cruising techniques under pressure — following the example of [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md), who had entered Watertribe events year after year to field-test his own designs.[22]

- **~2008, WCTSS Cedar Key gathering** — the outboard was left behind entirely aboard Little Cruiser, found unnecessary, and the boat was sculled to Atsena Otie Key and other nearby islands during the calms.[23]
- **2009 NC Challenge (inaugural)** — sculled about **11 miles** to finish **first in class** with Enigma fully loaded for cruising at roughly **400 lb**, averaging close to 2 knots except against tide and headwind out of Beaufort.[24]
- **2010 Everglades Challenge** — the first **300-mile** Watertribe Challenge completed, finishing first in class. The hardest test was **Gasparilla Pass at night**: a first attempt against a foul tide and **5-foot breaking seas** failed, forcing hours of waiting before sculling through successfully at the tide change. The 300-mile distance approximates the mileage of the usual Angelfish Creek-to-Georgetown winter cruising route.[25]
- **2010 NC Challenge** — the hardest rowing test yet: **14 miles / 7 hours** of sculling on the first day alone, well beyond a usual roughly two-hour training session, yet still finishing a few hours faster than the prior year.[26]

The conclusion these results support directly: even someone of average fitness can coastal-cruise a micro-cruiser without a motor, even under race time pressure — not only in the leisurely conditions of a winter Bahamas cruise.

## A fully engine-free solo passage (2013)

The technique's furthest expression was a **2013 solo Bahamas trip** aboard Enigma, made deliberately **without an outboard at all**, undertaken in the spirit of oar-and-sail-only voyaging and inspired by mentors **Sven Yrvind** and Matt Layden.[27] The same influences — Yrvind, and **Lin and Larry Pardey**, who cruised worldwide using only an oar as auxiliary power — stand generally behind the case that engine-free auxiliary propulsion is a serious, viable approach rather than a novelty.[28]

Sculling in this sense sits alongside, not in place of, an [auxiliary outboard](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md): most builders carry both, but the Watertribe results and the 2013 passage show the yuloh can substitute for the motor entirely when a sailor commits to the technique.

## Matt Layden's own yuloh speed, and the straight-vs-curved-blade trade-off

During the Everglades Challenge he won, **Matt Layden** himself averaged about **2 knots on the calm section** under yuloh power (see [boats/original-paradox.md](/boats/original-paradox.md)) — consistent with the general ~2-knot cruising speed reported elsewhere on this page.[29]

What separates him from everyone else on this page is not speed but **duration**. An independent 2006 account has Matt sculling **eight, nine, or ten hours** at a stretch, where two owners who had practiced the technique for years reckoned about 2 knots comfortable in Little Cruiser but nothing they could keep up all day. His WaterTribe racing is offered there as the proof that a small boat can be sculled efficiently enough to serve as *primary* propulsion; the lesson drawn for everyone else is deliberately more modest — most sailors will never scull hundreds of miles, but every one of them ought to be able to move a boat a hundred yards clear of a ship, off a lee shore, or back to the beach.[74]

The fullest direct comparison of yuloh design and its practical effect on power and physical strain comes from sculling Little Cruiser on Bahamas trips: **1.5-2 knots** once used to the technique, a best sustained one-mile average of about **2.5 knots**, and short sprints of **3.0 knots**. On a visit to Matt Layden after the Everglades Challenge came a chance to scull the original Paradox, with Matt coaching that the **whole body** go into the stroke. Paradox by then carried a **new sculling oar** — straighter than the plans' design and with a **wider, longer blade** — which proved **more powerful** (easier to control the blade's pitch) but required **much more wrist action**, enough to make the right wrist start hurting; Matt Layden reported that his own wrist had hurt during the first few days of the Everglades Challenge using the same new oar. Weighing the trade-off, the verdict favored Little Cruiser's own **more curved** oar.[30][31] Photos taken afterward showed the tips of both the new oar and the rudder reinforced with **bronze rod** — a rare admission that even the designer felt a real physical cost from his own new gear during the race documented in [boats/original-paradox.md](/boats/original-paradox.md).[32] A year later, Matt Layden confirmed his oar remained straight, saying it still worked well but was harder on the arm and wrist than a curved blade (see [yuloh and stowed-rig retention](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket)).

Having by then used both oars aboard Matt's own boat, that same preference for the original, more curved oar was restated: the extra curve makes the blade scull more automatically, whereas the newer, straighter oar — while more powerful and easier to control for blade pitch — demands noticeably more wrist effort, matching what Matt himself said about it.[33]

### A yuloh-against-oars comparison, from the boat that lost it

The only head-to-head on record between a yulohed Paradox and a rowed boat comes from the rowing boat, and it went the yuloh's way despite the odds: **"I could not keep up with Matt Layden when he was using the yuloh in his 600# Paradox and I was rowing a 650# Sea Pearl that was seven or eight feet longer."**[84] The observation was made in passing, while its author worked out whether to fit oars or a yuloh to his own boat for a WaterTribe race, and it is the reason he came down on the yuloh's side for long stretches: oars win over short distances, but "the yuloh would be more efficient over time," and the length and consequent weight of the oars a wide boat needs is what costs a rower on the bigger boat.[84]

Two cautions on the figures. The **600 lb** is an outsider's estimate of a Paradox's weight, not a measurement, and sits well below the design's 1,410 lb loaded displacement — it is presumably meant as bare-hull or lightly loaded weight, and should not be used as a displacement figure (see [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)). And the comparison is between two *sailors* as much as two rigs; the same source elsewhere reckons Matt Layden could hold **3–4 knots hour after hour** under yuloh in a calm, using the big muscle groups — a figure at the top of the range anyone else on this page reports, and consistent with the duration, rather than speed, that sets him apart.[85]

## Matt Layden's "motors are a luxury" philosophy, put into practice

Matt Layden's own stated view on engines — that motors are fine so long as a sailor understands they are a **luxury**, not something to be relied on to get out of trouble — has been deliberately tested by other builders. Returning from an overnighter on "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" with the outboard deliberately left at home, one builder rigged a makeshift yuloh from a **kayak paddle** that worked adequately and planned a proper yuloh as a next project. Applying Matt's philosophy directly, it became clear the outboard had been compensating for inexperience under sail — a crutch experienced sailors wouldn't need — and what could be managed without it was a pleasant surprise.[34]

A more qualified view holds that a motor is worth keeping as an extra tool, useful against strong contrary currents and on windless days, but weighed against the cost of disrupted trim, the burden of carrying several gallons of fuel, and ongoing maintenance — concluding it comes down to personal choice, and that a sailor who can do without an engine will likely become a better sailor for it. Sailing alongside Matt Layden for **two months in the Bahamas**, almost always together, taught the finer points of tides, wind shifts, and sail trim. That view went further than Matt's own **luxury** framing for the **yuloh** specifically, calling it essential aboard a boat like this — not only for propulsion at 1-2 knots, but as a push pole in the shallows and a fender against rocks and docks; given a straight choice between a motor and a yuloh, the yuloh wins, though Little Cruiser carries both and uses each for its own purpose: sail when there's wind, scull when it's calm and a short distance needs covering, motor when speed is needed on a windless day.[35]

A strikingly similar experience came around the same time from "little Jim"'s Portland cruise (see [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md)): **3 hours** fighting wind and tide within 2 miles of a safe haven (never in real danger, though the weather could have worsened), and wind and tide again for the final mile up the river to the boatyard, leaving the arms too worn out to expect much more use of them the next day. Despite this, the resolve held to stay motor-free for the season at least, allowing only that the launch site might need changing to make that more practical.[36] These near-simultaneous accounts show Matt Layden's motors-as-luxury philosophy being put into practice, under real difficulty, by two different builders in the same week.

The pattern holds among later owners too: dependence on the yuloh for propulsion during calms, even where an electric outboard is also fitted and kept in reserve for when it is truly needed, is reported independently of more than one Paradox owner.[37]

A counterpoint to the motors-as-luxury discipline comes from a scheduled, companioned crossing: sailing to the Isles of Scilly in company with another Paradox, both boats hoping to reach a further destination by a fixed date, going without the outboard would have meant a lot more waiting for the right wind — "if you have a timetable you really do need a motor." The reluctance is framed as psychological rather than physical: the same sailor has made longer coastal passages without a motor "without a second thought" when sailing solo and unhurried, so it is the combination of a schedule and a companion boat, not the distance itself, that tips the balance toward carrying an engine.[38]

## Whether Paradox can be rowed with conventional oars

Distinct from yuloh sculling, whether a Paradox can be rowed at all with conventional oars — accepting a non-optimal result — was raised as an open question, drawing on experience rowing rafts professionally and crossing flatwater stretches with repeated, punishing alternating forward strokes, and picturing a barstool-height seat on the cabin floor with extension arms holding oar pins roughly a foot off each side.[39] Standing and facing forward to row hits a real obstruction: the cabin top is in the way, and extending oar locks would be needed on either side of the boat, with oars about the same length as the yuloh; in practice, the yuloh doubles as a paddle in a pinch, though it tires the arms quickly over any real distance.[40] A photograph from the "Aussie Paradox" album, showing the hull afloat with her ground plate visible, suggested standing rowing looked quite feasible, possibly even without oar-lock extensions, though clearing the oars from the water between strokes might still need them.[41]

One rowlock design floated for the purpose is the "north woods" type, pinned through both the oarlock and the oar so the blade cannot feather, with the oar itself curved down toward the water to clear the cabin top — the same curved oars stowing neatly on deck where the yuloh normally sits; fitting such high-set oarlocks would let a builder dispense with the plans' two deck loops used for stowing the sail rig and yuloh.[42]

A further, untried oar concept proposes rowing standing up and facing forward, with oarlock brackets fitted to the sides of the hatch opening once it is open, well forward — reasoning there would be room to swing the oars through a large enough arc for adequate propulsion, and that the approach would address two recurring yuloh drawbacks: holding the head to wind once it pipes up, and stopping or reversing at close quarters.[43][44] A second oar-port variant, going through the hull sides rather than the hatch, was judged workable in principle but with real drawbacks: the storage shelves on either side would need to change to make room, and plugging the ports when not in use risks the hull's waterproof integrity; rowlocks sited either side of the cabin top close to the aft end of the open hatch — rather than at the hatch opening itself — were suggested as an alternative siting, again with the oarsman standing and facing forward to push the oars for momentum.[45]

A further data point on doing without an outboard entirely: one owner fitted no motor at all to "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" in her first season, specifically to force himself to learn to sail, coming to the boat mainly from a motor-boating background — the same deliberate discipline documented for "Whisper" above — which pushed him to use the yuloh for non-sailing maneuvering until he became quite proficient; a small electric trolling motor was added only afterward, stowed awkwardly enough that it is used only once sail and yuloh have both failed.[44]

## Carrying a spare yuloh: Smith's mass-and-momentum case against it

Whether to carry a **spare yuloh** — reasoning by analogy from canoeing, where a spare paddle is considered essential in case the primary is lost or broken — is bound up with a related doubt: given Paradox's weight, would she even scull well at all? A paddler's intuition suggested not, on the theory that a long, skinny, lightly built racing hull gets far more carry between strokes than a heavy displacement hull ever could, which would feel instead like pushing too much water and decelerating quickly.[46]

The general physics of mass and momentum runs the other way, with the intuition backwards: a **heavier** vessel carries **farther**, not less far, between strokes — the same principle that let old-time schooners work their way up rivers and around bends under momentum alone. Illustrated with paired examples at matched speed: a Hobie 18 doing 15 knots stops almost the instant its sails come down, while a supertanker at the same 15 knots will coast on for a long time after "all stop"; a skateboard and a hundred-car freight train, both on level ground, show the same contrast. A solo paddler in a kevlar canoe must maintain a constant rhythm to keep moving, but the same canoe loaded with a crew and two weeks of provisions and camping gear will carry through a pause in paddling. What a **light** hull gains instead is **acceleration**, not carry — the quality behind a catamaran's sudden burst of speed or a racing crew's snap off the start line — though even there, an eight-man rowing scull, with its tremendous power and low wetted surface on a long waterline, will ultimately carry farther than the accelerating catamaran.[47]

On the spare-yuloh question specifically, a spare was judged unnecessary: no more needed than a spare diesel engine aboard a sailing auxiliary, since the yuloh is **auxiliary** propulsion, not primary, unlike a canoe paddle, where losing the only paddle genuinely strands the paddler.[47] Plenty of yuloh how-to material exists online, but none of it worth the price of the Paradox plans — which already include **yuloh plans** as standard, confirmed separately.[48]

A firsthand account bears out the no-spare-needed judgment from the opposite direction: with the outboard not working, a yuloh drifted off in the current during a moment's distraction; rather than needing a second yuloh, the boat's own wooden seat board served as an improvised paddle to retrieve it, awkward but effective. See [boats/ironsides.md — losing the yuloh in the current, and the seat as an improvised paddle](/boats/ironsides.md#losing-the-yuloh-in-the-current-and-the-seat-as-an-improvised-paddle) for the fuller account.[61]

## A permanent tether against losing the yuloh overboard

Beyond keeping the blade seated on its pivot, a related but distinct problem is losing the yuloh overboard altogether — the failure behind the seat-as-paddle rescue above. A permanent-tether fix removes the risk outright: a loop of bungee fixed around the shaft just below the pivot, connected by a line that runs down the transom through a fairlead and back up on deck to a cleat, so that tightening the line holds the yuloh onto the pin; the line's inboard end stays permanently fastened on deck, long enough that the yuloh can still be stowed correctly, keeping it attached to the boat at all times, with a small snap hook allowing it to be freed entirely when the yuloh needs to come off for travelling. The arrangement was adopted after the yuloh had gone overboard about three times, following the same principle already used aboard Little Cruiser.[68]

A Chinese yuloh seen in use in Southeast Asia dispenses with a pin altogether, simply riding in a notch cut into the transom; fitting a leash was suggested as a comparably simple safeguard for that arrangement.[69] More commonly, a dedicated stern pivot similar to an oarlock both spares the transom chafing and tethers the yuloh if it is let go, with a ready-made swivel fitting of this kind reportedly available from Duckworks.[70]

## Slalom-stroke sculling oars, and whether they improve on the yuloh's falling-leaf stroke

A Japanese VE-SCULL sculling-oar design, said not to stall the blade's leading edge on the return stroke and reportedly simple enough that a trained monkey can use it, was raised as a possible improvement on the standard yuloh.[49][50] Whether that is a genuine improvement is doubtful: a first reaction judged it might be nothing more than an oar built for the slalom stroke rather than the falling-leaf stroke a yuloh uses,[49] a second correspondent, no better at conventional sculling himself, offered only the same reassurance that even an inexperienced user could manage it, without settling the question,[51] and a closer technical look agreed that whether a blade stalls is a function of its foil section, angle of attack, and how fast it is worked — factors any slalom-scull designer would already be accounting for, so the VE-SCULL's claimed advantage may not mark a genuine difference from a well-shaped yuloh blade at all.[52] The slalom stroke itself is understood to be the conventional Western sculling technique, with the yuloh's falling-leaf stroke historically the oriental system by contrast.[53]

The accompanying idea — fitting a fence to the underside of the blade, parallel to the waterline, to stop water breaking away and causing tip vortex[50] — runs up against how a yuloh is actually worked in practice: the shaft's angle over the stern is already varied by the sculler depending on the maneuver, steeper to accelerate hard or move fast for a few moments, more horizontal for ordinary cruising or in shallow water, so a fixed fence would need to be adjustable across that whole range to stay useful.[52] A fence at the blade's tip would still be useful for one purpose regardless of the tip-vortex question: stopping the blade's end from sinking into the mud when the yuloh is used as a quant/push-pole in the shallows.[52]

The slalom blade does carry one clear advantage over the falling-leaf yuloh blade: it can be used as a rudder while freewheeling, and it allows quicker turns to be made — a real, if narrow, point in its favor even where the stalling and laminar-flow claims turn out not to matter much.[52]

Nine months after first reporting work on a VE-SCULL-style oar, one builder confirmed still shaping it for his own boat, preferring its lighter demand on the wrists to the ball-and-socket yuloh's — a concern shared with Matt Layden's own newer, straighter yuloh, likewise reported hard on the wrist (see [Yuloh](/components/yuloh.md#building-the-yuloh-itself-blade-curvature-and-the-pivot-boltsocket)). The same builder identified the amateurboatbuilding.com article behind the design as the source of a separate, secondhand report circulating elsewhere on the list of a builder who found a plain vertical oar faster than his own yuloh (see [Yuloh — an alternative sculling-oar shape](/components/yuloh.md#an-alternative-sculling-oar-shape-the-vertical-scimitar-blade)), tying the two previously separate threads to the same original source.[54]

A further, unrelated mechanized alternative was raised the same week: a lever-actuated device, commercialized in 2008 as the "ScullMatix," works an ordinary oar automatically through a side-to-side lever motion rather than a learned sculling stroke, developed from the same Chinese/Japanese sculling tradition the yuloh itself descends from.[55] Two Paradox owners judged it looked distinctly awkward in practice, particularly set against video already circulating in the community of real yuloh technique aboard Little Cruiser and Faith.[56][57]

The VE-SCULL story's own "trained monkey" claim resurfaced nearly a year later as a joking aside under the mocking subject line "Ve-scull," embellished with the monkey getting "into the sake" — drawing a laugh in reply, and a further riff from the builder still shaping his own version of the same design, wondering aloud whether he might manage even fewer kilometres to the banana than the monkey — no further substance added.[58][59][60]

## A proposed flexible, fishtail-mimicking blade tip (2010)

A further proposal, distinct from the slalom-stroke question above, sought to mimic fish propulsion directly: building the outer 50 cm of the yuloh's blade from a flexible material to copy a fishtailing motion, on the reasoning that this would use the whole extent of each stroke more effectively.[62] The idea was likened to commercial monofin swim-fin design as a comparable precedent.[63] Refined further, the proposal specified tying flexible fin material to the oar's pole in a symmetrical arrangement and cutting away the surplus.[64] It drew a skeptical technical reply: a flexible tip would make an entirely different device from the yuloh as designed — a slalom-stroke oar might absorb such a change as an evolutionary step, but the Paradox yuloh uses a falling-leaf stroke instead, in which the blade must behave like a wing, and flexible wings are not generally a good thing.[65] A fuller reply distinguished the fish comparison directly: a fish's tail motion is entirely different from a yuloh's falling-leaf stroke, closer in principle to a slalom-type stroke that has, in some existing though uncommon devices, been developed toward a flexible fishtail; the falling-leaf stroke produces thrust the way an aircraft wing does, which a flexible blade is hard to picture doing — a position offered as open to being proved wrong.[66] Answering in turn, the idea's proponent held that the yuloh is not a critical component of the boat to begin with, existing only to provide motion when sailing is not advisable; that a yuloh's efficiency is understood to increase the closer it works to vertical, easily shown mechanically and illustrated by the gondolas of Venice; and that a flexible tip would not mimic a wing at all but a fish's tail, pointing to the ordinary swim fin as a working example of effective flexible propulsion.[67] The modification was floated but never reported built or tried.

## Coming to a yuloh after decades of plain sculling

A further first-hand contrast, distinct from the beginner accounts above, comes from someone with decades of prior experience sculling a conventional oar on his own dinghy — "hard, concentrated work." A proper yuloh, by comparison, is "a revelation." Even that much conventional sculling experience did not make the yuloh intuitive on first use: trying a fellow owner's yuloh aboard "Little Jim," the technique "wasn't intuitive."[79]
