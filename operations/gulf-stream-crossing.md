# Gulf Stream crossing (Florida–Bahamas)

The passage across the Gulf Stream between Florida and the Bahamas aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) — and, on at least one occasion, sailed alongside [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) himself in the original [Paradox](/paradox.md) — is the signature open-water passage of the whole [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) design family. It is roughly **65 nautical miles** of open ocean, made **five times** (later "now seven") by the time of a May/June 2000 interview, and repeated many times since.[1] This concept records the specific, repeatable method developed for it: timing, routing, and — the part that surprises most newcomers — collision avoidance with ship traffic, treated as the real danger of the crossing, not weather. It is a companion to [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md), which covers storm-survival rigging (sea anchor, rudder position, lying a-hull) rather than passage planning.

## The crossing itself

The shortest Miami–Bimini route is not a straight line: the Gulf Stream sets **north** along its axis at up to **3.5 knots**, which nets out to roughly **2.5 knots of northward drift per hour** over a **12-hour crossing** — enough that a boat aiming straight for Bimini would be swept well north of it.[2] To compensate, the crossing starts from well south of the direct line, departing from **Key Largo** or, a little farther north, **Angelfish Creek** (stock up in Key Largo first, since "there really isn't anything at Angelfish Creek"). Get up and depart around **6 a.m.**, steer **due east** if motoring, and hold that course without much adjustment until the **last five miles** to Bimini, where the Stream's effect lessens and the true bearing to the island can finally be steered.[3] The return trip typically makes landfall near **Fort Lauderdale**, from which the route runs down the Intracoastal Waterway — "the ditch" — back to Key Largo.

## Weather doctrine: never cross with a north wind

The one hard rule is directional, not a wind-speed threshold: **never cross with the wind out of the north**, because a north wind blows directly against the north-setting Gulf Stream current and produces dangerously steep, breaking seas.[2] East winds are merely slow going (on the nose); the seas only turn genuinely nasty with north wind against the current.

The timing exploits the normal wind rotation ahead of a cold front, which **clocks SE → S → W → NW → N**. The ideal is to leave while the wind is **still out of the south**, so that it swings favorably onto the beam and then the stern as the crossing proceeds, ideally reaching port before it backs around to the north. A secondary strategy is to leave right during a **weak** cold front itself, since frontal north winds, even though from the "wrong" direction, are at least steady rather than shifting — acceptable if under about 10 knots. Dead calm or very light days are good too.[4]

Good weather windows are scarce: a small boat typically gets only about **one good Gulf Stream crossing day per week** in January–March, and the wait for the right conditions has sometimes stretched as long as **two weeks**.[5] A large, capable boat (for example a 60-footer) could cross almost any time, but would still get "bounced around" in 10-14 ft seas when it's blowing 35-40 knots out of the north — the small-boat weather discipline is about avoiding that scenario entirely, not surviving it.

The doctrine holds even after departure: if conditions look **foreboding**, turn back — "don't be afraid to turn around even if you have already started." This has been done exactly so "several times in the first ten miles of a crossing."[6]

## The real danger is ship traffic, not weather

The greater hazard crossing the Gulf Stream is **man-made**: large ships, many running on autopilot, may not see a small boat even with radar, and cannot maneuver or stop in time regardless. The collision-avoidance method:[7]

- **Cross in daylight** whenever possible.[8]
- Take a **hand-bearing-compass** bearing on any approaching ship, then repeatedly recheck it. A **steady, unchanging bearing means a collision course**.[9]
- **Never try to cross ahead of a ship's bow** — ships travel "upwards of 14 knots," faster than it looks from a small boat's low vantage point.[7]
- When several ships are visible at once — for example a 1998 return trip to Fort Lauderdale with roughly a dozen ships in sight in the last hours of the crossing — prioritize bearing checks on only the most threatening ones.

The stakes are not abstract. A couple were **run down at night in the middle of the Gulf Stream** by another sailboat: its skipper had switched the boat to autopilot to make coffee and had shut off his navigation lights to save electricity, and a lookout posted on deck aboard the striking boat never saw the smaller boat coming.[10] The scariest moment on the crossing in Little Cruiser was being caught at night between two large freighters, unable to judge either ship's distance or closing speed from their lights alone — which is why daylight is now the preferred time to make the passage.[8]

## Real crossings as evidence

- **1993** — the first Bahamas passage in Little Cruiser, departing after several days of 35-knot northerlies had finally eased. The crossing left in light air and met large Atlantic swells "as tall as our mast" that blanketed the wind in the troughs; the sails were sheeted in tight and the boat **motor-sailed 14 hours**, making landfall at **Cat Cay** (at the time still storm-damaged from Hurricane Andrew).[11]
- **1995** — sailing in company with Matt Layden, newly arrived overland in the original Paradox. Both boats crossed in "sloppy" 5-7 ft seas; the crossing became **becalmed 25 miles out** (about halfway) at daybreak when an unpredicted easterly headwind replaced the forecast beam wind. Little Cruiser motored the rest of the way and made Bimini that afternoon after **20 hours**; Matt — "always the sailing purist" — refused a tow and instead sailed or sculled, arriving a day later.[12]
- **2001** — a much quicker crossing, completed in **9.5 hours**, on a cruise that departed Key Largo for the Exumas, Cat Island, Georgetown and Andros.[13]
- **2013** — a solo **night** crossing, made "amongst dozens of fast moving ships" and without an outboard aboard, a direct test in practice of the bearing-check ship-avoidance method described above.[14] A later retelling puts the outbound leg at **17 hours** from Key Largo to a safe harbour in North Bimini over particularly calm seas, with shipping the whole difficulty. The **return** is the sharper illustration of what an engineless crossing demands: the passage went perfectly until the **wind died 12 miles from land**, leaving several hours of **sculling** to reach Fort Lauderdale.[15] See [sculling and yuloh technique](/operations/sculling-and-yuloh-technique.md#a-fully-engine-free-solo-passage-2013).

The **1993** entry gains two details from the same retelling. That first crossing departed from **Miami** — the boat towed down for the purpose — rather than from the Key Largo/Angelfish Creek start the method above settles on, and customs was cleared at Miami again on the way home. And the swells "as tall as our mast" are given a number: the mast is **14 feet**.[15]

Weather can turn dangerous even on the far side of the Stream, once inside the Bahama Bank: on the 1993 trip, the **75 nm** run from Chub Cay (Berry Islands) to Bimini began in 15-20 kt wind that built overnight, with a cold front, to at least 40 kt. Little Cruiser held hull speed downwind at **5 knots** with her last reef tied in, surfing regularly in breaking seas; when one crew member became too seasick to continue steering, the other took the helm for the remainder of the **17-hour** passage.[16]

## Resting mid-passage: the sea anchor option

On a long or rough crossing, a **parachute (sea) anchor** is the option for stopping to rest rather than pressing on exhausted — carried "in case we want to rest or we get caught out in something really ugly," though one has never actually been needed in a genuine emergency.[17] The full treatment of sea-anchor deployment, rudder handling while riding to one, and the alternative (not recommended) of lying a-hull is covered in [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md); ground tackle and rode specifications are covered in [ground tackle](/operations/ground-tackle.md).
