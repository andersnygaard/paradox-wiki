# Trailering — tow weight and vehicle capacity

See also [car-topping conversion ideas](/design/ideas/car-topping-conversion-idea.md) for car-topping considered as an alternative to trailering altogether, on Enigma's lighter hulls.

A Paradox's **fully loaded, ready-to-sail weight** — not its bare hull weight — is what determines whether a given tow vehicle is adequate. That loaded figure is around **410 kg**, comfortably within, for example, a 2000 Volkswagen Golf TDI's 900 kg tow rating (a bare-hull figure of a little over 650 kg is sometimes quoted separately, and refers to the unloaded boat).[1][2]

Reported loaded weights vary somewhat depending on the individual boat's build and exactly what is counted as "loaded": alongside the ~410 kg figure, a heavier-built boat's loaded weight has been recorded at roughly 620 kg (~1,365 lb — see [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)), and the design displacement at the design waterline is **1,410 lb** — twice a familiar 20-footer's weight at first glance, until the figure is understood as loaded and crewed rather than bare hull.[3]

## Converting a boat's own displacement into an actual trailer load

The trailer load a Paradox actually presents is well below her full sailing displacement, because most of that displacement leaves the boat before she goes on the road. Starting from a boat ballasted to about 1,400 lb displacement, taking out the skipper, the water ballast, and a few other items just as easily carried in the tow vehicle brings the real trailer load down to around **1,000 lb**; removing still more — anchors, lead ballast, tinned food — trims it further, at the cost of reloading everything at the launch site. A heavier anchor or a heavier skipper taken on afterward can simply be offset by removing a little lead.[4] Because ballast sits low specifically to aid self-righting, however, treating it as a free variable to trim for trailering is not a simple, cost-free adjustment.[5]

Rigged and loaded exactly to the design waterline, the 1,410 lb design displacement breaks down as follows: towed fully loaded except for a roughly 200 lb skipper, trailer load comes to about **1,210 lb**; with the water ballast tanks also empty, about **1,060 lb**. Built boats rarely hit the exact design displacement, and a Paradox shows no noticeable handling difference from 100 lb more or less than the design figure, possibly considerably more — sailing without the water tanks topped up (a 150 lb difference) or with an extra passenger aboard both go largely unnoticed, and checking actual displacement is rarely necessary once a boat is sailing well. **Matt Layden**'s own practice on a long cruise was to remove some ballast and replace it with stores, then replace depleted stores with sand or gravel in bags as they were used up.[6][7]

Of a Paradox's roughly 1,400 lb sailing displacement, on the order of **400 lb is typically lead ballast**, with the balance made up of gear, stores, water, and the skipper — a boat carrying several anchors and a lot of stowed food and gear correspondingly needs less lead to reach the same total.[7]

## A trailer-failure data point, and the case for overbuilding

A stock or lightly-modified trailer built for a small, lightweight boat is **not adequate** for a Paradox. On "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)," driving home from a sail, a loose strap led to the discovery that the trailer's **winch was bent and nearly pulled out**, its mounting support also bent, with **4-5 teeth broken off the winch gear** — a close call underlining the governing lesson that a Paradox needs a well-built trailer, and that the general advice is to **overbuild**.[8]

The safest approach is to go well over the top on sturdiness rather than adapt something marginal: a trailer built for an inflatable boat, expected to be just within the Paradox's weight limit, is a case worth checking against professional judgment by shopping the adaptation around to several trailer manufacturers before trusting it.[9]

## Glen Maxwell's trailer specifications, and a loaded-height target

The most detailed trailer-build specification on record comes from "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)." The root cause of trailer failures like the one above: a trailer built for a boat of only about **500 lb**, where a Paradox loaded and ready for a week's cruising comes in close to **900 lb**.[10] The recommended rebuild sequence:

- Remove the fenders.
- Fit a **longer axle** and **heavier three-leaf springs** (2,000 lb rated).
- Mount the axle **on top of the springs** (rather than underneath) to lower the trailer.
- Have the **spindles welded to the top of the axle** instead of coming out the ends — a further 1.5 in of height reduction.
- Build **fender extensions** afterward to bring the fenders back out over the tires, clear of the narrower frame.

This sequence gave Zoë's trailer a **loaded height to the bottom of the boat of 14 in**; adding the Paradox's roughly **9 in waterline draft** comes out to just under 2 ft overall — the target worth aiming for. Even this setup would not suit every launch site: at **Apalachicola** (see [Apalachicola Bay — a proposed meeting ground, scouted solo](/merits/gatherings.md#apalachicola-bay-a-proposed-meeting-ground-scouted-solo-2002)), the drop-off is shallow enough that launch or recovery at low tide is in doubt, and a **magic-tilt-type trailer** may help in such cases. Trailer parts of the kind described above are available from **Northern Tool & Equipment** (catalog by phone, 800-556-7885).[10]

This ~900 lb loaded-for-cruising figure converts to almost exactly **410 kg** — the same fully-loaded weight used above for tow-vehicle sizing, just expressed in different units.

## Ground clearance for Gulf Coast ramps

Ground clearance from the bottom of the boat to the ground is a key rebuild variable for shallow ramps. A trailer rebuilt to leave **24 3/4 in** of clearance was judged workable for **Cedar Key**, whose ramp is comparatively steep, by comparison with "Zoë," whose hull bottom sits **15 in** off the ground — a height at which retrieval at low tide was still in doubt at **Apalachicola** (see [Apalachicola Bay — a proposed meeting ground, scouted solo](/merits/gatherings.md#apalachicola-bay-a-proposed-meeting-ground-scouted-solo-2002)), though other ramps in that area reportedly allow launch/retrieval at low water. Switching to **pontoon (low-profile) tires** can gain roughly another **3 in** of clearance reduction where needed; a **tilt trailer** is a workable base design for this kind of rebuild.[11][12]

## Low-profile and retracting-trailer concepts

For launching off a **slip** without wetting the wheels or brakes, and also loading/unloading onto a **flat surface such as a beach** at low tide, several approaches have been discussed[13]:

- **Drift-boat-type trailers** (used to launch drift boats into very shallow rivers from the banks) are a useful reference design.[14]
- **Torsional axles** — each side sprung/mounted independently of the other — allow the bottom of the boat to sit lower, below the axle itself, and are available from **Northern Tool & Equipment**. In the UK, almost all trailers use **independent rubber-in-compression suspension units** rather than leaf springs, which are rare to see there.[14][15][16]
- A **motorcycle trailer** design using a torsional axle plus a **winch** to lower its bed flat onto the ground suggests a possible scaled-up approach for a boat trailer, though likely expensive to build as a one-off.[17]

A more radical concept, considered but never built: mounting a pair of **torsion axles inside the boat structure itself**, at bulkhead #3, so that once the boat was launched the axles (with wheels attached) could be **unbolted or unpinned from inside and pulled away** from the hull while it floated — with the **towbar** similarly detachable from the stem. The wheels, axles, and towbar would then stow in the tow vehicle until needed again, with the mounting ports in the hull sides and stem covered over when not in use. The idea is judged to have merit but was never developed further.[18]

A related concept seen on a dinghy at a boat show: a towbar slotted through a hole in the bow, with wheels that **lowered like an aircraft undercarriage** by winding a handle. That particular implementation is judged flawed — it used standard car suspension parts, likely a maintenance nightmare, with doubts about how a boat's structure would hold up to road-pounding with wheels mounted so directly to it — but the **basic principle is sound**. A simpler variant: a **suspension-free launching undercarriage** used together with a car-transporter-type road trailer, retracting once on the trailer to lower the boat onto conventional supports for the road. A third variant: **leaving the tow ball on the car** and simply **dropping the trailer's back end onto the ground** to lower the boat for launch/recovery, rather than retracting the axle or undercarriage itself. As with the other concepts in this group, any such actuating mechanism would need to be simple and inexpensive to be worthwhile — none of these retracting-axle or retracting-undercarriage concepts is recorded as ever having been built or tested.[19][20]

## A small, affordable trailer option, and its real-world weight limit

The **"T-2000"** trailer sold by **Portage Pal** (`portagepal.com`) is a small, relatively affordable commercial option — but inadequate for a Paradox: it would **not even carry an empty Paradox**, let alone one loaded with stores and food for a couple of weeks' cruising, which brings her weight up to around **1,000 lb**. A **custom-built trailer** rated to a proper **2,500 lb capacity** costs only a little more than the undersized commercial option — a concrete counter-example reinforcing the same don't-under-build-the-trailer lesson documented above.[21][22]

## Adapting a used heavy-duty trailer (the Bolduc/Layden approach)

Long experience trailering [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) alongside Paradox points to a **used trailer that is fairly heavy duty** for either boat: because both designs have **flat bottoms**, they adapt readily to almost anything from a flatbed to a custom trailer with simple modifications — a looser, more forgiving approach than the detailed built-to-spec rebuild worked out for "Zoë" trailer (above).[23]

Even a repurposed trailer must not be under-built: a lightweight-to-medium-duty trailer rated for only **1,400 lb**, used for Little Cruiser, has needed **welding gussets and reinforcement plates onto it ever since** — bouncing down the road and across railroad tracks with a full load cracks the welds. It also went through **several blow-outs** with the trailer's original **skinny 4 in x 12 in** tires before switching to **full-size trailer tires** matching those of the tow vehicle. As set up, Little Cruiser rests on the trailer on **two treated 2x6 boards amidships** and a **2x4 board forward**, with the axle mounted **atop the leaf springs** — the same height-lowering technique used for Zoë's trailer above — allowing launch at most ramps.[23]

**Matt Layden**'s own approach illustrates the alternative end of the spectrum: rather than build or heavily modify a trailer, he simply bought a good used heavy-duty powerboat trailer with full-sized wheels, cheaply. It is a characteristically pragmatic, minimal-effort approach to boat-support infrastructure, consistent with his prioritizing simplicity and low cost elsewhere (see, e.g., his original ~4-month, bicycle-trailer-hauled build in [original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md)) — here applied to trailer selection rather than the boat itself.[23]

## Trailer equipment: brakes and bearing buddies

A well-specified trailer need not have brakes: the custom-built trailer for "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" has **no brakes**, but does use **bearing buddies** (grease-fitting hub caps that keep wheel bearings packed and protected during repeated launch/retrieval) — a short, concrete data point rounding out the trailer specification given above.[24]

## Always carry a spare tire

Even a **brand new** trailer can suffer a **tire blowout** — one occurred before even reaching the boat ramp, with no spare aboard, on a long Cumberland Sound trip aboard "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)." The failure happened close enough to a tire store to walk to for a replacement — a matter of luck, since the same problem far from any town would have been far worse. The lesson, distinct from the build-quality/overbuilding lessons above since the trailer itself was new and presumably sound: **always carry a spare**.[25]

## UK trailer weight limits, and whether brakes are necessary

The governing **UK legal limit** for an unbraked trailer: it may weigh up to **750 kg gross**, or **half the towing vehicle's kerb weight**, whichever is lower — so adapting a candidate dinghy trailer (large transverse leaf-spring suspension, unbraked wheels) for a laden "Enuf" may well be legal without brakes. In practice, though, a loaded trailer of that kind would be very difficult for one person (or even two) to manhandle with no brakes at all; keeping the wheels from getting wet during launch and recovery makes the lack of trailer brakes much less of a liability. For comparison, "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" and "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" both ride on fairly ordinary trailers.[26][27]

A choice of tow vehicle between an aging short-wheelbase Land Rover (poor brakes, low speed) and a modern saloon (higher speed, better brakes) need not weigh unbraked-trailer risk too heavily: private trailers are stopped and checked mainly when flagrantly breaking the law (such as speeding), since enforcement effort concentrates on commercial trailers, which are more often overloaded or in poor condition.[28]

A concrete worked example against the same 750 kg limit came from a prospective builder totting up his own trailering weight in advance: subtracting an estimated 70 kg of water ballast and 80-90 kg of crew and gear from the total put the boat itself at 500 kg or under, comfortably inside the unbraked limit — prompting a request for the specific loaded trailering weight of "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" and a recommendation for a suitable trailer; noted "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" already trails on an unbraked trailer.[29]

A real weighbridge reading against the same limit came in just over it: "Faith"'s own trailer, weighed loaded with food and basic gear but no water in the tanks, came to 760 kg against the 750 kg unbraked ceiling — so heavy gear is now loaded only after arriving at the slip, to stay legal on the road.[30]

## Little Jim's actual trailered weight, and two contrasting UK trailer setups

The request above drew two firsthand answers. "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"'s own trailer is unbraked, with the wheels always below the water at launch and retrieval — the depth needed to get the boat on or off — and sealed bearings that have given no trouble.[31] "Little Jim"'s trailered weight settled the question directly: at last weighing, the boat came to **526 kg** on the trailer loaded with stores, and the specially built trailer itself — sturdy enough to need no wheels in the water at all — weighs just over **400 kg**, for an all-up weight well over **900 kg**; staying under the 750 kg unbraked limit might well be legal, but brakes are worth fitting regardless on anything but a level driveway.[32] Weighing the two contrasting setups — one keeping the wheels wet and unbraked, the other dry and braked — a braked trailer was judged easier to control on slipways and ramps, recalling an older unbraked trailer-tent of about 500 kg that had given "a few fun times" on sloping ground; building a custom trailer with a neighbour experienced in welding, from a donor caravan chassis, was then considered as a way to get a wheels-dry launch without the storage space a trailer as large as "Little Jim"'s own would need at home.[33][34] Pressed on exactly that storage worry, "Little Jim"'s own trailer was described as built as near as possible to the boat's own size, with the drawbar sticking out the front and the wheels sticking out the side; narrower storage was offered as possible in principle by raising the boat and fitting the wheels underneath instead.[35]

## UK combi-trailer prices, and buying a fellow builder's used trailer

UK prices for a suitable trailer vary widely depending on weight rating and whether it is bought new, built custom, or found used. A new combi trailer commands enough of a premium that buying secondhand from a fellow Paradox owner, on the strength of that owner's own experience with it, is a worthwhile way to beat the price of new. By contrast, a lighter-built boat can get away with a lighter, cheaper trailer, and a new stock trailer bought at a discount off list price costs distinctly less than a heavier combi trailer would. A custom-built trailer, sized to a boat's own requirements for simple single-handed launch and recovery, sits in between — in line with the fuller custom-trailer cost accounting given for "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md#weigh-in-and-a-full-build-cost-accounting-januarymarch-2003)."[36][37][38]

## A stock power-boat trailer, adapted for tongue weight

A **regular power-boat trailer** works fine for towing both Little Cruiser and Swamp Thing, needing only custom **2x6 treated-lumber bunks** shaped to follow each hull's rocker. Because that kind of trailer is designed around a boat with a heavy engine hanging off the transom, a Paradox has to sit centered **slightly ahead of the axle**, leaving the stern looking as if it hangs off the trailer a bit — the tradeoff that gets the **tongue weight** into the right range, roughly **100-150 lb** for a Honda Accord tow vehicle and a fully-loaded, expedition-ready boat. The exact figure is not critical, but too little tongue weight lets the boat bounce and pitch sharply over bumps; on periodic 900-mile trips to the Florida Keys, Little Cruiser's trim on the trailer has been adjusted using trip stores and loose lead ballast to keep the tongue weight comfortable.[39]

Building from a similar stock power-boat trailer, one builder modified his to **sit lower** for launching at low tides, ahead of attending the group's Cedar Key gathering.[40]

## A 2016 recurrence: transverse versus longitudinal bunks

Looking into buying a trailer, one builder weighed an extendable tongue or a hinged drawbar arm to get the trailer deeper into the water at launch, against two competing bunk layouts for a boat now carrying water ballast: **transverse bunks** under bulkheads 2 and 4 — the same general approach a previous, unballasted stitch-and-glue boat of his had used — against a **transverse bunk under bulkhead 1** paired with **two longitudinal bunks** centered under bulkhead 3 and the chines. Reasoned that U-shaped transverse bunks could automatically secure the boat once loaded, where longitudinal bunks would instead need tie-downs, and wondered whether the boat — lighter once her water ballast is drained — would trim bow-up enough on the trailer to need a guide up to the bulkhead-1 bunk at all.[41]

Told in reply that bunk placement is not structurally critical on a boat built to the plans — the structure is strong enough to take the load anywhere. One owner's own trailer instead uses **four transverse rollers**: two supporting the boat while travelling, and two further aft that let the boat be winched on and off. This arrangement means never having to put the wheels in the water at a slipway, and allows launch and recovery even on a dried-out beach; after 13 years the trailer's brakes have still never been wet.[42]

A further reply cautioned that fitting bunks at all implies submerging the trailer to float the boat on and off, which is bad practice for any trailer and especially a braked one — galvanised hollow rectangular box sections rot out from the inside over time, and a fully cruise-loaded Paradox is too heavy for an unbraked trailer regardless. The trailer that came with one owner's own boat was of that unbraked, 750 kg-rated kind, and proved totally inadequate — its suspension fractured on a journey home. Two owners' current trailers, by contrast, are "totally sorted," allowing launch either onto dry ground or straight into the water depending on the tide; because Paradox has a flat bottom, a flat trailer frame fitted with rubber rollers is straightforward to build, letting the boat be winched on or slid off without ever wetting the trailer itself.[43] A further owner's previous, non-Paradox boat had likewise towed fine on a random-axle unbraked trailer, and his own Paradox trailer carries no brakes either.[44]

Weighing the replies, the same builder noted a large difference between his own country's and the UK's unbraked-trailer weight limits — under 2,000 kg needs no brakes in New Zealand, against under 750 kg in the UK — and explained that dry-launching and waiting for the tide, workable enough in the UK, would not suit lake and harbour sailing grounds where the wait could be endless. Having priced a custom-built trailer and found the cost unrealistic, planned instead to modify a secondhand trailer, ruling out a bespoke tipping design as impractical to copy; asked whether the flat-roller design described above has any tipping component, and how long it is.[45]

The reply confirmed no tipping mechanism at all — just a rigid, and therefore heavy, platform, built so the back of the trailer lines up with the back of the boat when properly stowed; with the wheels at the water's edge, the trailer's own back end then sits over water deep enough to float the boat off. Without brakes, a loaded trailer would be liable to run away on the first slope encountered, though being able to drop the boat off the trailer onto the ground is useful for trailer maintenance. The hull itself is "so over designed" that damage to it while winching aboard is rarely a real concern; the winch itself should be sized several sizes beyond what would seem necessary — a 900 kg winch, whose gearing makes hauling the boat on easy, was recommended from experience.[46]

A neighbour's free, secondhand trailer, needing some work, was volunteered soon after; fitting it with a number of transverse rollers was the next step planned.[47]

## Trailering the Bolducs' way (microcruising.com)

An account of trailering [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) gives an independent, long-running real-world counterpart to the specifications and lessons above. Little Cruiser's shallow **9-inch draft** makes her easy to launch at any ramp, and she tows well behind a small four-cylinder car — a notably lighter tow vehicle than generally discussed above.[48][49]

Little Cruiser was routinely trailered from **Greensboro, North Carolina**, to **Key Largo, Florida** — a one- to two-day drive — before launching for a Bahamas crossing.[50] Underway, the mast **lowers** on its struts/tabernacle to clear low bridges, allowing passage underneath without waiting for a bridge opening.[49] Cross-link [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md).

A distinct practical figure covers the other end of the trailering process — not towing capacity, but time to get sailing once at the ramp: rigging a Paradox has been timed at about **one hour, car to boat**.[51]

## Tow-vehicle capacity: ballast carried in the car counts too

A distinct consideration from the trailer and boat weight already covered above is the **tow vehicle's own rated towing capacity**, which is not simply reduced by the trailer and boat alone: any ballast, gear, or crew carried inside the tow vehicle itself also counts against that vehicle's braking and suspension loads, and so against its towing capacity. Water or sand ballast, rather than fixed lead, is one way to keep a scaled-up boat's road-weight impact on the tow vehicle in check, since it can be added or drained as needed; keeping crew weight low and amidships, and choosing a low-aspect sail rig with quick reefing capability, were also recommended in the same context — raised in reply to a prospective builder who wanted a 120% Paradox but judged his own car unable to tow it (see [Building Paradox larger — a 2009 coda](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-2009-coda-two-further-declared-attempts-a-towing-weight-fix-and-the-enigma-460-alternative)).[52]

## An oversized general-purpose utility trailer, doing double duty

Most of the record above works one way — starting from a boat trailer and adapting it to carry a Paradox. The choice can be made from the other end instead: buy an **oversized general-purpose utility trailer** big enough to take the boat, so that it doubles as a boat trailer and is put to many other uses when not launching the boat.[53] One such rig in long-term use is a galvanised, mesh-sided box trailer carrying the hull directly on its bed, with a spare wheel stowed beneath the bow — a boat trailer only on the days it is needed as one, and an ordinary load-hauling trailer the rest of the year.[53]

The trade is straightforward. A flat-bedded utility trailer gives up the launching convenience a purpose-built boat trailer is designed around — the rollers, the tilt bed, and the low loaded height that keeps a hull's bottom close to the ramp (see [ground clearance for Gulf Coast ramps](#ground-clearance-for-gulf-coast-ramps) and [low-profile and retracting-trailer concepts](#low-profile-and-retracting-trailer-concepts)). What it buys back is that the trailer earns its keep year-round instead of standing idle between cruises, which suits an owner trailering a few times a season to distant grounds rather than launching weekly at a local ramp.[53]

## A 600-mile tow on an unbraked road trailer

A Paradox has been towed 600 miles round trip — from the East Coast of England to Falmouth and back — on an **unbraked** road trailer behind an ordinary Ford Mondeo family saloon, without difficulty.[54] The same tow vehicle towed four different trailer-sailers in total over twelve years, including through the hills of Devon and Cornwall, without ever feeling inadequate to the job.[55]

## Reversing without docking arms, and two launch/recovery procedures

A boat that sits well forward on her trailer can completely block the tow car's rear view while reversing, unlike a smaller, lighter boat that sits further back on hers — making it essential to line trailer and car up centrally at the top of a slipway before reversing, and to hold that line throughout, which is easier said than done.[56] Launching and retrieving single-handed is harder without **docking arms** fitted to the trailer: without them the job becomes a considerable handful, particularly if the slipway is steep and exposed to waves, more so with a transverse current running.[57] Guide posts fitted to a trailer let the whole retrieval be done single-handed instead: lower the trailer by hand nearly to the water's edge and chock it, then run a rope from the trailer's front support to the tow car's ball hitch to lower the trailer fully into the water so the boat floats free above it while being winched forward.[58]

With a short tow bar, the trailer has to be lowered into the water on a rope run from the car's tow ball, taking a single turn round the ball to control the descent as the car eases forward. An alternative procedure: chock the trailer's wheels near the waterline, unhook the trailer from the car, drive the car up the slipway, link car and trailer with a rope, drive forward a little to pull the chocks free, then reverse slowly until the boat floats clear, securing her by the painter before retrieving the empty trailer on the rope. A long tow bar simplifies the whole sequence down to reversing boat and trailer straight into the water until the boat floats off, then towing the trailer straight back out, still attached to the car.[56]

A pre-launch checklist from one changeover of ownership: check periodically that the trailer's lighting board is held only by turn screws that can vibrate loose underway (taping is worth doing); keep the boat trimmed bow-heavy for road travel and don't forget the bow lashing and the aft strop; remove the lighting board before launching; run a thick warp from the ball hitch for the extra depth needed to float the boat off; and chock the boat on the slipway to separate trailer from car only once there is enough bow weight aboard that she will not fall back onto her rudder.[59]

## Faith's trailer failure, and a rebuild from a scrapped van axle (July 2011)

A serious longitudinal crack was found at both ends of "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"'s trailer axle beam — a hollow galvanised square tube linking a pair of independent "indispension" suspension units — most likely caused by torque under the boat's own weight; the trailer, rated at 750 kg, was loaded close to that limit, and a welded steel-plate repair was judged only a stopgap given the thin metal and burnt galvanising at the weld. The "indispension" units themselves gave almost no springing at all: jacking the trailer frame and releasing it sharply produced barely any visible movement in the suspension arm, leaving a ride harsh enough to scatter the boat's own contents "as if a bomb has gone off," and judged likely to have contributed to the axle failure in the first place — their only real merit a quick, convenient bolt-on trailer build; in use on the road, "dreadful." A replacement was begun instead, built around a rear axle salvaged from a scrapped van: dropped below the hubs for a lower load height, rated for a ton so amply strong, and expected to ride softer — though the van's own spring spacing proved narrower than Paradox's beam, so the hull would not sit directly between the springs. Open questions raised for the rebuild: whether rollers or slippery-plastic-covered bunks work better for sliding the boat on and off without dunking the wheels and brakes, and whether a hinged, break-back trailer bed is worth building.[60]

The replacement trailer above was finished and photographed in December 2011: built mostly from ordinary steel angle rather than hollow box section, specifically to avoid trapping and rusting from sea water, with a tipping bed that lets the boat run down the rollers under gravity for launch or recovery even on a level, dry surface — useful for leaving the boat ashore at low tide, or simply for easier maintenance. The promised salvaged-van axle was duly fitted, riding on plain leaf springs rather than the harsh "indispension" units blamed for the original axle's crack, giving a noticeably smoother ride.[61]

A separate build's owner reported the identical wish-list from his own trailer, which works well except for reloading the boat on soft ground — a fix already planned — and offered to demonstrate its finer points in person.[62] The offer was taken up,[63] with an invitation to see the trailer in actual use at a planned sail from Ilfracombe across to Lundy the same weekend.[64]

The same flat-roller design drew further praise a few days later — it lets a boat be winched onto its trailer from a firm beach, leaving only the harder problem of recovering off a genuinely soft beach still unsolved, half-joked as worth patenting by whoever cracks it.[65] The reply agreed, but judged a soft-enough beach — the deep, gloopy mud found in Poole Harbour — would defeat any such solution short of a helicopter.[66] A cheap folding utility trailer (Harbor Freight, about $289, rated to 1,195 lb, foldable flat against a garage wall for storage, with aftermarket "buddy bearing" hubs suggested to waterproof it) was floated as a light-duty option, on a par with the [T-2000](#a-small-affordable-trailer-option-and-its-real-world-weight-limit) above for its light weight rating.[67] A further owner's own indispension-type suspension gave a markedly softer ride than the harsh, contents-scattering experience recorded above — loose items aboard stayed exactly where they had been left after a first trip, with no need to wedge anything down — raising the possibility that the rubber in these units simply hardens with age, harsher on an older set than a newer one.[68] Consistent with that theory, Faith's own several-years-old indispension units have visibly lowered the trailer and grown noticeably harder-riding over the years — a guess offered that the units are manufactured cheaply, probably overseas, with durability and quality a lower priority than cost.[69]

The same cheap Harbor Freight folding trailer above has one real drawback for a Paradox owner wanting to waterproof it: its hubs are automotive rather than trailer hubs, sourced by the manufacturer from an unnamed Chinese supplier, and no bearing-buddy product on the market has been found to fit them.[70] Where a factory bearing-buddy kit's own adaptor rings don't fit a replacement set of hubs — as happens after swapping suspension units — a workable fix is to saw the end off the new hubs' bearing caps and braze the buddies directly into them.[71]

A wider search of other Indispension-unit owners' own online accounts corroborated the aging-suspension theory above with widespread further reports of the same trouble, reinforcing the sense that the manufacturer's advertised load ratings run optimistic. The replacement trailer being built from the scrapped van axle is now specified as leaf-spring suspension with brakes that work in both directions, forward and reverse.[30]

## A swing-beam roller trailer for JoHanna (July 2011)

A **De Graaff SB700-2ADJ** swing-beam roller trailer, a standard commercial unit rated for a **580 kg** carrying capacity, was adapted by tying together each pair of adjacent rollers with steel studding and tube so they rotate as a single unit regardless of where the boat sits on the trailer, plus extra rollers added to the rear pair to help the boat self-align on retrieval. Loaded and unloaded three times without difficulty, including a single-handed recovery at Ridge Wharf, Poole Harbour, using only the winch and without dipping the hubs. Further work planned for the following winter: full-width supports carrying four pairs of rollers forward and six pairs aft.[72] Like Faith's and (per one owner's own review of the group's history) Zoë's, this trailer is unbraked; how close to its rated capacity it runs loaded for the road was left an open question.[73]

## Rebuilding and specifying a De Graaff trailer

A **De Graaff SB 730/2 ADJ** trailer, adapted with flat rubber rollers to spread the load and guide arms to keep the boat straight on retrieval in a side wind or current, is one specific rebuild on record for a Paradox — the tow bar had to be shortened to let the rig fit into a domestic garage, and the trailer's winch is rated to handle up to **1,102 lb**.[74] The same manufacturer's advice on coupling load: downward thrust on the coupling should be no more than **73 kg** and preferably not less than **50 kg** (30 kg judged possibly sufficient but not recommended), measured in practice with a bathroom scale; correct trim on the trailer depends on the boat's forward ballast and battery being aboard.[75] A trailer's axle, originally mounted too far forward for adequate tow-ball thrust, was moved **17 cm** rearward by loosening the four u-bolts securing the axle carriage; the rule of thumb given for correct hitch loading generally is about **10% of the combined weight** of trailer and boat.[76] A virtually brand-new De Graaff trailer of this kind, used only twice, cost markedly more than a comparable secondhand road trailer bought years earlier, consistent with the general escalation in trailer and build costs recorded over the intervening years (see [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md)).[77][78]

## Single-handed launching as the real reason not to skimp

Beyond load capacity, a trailer's practical ease of use matters in its own right: a trailer built well enough to make **single-handed launching easy** can be the difference between actually going sailing and staying home — a restatement of the overbuilding lesson above framed around motivation rather than just structural margin, and consistent with the single-handed launch/recovery difficulties already recorded (see [reversing without docking arms, and two launch/recovery procedures](#reversing-without-docking-arms-and-two-launchrecovery-procedures)).[79]

## A Pennsylvania newcomer's question, and an underrated trailer's real-world fix (October 2010)

A Pennsylvania newcomer asked broadly about trailer type and size, tow vehicle, road behavior, and launching technique.[80] The reply drew on "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)"'s own trailer, adapted from a trailer normally sold for light, fourteen-foot aluminum fishing boats and always rated for a lighter load than a loaded Paradox: load-rated tires and a lower-geared, higher-capacity winch were fitted, and retrieval is done simply by imitating other boaters' launch/retrieve technique at a home ramp only a quarter-mile away. "Little Jim"'s own purpose-built trailer (see [Glen Maxwell's trailer specifications](#glen-maxwells-trailer-specifications-and-a-loaded-height-target) and [Little Jim's actual trailered weight](#little-jims-actual-trailered-weight-and-two-contrasting-uk-trailer-setups) above) was singled out as the most excellent trailer design on record, if pricy.[81]

## Al Law's own launching-ramp trailer, and a return to the weight caution (October 2010)

The same exchange drew a direct reply restating the standing weight caution above: a Paradox weighs about 1,400 lb in sailing trim and still over 1,000 lb with the ballast removed, before the trailer's own weight is added on top.[82]

Al Law's own trailer — custom-designed after no commercial trailer met his needs, with photographs kept on his personal website — allows single-handed launch and recovery with little physical effort; the drawback is needing a proper slipway a car can reach. It rides docile on the road behind a 2-litre diesel car, needing a lot of clutch work only on the odd steep slipway. The controlling design requirement was keeping the trailer's own wheels dry: the trailer itself acts as a launching ramp, putting the boat into water deep enough to float, which puts considerable upward force on the towball — the tow-bar arrangement has to be strong enough to take it.[83]

## Designing and building your own trailer economically, rather than buying bespoke (2015)

A bespoke trailer for a Paradox is essential — a totally different job from a "normal" boat trailer, and expensive to have made professionally. Designing and building one at home is a more economical route; a previous "normal" trailer, by contrast, was "disastrous."[84]

## A modified jet-ski trailer (2015)

A **jet-ski trailer**, modified for A$800 plus a little more for local modification work, made a working Paradox trailer: the boat sits level under tow and handles well behind the car, with the **rear-most trailer beam removable** to allow launching in shallow water. Full build account: [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#materials-cost-and-a-modified-jet-ski-trailer).[85]

## A further 2015 restatement, and a comparison boat's empty weight

A prospective buyer weighing which vehicle to drive on a long trip to collect a secondhand Paradox — his own car's towing capacity limited to about 1,000 lb — asked in January 2015 for the boat's empty weight with no water ballast.[86] The reply restated the standing figure above: a car rated to pull 1,100 kg (2,424 lb) tows the fully loaded boat comfortably, and the loaded boat itself, without crew or water ballast, weighs around 1,000 lb; emptying the hull further sheds some of that, though the trailer's own weight then has to be added back on top — a 1,000 lb-only towing capacity would likely struggle unless the trailer itself is very light.[87] A non-Paradox comparison boat, a Cal21, was separately put at about 1,100 lb empty — a reasonable ballpark match.[88] A later reply on the same thread clarified that his boat name, given only in passing above, in fact names an Enigma 460 he already owns and has sailed rather than the Paradox he was buying — expecting the Paradox to prove even better again, for the same enclosed, protected style of sailing — and that it would still be at least four months before the new boat was in the water.[89]

## A further 2015 account: visibility and ramp-to-sailing time

A further firsthand account, from a 2,500-mile-plus tow to Florida made without incident, credits the narrow hull with a real trailering advantage: it stays visible past both rear-view mirrors, well clear of the hidden blind spot a full-width boat would create, and the boat's own windows let the main mirror see straight through her from behind. The same account puts a figure on the trailer-to-sailing turnaround: **8 to 12 minutes** from arrival at the ramp to actually sailing, depending mainly on how far the tow vehicle has to be walked back from the parking spot.[90]

## Dry-launching onto an exposed slipway at low tide

A deliberate alternative to launching straight into the water: drop the boat off the trailer onto a slipway left dry by a receding tide, then simply wait aboard — making coffee, reading a book — for the rising tide to float her off, rather than queuing at a slipway shared with paying customers and other boaters.[91] The technique only works where the drying stretch below the slipway is extensive enough — acres of mud, up to half a mile to navigable water, in one account — that no powerboater would want the ramp regardless, leaving it entirely uncontested until the water returns.[91]

A second, independent case turns a boatyard's own access-control system to the same advantage: where the slipway and storage compound can only be entered while staff are on site, launching while the tide is still out, unloading the boat, and securing the car and trailer in the compound before the water returns lets the crew sail off over the horizon by the time the yard locks up for the night, with no one else able to get a vehicle in or out until the following day.[92]

The technique does not transfer everywhere. Where slipways sit in marinas or deep harbours with only a modest tidal range, boats launch regardless of the state of the tide and a dry slipway is never on offer in the first place; and where a drying estuary's own bar crossing is dangerous, floating off at low water is not a safe option regardless.[93]

## A 2017 recurrence: a flatbed conversion, a restated roller design, and a carpeted-bunk build

A new builder's request for a trailer design drew three independent answers reflecting three different approaches.[94] One rides a small flatbed trailer that came with the boat, adapted with a 4x4 timber crosswise at both the front and rear of the bed to accommodate the hull's own curve and rocker, and carries spare tire assemblies as a matter of course.[95] A second restated the [roller design](#glen-maxwells-trailer-specifications-and-a-loaded-height-target) built years earlier and already documented above: a series of horizontal rollers plus a couple of vertical ones to keep the boat centered, simplified since by removing two forward horizontal rollers found to do little; over-engineered, but fifteen years old without significant problems, and able to launch and recover from a slipway, or onto the ground, without the wheels ever entering the water — restating two standing tips from the same experience, a winch several sizes bigger than seems necessary (this one rated at 950 kg) and a braked winch and trailer over the standard ratchet type, since manhandling is much easier braked.[96] A third builder, who had already built and converted Paradox trailers before, ties two 2x4s crosswise on the trailer about six feet apart, then lays two carpeted 8 ft 2x8s lengthwise above the trailer's own frames on top of the 2x4s; the boat's weight bends the longitudinal boards down until they touch the frame, carrying the hull along eight feet of its length on each side — where it is strongest. Less elaborate than the roller design above, but effective in his own experience.[97]

## A bow attachment point for the trailer winch: placement, and going without one

Not every boat carries the U-bolt or eye bolt typical at the bow for hoisting onto a trailer; without one, the trailer's winch cable can instead be hooked around the bow cleats, though this pulls the boat on at an angle — forcing the bow down under the trailer's rubber support block rather than pulling it in a straight line onto the V-shaped block — costing extra time getting the boat properly seated.[98]

One placement given in reply: about 14 in below the deck, at the stem's thickest point, chosen partly with an eye to what would happen taking a tow — but judged, in hindsight, too low, since it makes for a less horizontal pull onto the trailer; a bolt around 10 in below the deck was offered as the better choice if fitted again. The fitting in that builder's own experience is used only to secure the boat to the trailer, anchoring instead being done from the deck cleats, and the boat is floated onto the trailer rather than winched up.[99]

Three further replies gave their own numbers. One sets the bolt so the winch's pull is horizontal when the bow sits against the trailer's snubber, coming out to about 450mm below the deck on that boat — used solely for hauling onto the trailer, never for anchoring, which is done from the deck cleats instead.[100] A second builder gave the same caution independently: mounted too high, the bolt forces the bow down as the winch takes up, complicating the final approach onto the trailer.[101] A third places it level with the top of the winch's own coiled strap once the boat is fully hauled on.[102] The second builder, answering the original question afresh a week and a half later, gave his own bolt an actual figure this time: level with the boat's own trailer winch, set while bending plywood around the bow post during construction.[103]

See [breast hook — backing for a bow ring bolt](/components/breast-hook.md#backing-for-a-bow-ring-bolt) and [ground tackle — a stem U-bolt](/operations/ground-tackle.md#attaching-ground-tackle-at-the-bow-cleats-a-stem-u-bolt-and-chafe-protection) for the same fitting's anchoring role, including the observation that a single stem U-bolt can serve double duty for both trailer towing and ground tackle.

## Removing the boat from the trailer entirely, for storage or a beach launch (October 2018)

A separate question from launching at a ramp: whether a Paradox is ever taken bodily off her trailer while on the hard, for storage rather than for sailing. One owner, whose bunk-type trailer has a pivot and locking pin rather than rollers, wanted to store the boat close to ground level in a garage over winter and, with a possible future Everglades Challenge entry in mind — which requires launching self-supported from a beach above the high-tide line — also wanted a way to slide the boat backward off the trailer once the pivot pin is released, considering inflatable rollers rated to 2,000 lb to take the load on soft sand.[104]

Three answers gave three different ways of getting a boat off her trailer entirely. On a low, roller-based trailer, launching is simply pushing the boat forward off the trailer until she tips and her rudder touches the ground, then driving the tow vehicle forward to leave her behind; recovery reverses the sequence — rollers fitted at the trailer's rear run along the ground once the trailer itself is tipped up, its rear end is presented under the bow, and winching pulls the trailer in underneath the boat. A couple of boards, carried aboard, can be laid down for the rollers to run on if the ground is soft or too rough.[105] A second method calls in outside help: a neighbouring farmer's tractor lifted the boat straight up on two slings joined by a rope while the trailer was pulled out from under her and replaced with logs to sit on, then rolled her into the shop on the logs as rollers — done with the boat's full permanent ballast aboard throughout, without trouble; once inside, a jack raises each end in turn to fit support beams underneath for bottom work.[106] A third rolled the boat the same general way, using a long tow strap (a "snatch strap," in Australian usage) lifted with a chain block hung from a shed roof, the hull cushioned on thick woven plastic bags with a friend on hand to steady her — the same method once used to lift a steel beam with ordinary sailing pulleys and rope. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a bow U-bolt figure given, and Thor lifted off her trailer by chain block](/boats/thor.md#a-bow-u-bolt-figure-given-and-thor-lifted-off-her-trailer-by-chain-block-october-2018).[107]

A few days later the same question was pressed further, specifically as a beach-launch problem: wanting to put the boat ashore above the high-tide line on a Florida beach, unsure whether she could simply be pushed backward off the trailer once the pivot pin was released, with inflatable rollers again considered to take the load — noting that the EC300 event specifically requires a self-launch above the high-tide mark, from the beach.[108]

The reply gave practical technique: haul a boat down a beach stern-first, since the rudder becomes a liability going forward; failing any special equipment, "walking" the boat sideways works too — slow, but it gets there. The deeper point made in the same reply is that a trailer properly designed and built for the boat, rather than whichever trailer happened to be on hand, pays for itself in ease of handling: a purpose-built trailer lets its owner single-handedly launch and recover his own Paradox in about fifteen minutes, in his late 60s, without breaking a sweat — meaning he is more likely to go out for a short sail on a whim, and so ends up sailing more often as a result.[109]

Closing the exchange, considered an inflatable fender product (named only for reference, not as a recommendation) to help slide the boat backward off the trailer once the pivot pin is released, and separately learned of bearing-buddy hub protectors as a further trailer improvement worth adding.[110]

A further reply to the same request for a trailer photograph added two more details. Inflatable rollers have a second use beyond sliding a boat along the ground: laid flat and deflated under a boat already resting on the ground, then inflated, they can lift the whole hull clear of the ground rather than merely roll it. And bearing buddies, while agreed to be a genuinely good invention, were never fitted to this particular trailer, since its wheels never go into the water in the first place.[111]

The same exchange also turned toward the one person who had already solved exactly this problem: a suggestion was made to ask Matt Layden directly how he managed it when he won the Everglades Challenge in the original Paradox in 2003 — a race whose start required precisely the same unsupported beach launch above the high-tide mark. The suggestion turned out to be already tried: an inquiry passed on a month earlier through the event's own founder had drawn no reply, consistent with the designer occasionally being away on a challenge of his own.[112][113]

A further voice, drawing on firsthand WaterTribe race experience, put the practical shape of the problem plainly: competitors typically help each other get boats off their trailers at the start of a race like this, but getting the loaded boat from there into the water alone afterward is the real problem to solve, since a Paradox ballasted properly to the plans is very heavy.[114] That remark prompted a question about how much of that weight is water ballast that could simply be drained before a beach launch — answered, along with a further, non-water source of the boat's weight, at [boats/thor.md — a ballast-composition clarification](/boats/thor.md#a-ballast-composition-clarification-and-a-rogue-wave-account-restated-october-2018).

## Why trailering with the mast through the vent proved impractical

Matt Layden's plans offer stowing the mast fully inside the hull for trailering, passed through the aft vent port, as an alternative to leaving the assembled rig secured to the side-deck stowage rails. In practice this proved far from easy, and the stowed mast got in the way if the crew wanted to sleep aboard while the boat sat on the trailer — the reason the whole rig, mast included, is more often kept assembled and secured to the port side-deck stowage rails instead, trading a bulkier road load for a much faster rig set-up at the water.[115] By contrast, a different builder's practice stows the whole rig inside the hull specifically for the road, loading and retrieving it through a separate transom inspection hatch rather than the aft vent, and rigs the boat fully only on arrival at the launch site — see [components/deck.md — rigging at the launch site, and stowing the spars for the road](/components/deck.md#rigging-at-the-launch-site-and-stowing-the-spars-for-the-road) for the contrasting account. A further reason favours side-deck stowage specifically: it avoids re-threading the rig's control lines through the vent box each time the boat is rigged at the launch site, besides keeping the cabin interior clear for living aboard while under tow — a consideration judged worth allowing a very slightly longer mast to accommodate.[116]
