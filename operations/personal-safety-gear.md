# Personal safety gear and man-overboard preparedness

Distinct from the boat-handling storm tactics covered in [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md), this concerns the personal gear and habits a Paradox sailor carries on their own body and around the cockpit for safety at sea.

## Continuous life-jacket wear, a jackline tether, and a trailing rescue line

One owner wears a life jacket the whole time at sea; its collar, large enough to keep the head above water if unconscious, doubles day to day as a comfortable pillow, "so no bother at all."[1] In rough weather he ties himself in to a point inside the boat, and offshore trails a floating rope behind — bright yellow water-ski rope, chosen for staying on the surface and for very low drag — the same rig he had already described rigging for a 2017 night passage (see [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#a-camping-cruise-in-spencer-gulf-and-a-freak-wave-knockdown-february-march-2017)).[1]

## A survival beacon is a legal requirement for the boat, not just the sailor

By law a survival beacon must be carried on the boat itself. A second, small, unobtrusive personal beacon sewn into a life jacket is a useful supplement, but carrying only the personal unit does not appear to satisfy the requirement for the vessel to carry one — the law is understood to be firm on that point.[1]

## "Reef hard and reef early"

An old adage from gaff-rig sailing, read in a book on the type: in bad weather, reef hard and reef early. Leaving port already reefed rather than under full sail is the practice on any trip forecast windy or gusty.[1]

## The enclosed steering position, and general seamanship maxims

A protected, enclosed steering position does a lot to reduce fatigue and exposure to weather, keeping the skipper warm and safe through conditions that would wear a cockpit sailor down — one reason a Paradox continues to take care of herself with little fuss or input even after a long day out. A junk rig can be dropped in seconds into lazyjacks, out of harm's way, when trouble threatens. For positive buoyancy in place of foam, a number of plastic containers tied in place will serve, though foam is not objected to on principle — only judged, for one builder, too complicated to fit given his own skill level. General seamanship maxims offered alongside these specifics: stay out of tight situations, always try to keep open water around the boat, and plan ahead.[2]

The same message restated that another Paradox in Western Australia has been rolled over in breaking waves in shallow water, with a lot of damage, but was salvaged and repaired — the same incident already on record in full at [boats/small-fry.md](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source).[2]

## A skeptical physics critique of the trailing rescue line, and a counter-anecdote

The trailing floating rescue line above drew a worked, skeptical critique of whether it would actually work as a man-overboard recovery aid. Allowing about ten seconds to recover from cold-water shock and another ten to orient and start swimming toward the rope, a boat making 4 knots (2.06 m/s) will already have sailed about 40 metres before the swimmer is moving toward it at all — so the trailing line would need to run at least 100 m to be within reach. Grabbing a rope moving at 4 knots with wet hands, then hauling hand over hand against a 4-knot wall of water in the face for the same 40 to 100 metres, was judged a serious physical task likely to leave the hands raw and bleeding well before the climb back aboard. The critique was offered as untested and testable — proposing that anyone willing could check the grab-and-haul portion on dry land, by having a car tow a rope at 4 knots (4.6 mph) to grab bare-handed — with an invitation to report back if anyone ever actually tried the full recovery at sea.[3] The invitation was answered directly: tested repeatedly, the actual technique for climbing back aboard on a trailing line is to lie on the back while pulling on the line overhead, which keeps the face out of the water, favors lifting over diving, and lets the stronger arm muscles work through straight wrists rather than bent ones — arrived at on the reasoning that testing a technique in advance means fewer surprises when it actually matters.[4]

A counter-anecdote, from well outside Paradox sailing, offered a milder data point: as children sailing 25 ft daysailers in Miami, throwing a line astern and being towed and pulled back in was a routine game, costing at most a lost swimsuit bottom but never a bloodied hand, and was "relatively easy for a moderately fit person" even without the boat slowing down. The comparison is inexact — a slow, controlled recreational tow with no cold-water shock and no urgency is not a genuine offshore man-overboard emergency — so it qualifies rather than resolves the critique above.[5]

A further, structural objection to the whole man-overboard scenario points to a small boat's own dynamics rather than to the swimmer's: the weight shift as a solo skipper went overboard would itself turn the boat upwind and slow her to roughly 1 knot rather than the assumed 4, even with the tiller left locked, and the drag of a body holding the trailing rope would on its own be enough to bring the boat close to a stop.[6] A trailing line not made off on the boat's centerline compounds the effect, since any change of speed it induces creates its own turning moment — reinforcing, rather than contradicting, the case above for acting quickly once in the water.[7]

## A personal man-overboard kit

One approach to personal gear worn or carried specifically for going overboard alone offshore: a climbing harness clipped to a line secured to the boat, tempting though it is to unclip if something needs doing quickly; a bicycle helmet; an EPIRB; a VHF radio, not yet used in practice; and two cellphones loaded with chart software, doubling as a backup compass — a tiller autopilot is still being weighed.[6]

## Alcohol as a factor in falling overboard

A common circumstance behind falling overboard, relayed from a contact in search and rescue off tropical Queensland waters: most who go overboard are men, and most later found had alcohol in their system, the presumed pattern being a moment's distraction at the rail, tipsy, while under way. The practice this suggests: no drinking aboard at all, short of a cruise ship.[7]

## Two early mistakes: a hauling strain, and an unsecured rudder

The first time under real pressure in his own Paradox, hauled the sail so violently that it took a month for his own back muscles to heal — an overreaction to a furl that, in truth, was not needed so quickly, nerves getting the better of him. A separate early lesson concerned the rudder: raised in shallow water for an overnight stop but not properly tied off, it later swung down full tilt onto the firm sandy bottom just as a foot went underneath it, breaking a toe and leaving a limp for weeks. Both are offered as evidence that pressure teaches the hardest lessons fastest.[8]

## Lifelines and pulpits: a permanent bow-to-transom line instead

On a boat sailed entirely from inside the cabin, the rare occasion the sailor must actually go out on deck — for docking, for tying up, or for loading onto the trailer — is felt as far more exposed than sailing itself, since the cockpit-and-cabin arrangement otherwise keeps the sailor secure the whole time under way.[9] No Paradox has been reported fitted with lifelines or a pulpit in the traditional yacht sense; the design's whole arrangement is instead read as built to contain the sailor within the cockpit and cabin rather than send them onto an open deck at all.[10]

The alternative two builders independently arrived at is a light permanent line run each side from bow to transom (or bow to stern). It doubles as an instant bow rope for tying up to a pontoon, lets the anchor be set from the bow without leaving the cockpit, and — left with enough slack — gives a harness a point to clip to on the rare occasion the deck must be worked underway; over some twenty years it has never actually had to serve that last purpose.[11][10]

## Technique for going forward, rather than hardware

Working the foredeck with the sail hoisted is avoided outright — a swinging boom is reckoned as likely as anything to put a sailor over the side — so the practice is to go forward only with the sail down, using the mast as the main handhold once there.[11][10] Where going forward is unavoidable, the technique is to stay on the boat's centerline, lie down to keep weight low, keep the boom clear with the sail struck, brace against the mast, wear a life jacket, and trail a rescue line — judging that knowing when it is safe to go forward matters more than any single piece of gear, and preferring, where conditions allow, to sail close to shore and step off beside the boat to deal with a problem rather than leave the cockpit underway.[10]

A further practice pairs the trailing floating line above with a line tied to the life jacket itself whenever the hatch is left at sea, so a sailor out of the cabin is already tethered; going overboard is reckoned to broach the boat almost immediately, keeping her from getting far in the time it takes to swim for the trailing line. Climbing back aboard in rough water wearing a bulky life jacket is acknowledged as genuinely hard, worth practicing from calm water in advance rather than for the first time in an emergency — over five years of sailing, this particular practice was never actually tested by a real fall.[12]
