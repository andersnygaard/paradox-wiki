# Hull speed — the displacement-speed formula and real-world GPS figures

## The classic displacement-hull formula

The [Paradox](/paradox.md)'s theoretical hull speed is given by the standard displacement-hull formula:

> hull speed (kt) = **1.3 × √(waterline length in feet)**

Applied to a Paradox's roughly **13 ft 5 in waterline** (√13.663 ≈ 3.663), this works out to **1.3 × 3.663 ≈ 4.76 kt (about 5.4 mph)** — the conventionally "expected" maximum displacement speed for a hull of that waterline length.[1]

The formula's real-world relevance is limited, however: **it is not unusual for sharpies to sail much faster** than this theoretical figure — a hull-form observation consistent with the Paradox's flat-bottomed, chine-runner design (see [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)).[1]

## A real GPS speed data point, from a Clearwater, FL trip

Logged performance data from a passage toward **Clearwater, FL**, sailing **about 6 miles offshore** on a **broad reach**, in **12-15 kt winds** and **2-3 ft seas**, under full sail, gives a concrete real-world figure:[1]

- **GPS readings of 5.9-6.1 kt** sustained for about **two hours**, until the wind dropped.
- A **maximum recorded speed of 6.3 kt** for that leg — roughly **30% above** the theoretical 4.76 kt hull speed given by the formula above.
- Hardening up **close-hauled** into the same seas ("smashing into the seas"), the best speed attainable dropped to **4.7 kt** — almost exactly matching the theoretical hull-speed figure, illustrating how much of the "excess" broad-reach speed comes from sailing angle and sea state rather than the hull alone.[1]

This is "not too shabby for a 13'5" water line length" — a rare, concretely logged example (GPS-timestamped, not just estimated) of the Paradox exceeding its nominal displacement hull speed under favorable conditions, complementing the boat's other recorded performance data (see [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) and [pointing and tacking-angle expectations](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md)).[1]

## A motoring speed data point: "Whisper"

A separate, concrete **motoring** speed data point comes from a weekend aboard "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" with no wind to sail. Running a **4 hp four-stroke outboard** — "too much for a Paradox" — the GPS-logged curve was a little over **2 kt at just above idle**, and about **5 kt at just under 1/4 throttle**, "a good cruising speed, very comfortable." **6 kt** was attainable but meant the hull was "pushing a lot of water." This is the only recorded GPS-logged motoring (as opposed to sailing) speed curve for a Paradox, and a useful complement to the sailing-speed figures below.[1a] Notably, 5 kt under power here matches almost exactly the "~5 kt under power" figure for the Layden-design family (below), despite the two data points coming from independent boats and owners.

## A two-day comparison against a Bermuda-rigged dinghy

A two-day passage sailed in company with a Whiting 16 — a modern, wide-transomed Bermudan-rigged fibreglass dinghy with a 2.4 m beam against a Paradox's 1.23 m — gives a rare direct, if informal, point-of-sail comparison, in light air never exceeding 5 kt on either day. Downwind the Paradox drew away, the dinghy falling far enough behind to need a tow, given only on condition of leaving plenty of scope, since a Paradox under way ploughing into the back of another boat would give it a considerable fright; upwind the dinghy had the clear advantage; on a beam reach the Paradox again seemed to have the edge, if less decisively on the second day. Becalmed on the return leg, the Paradox ghosted along at **0.4 kt** while the dinghy sat motionless and had to motor home, and averaged **0.7 kt** over the whole becalmed return — enough of a gap that the dinghy's own sailor, arriving later under power, was surprised how quickly the Paradox had got there under sail alone. By the encounter's end, having looked over the Paradox's interior, he was won over enough to ask, half-joking, whether he would like to swap boats; the answer was no.[6] GoPro footage recorded during the encounter survives.[7]

The same encounter prompted a fellow owner to weigh a sprit rig's compactness against a Bermuda conversion for his own boat; see [boats/thor.md — a last look at the inherited Bermuda rig](/boats/thor.md#a-four-day-passage-and-a-last-look-at-the-inherited-bermuda-rig-april-2019).

## Real-world speed figures from the Bolducs (microcruising.com)

Repeated, concrete GPS/log speed figures for [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) corroborate and extend the 4.76-kt theoretical figure and Clearwater GPS readings above. Its practical hull speed is consistently cited as about **5 knots** — for example, running downwind during the 40-knot Chub Cay-to-Bimini gale, "the boat did hull speed down wind (5 knots), and this was with our last reef tied in!"[2] An independent *Cruising World* account likewise gives an average sailing speed of **5 knots**, "waiting for a weather window."[3]

The figure breaks down further: **up to 4.8 knots** upwind "in the right conditions," with a typical passage average of about **4.5 knots under sail** and **5 knots under power**.[4] In big following seas she can briefly exceed hull speed, surfing to **8-9 knots** in short bursts (see [sailing characteristics](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md)).[5] A concrete windward data point: a 26-mile beat from Morgan's Bluff, Andros to West Bay, New Providence, in a steady 22-27 knots of wind and 8-ft seas in the Tongue of the Ocean, saw Little Cruiser "average over 4 knots" while pinching to windward the entire way.[5] See also [sailing characteristics](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md).
