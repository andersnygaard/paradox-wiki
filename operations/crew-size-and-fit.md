# Crew size and cabin fit ("wearing the boat")

Because the [Paradox](/paradox.md) is sailed single-handed from inside a small **enclosed cabin**, how a given sailor's **height and weight** fit the boat comfortably is a distinct question from the boat's general [seaworthiness and comfort](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#open-newcomer-questions-cabin-comfort-capacity-and-perceived-safety) — this concept is specifically about **body-size ergonomics**. The phrase "**wearing the boat**" recurs as shorthand for a Paradox that fits a particular sailor snugly, in the way a kayak "fits" its paddler — a self-deprecating coinage joking that at his size a big sailor "would be wearing the boat," while still judging the design had "all else" he wanted.[1] The phrase is "a very good description of sailing in these little boats," the sensation comparable to a sea kayak, and being aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) is like being in a car: in rough conditions "you really don't get thrown about much like in a large boat," because the enclosed, form-fitting cabin holds the sailor in place.[2]

## A cardboard mockup test

A **cardboard mockup of the cabin**, built at home to check size and utilisation before committing to plywood, is the standard way to test fit before building — as demonstrated while assembling [Walden](/boats/walden.md). At **6 ft tall and 210 lb**, the space proved **fine** across several "evolutions" — turning around, getting out of a sleeping bag, sitting with knees against the side bins. The one concern was **headroom with the hatch closed**: even with very short hair, **one or two more inches** of height there would help.[3]

An open fit-out question from the same mockup: whether the space outside the water tanks — below the floorboards and side bins, **between bulkhead #2 and bulkhead #3** — is where the lead ballast pigs go, and/or whether a smaller gel-cell battery could fit there on each side instead of one larger battery in the storeroom.[3] This is a distinct compartment from the bin panels already documented between bulkheads #1 and #2 (see [interior bins and hinged floor](/components/interior.md)) and from the bottom-of-hull [ballast stowage](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) already described.

Being physically containable is not the same as being **safe to sail alone** at a given size: a sailor about as tall (6 ft) but heavier than the mockup above had been recommended against the Paradox for someone his size by others, and wanted confirmation the boat was genuinely safe for him, not merely that he could fit inside it.[4]

## A builder concludes the boat is too small for him

At **6 ft 2 in and 240 lb**, one builder, after his own cardboard mock-up, concluded he was **"too big, too old, and too stiff"** to maneuver inside — citing sitting bolt upright with straight legs and belly-crawling forward (when needed) as particularly difficult, despite liking the boat otherwise. The stated **personal minimum** for a boat of this type: **48 in of sitting headroom** and **72 in (6 ft) of beam** — noting a Paradox-like boat built to those minimums would still be achievable, i.e. this was a call for a **stretched or widened variant**, not a rejection of the concept (compare [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md), which addresses lengthening but not beaminess).[5]

## Pete Hodges proposes concrete modifications for a taller sailor

A taller sailor (6' 3"), measuring his own requirement against a wall mock-up, worked through the same headroom problem with real numbers instead of a size verdict: the cabin's roughly **36 in** of height at the rear (roof to sole) was only barely workable sitting on a 3 in phone book, and **39 in** was judged comfortable — **3 more inches** than stock.[6] Rather than treat the boat as simply too small, several concrete ways to find those inches without a full redesign were worked out:

- **Enlarge the whole boat ~5%** — rejected as impractical on reflection, because the **bulkheads and stem are not vertical**, so a uniform enlargement would require full lofting or trigonometry rather than a simple percentage bump, risking cutting mistakes. (A simpler 10% uniform-scaling answer addresses this concern directly — see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).)
- **Lower the sole by 1 in** — trades away some water-ballast volume and enough storage height that "many can goods would no longer fit standing up."
- **Add steel-strap ballast on the keel exterior** to make up for the lost water ballast: multiple **3/16 in × 5/8 in steel straps**, bent to the keel's shape and run lengthwise along the outside of the bottom, then **epoxy-coated and faired** — putting substitute weight as low as possible while also protecting the bottom.
- **Raise the cabin roof** — about 1 in at the front, 2 in at the back — increasing the roof's rake but only trivially adding weight (more windage is the trade-off).
- **Raise the mast/boom height by up to 1 in** without unduly increasing broach risk, "especially if you put some steel on the outside of the bottom of the hull" (i.e. paired with the steel-strap ballast idea above), which would let the cabin roof rise a further inch fore and aft. The mast could not simply be lengthened without losing its ability to stow inside the boat, and sometimes needs to be shortened to compensate for an anchor light fitted at the top (see [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md)).

Combining all of the above gives a possible headroom gain of **3 in at the front and 4 in at the back** — enough to reach the 39 in target — with the acknowledgment that "this note is going to drive the purists crazy."[6] This modification approach sits alongside the simpler uniform-10%-scaling answer described at [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md).

## A 2014 exchange: the boom, mast, and hatch consequences of raising the cabin

A prospective builder needing **37 in** to sit upright with "proper" posture asked how much could be added to the cabin top's overall height, inclined otherwise to build as close to the stock design as possible rather than adopt a scaled-up variant.[140] Raising the cabin has an immediate, narrow mechanical consequence at one specific point: the boom must be free to rotate about the tack-strut deck fitting at the cabin's forward corner, and it is that corner — not the cabin top generally — that fouls when the roof is raised there, a problem added deck camber does not sidestep.[141][142][143] A raised roof also changes the sailor's habitual sitting height: at the stock height hair only brushes the roof sitting bolt upright, never while actually under way (typically reclined), but a raised roof risks bringing the head into range of the boom on an unexpected gybe.[143] Lowering the cabin sole instead of raising the roof carries no obvious drawback of its own — the lost water-ballast volume can simply be replaced with lead — and a folding-seat technique already used for comfort gives a better backrest at no extra height cost: notching the cleats that support the seat so its folded-up back edge drops into the notch gives an instant sloping backrest, more comfortable than the vertical bulkhead.[143]

Raising any part of the cabin by 3 to 4 in carries a second, wider consequence: the boom's topping-lift strop must lengthen by the same amount or the boom fouls the hatch; a longer strop in turn throws the boom out of parallel with the doghouse and forces the mast itself longer, which then no longer fits inside the hull for trailering without further modification — "a huge number of interdependencies in this design," most such changes judged not for the better, with the designer's own dimensions trusted as already worked out.[144]

One owner had already tested a version of the change in practice: **50 mm** added to the hull height throughout, the cabin top consequently 50 mm taller as well, with no structural issues found; a further 50 mm added specifically to the cabin top for extra headroom would leave it only a few millimetres narrower on top, since the new pieces are sized from the work already in place — an acceptable trade against the added windage, immaterial on a moderate-displacement cruising boat. Domes have been seen fitted with some success on other boats; a custom acrylic dome, ordered some years earlier, proved "surprisingly inexpensive."[145]

A further proposal worked several of these dimensions together rather than singly: **1 in off the cabin sole, 1 in added to the sheer, and 1 in added to the cabin top**, for **3 in** gained overall, keeping the rig's working geometry unchanged by raising the sail's center of effort the same **2 in** the roof rises — adding 2 in to the mast and mounting the furling arm on a 1 in block above the deck.[146] The proposal draws additional plywood for the larger side panels and taller bulkheads it implies, and the same mast-length trailering problem already noted above — a 2 in longer mast no longer fitting inside the hull without further modification; whether the extra height would actually be used is doubted on the same grounds as above, since a comfortable sitting position on the cabin sole has the body sloping backward rather than sitting bolt upright.[147]

Answering that same closing wonder about "some big guys out there," a fellow owner's own testimony already on record — 6 ft 4 in and 220 lb, finding the cabin "MUCH MUCH bigger inside than anyone would ever imagine" and having spent a night aboard comfortably (see [Scout](/boats/scout.md#citations)) — was pointed to as evidence that no one built that large has reported struggling with headroom in practice.[148]

A two-month lull in the thread closed with a proposal that sidesteps the lengthened-mast/no-longer-fits-inside problem raised above: raise the **mast step** itself by an inch or two instead, gaining rig height directly at the partner rather than through a longer spar, at the cost of a little extra load on the partner — offset, in this instance, by a sheer also raised an inch. The same **2 in** figure applied instead to the mast's own length reopened the standing trailering-clearance question: how much clearance a stock, completed Paradox actually leaves for stowing the mast inside, and whether navigation lights need removing for the stow, including which lights other owners have been pleased with.[151] The mast-step proposal drew a caution against it: the mast already has insufficient bury as designed, so raising the step further would only reduce it more.[152] On stowage and lighting, cabin space for the mast is fairly tight; a low-profile running light, built at home, was needed since commercial units were all too tall to fit, and a top-hat-shaped hatch cover is one way to accommodate a mast that ends up protruding past the transom.[153]

## A real two-up weekend: Glen and Nancy at Sebastian, FL

The first concrete two-person cruise on record aboard a Paradox: a couple (5'9"/190 lb and 5'6"/185 lb) spent a weekend sailing and camping at Sebastian, FL — "a beautiful place to sail around," with many clean, uninhabited spoil islands to go ashore on — the first time "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" had carried two people, and it went well. With two small dogs also along, sleeping two aboard was judged "too much" for the cabin, so the crew pitched a tent ashore to sleep, while still doing all their cooking aboard the boat. Weather was favorable throughout: 80s°F by day, cool nights, and no biting insects.[7]

## A real-world headroom data point: "Whisper"

The stock cabin's headroom as actually built, rather than as mocked up or drawn, is confirmed by the secondhand Paradox "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)": at **5 ft 11 in**, there is **"just enough height to sit up straight with a little to spare."** The cabin looks slightly different in photos because of "Whisper"'s **removable rear window**, which doubles as "a great perch in good weather."[8] This is a rare direct confirmation — from an actual as-built boat rather than a mockup or the plans — that the stock cabin accommodates a sailor just under 6 ft with a small margin, consistent with (if slightly more generous than) the 6 ft/210 lb mockup finding above.

## Glen Maxwell's measured cabin dimensions

Two exact figures from an as-built "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)": from the top of the **bench seat** to the underside of the closed hatch, **67 cm (26.8 in)**; from the **cabin sole** to the same point, **91 cm (36.4 in)**. These are the most precise recorded cabin dimensions from an as-built boat, complementing "Whisper"'s sit-up-straight data point above with hard numbers rather than a sailor-height comparison.[9]

A thin (180 lb), 6 ft 4 in prospective builder's question about the sleeping area's exact length drew two further as-built data points confirming the plans' dimensions can be nudged without altering the boat's lines.[10] One boat, "[Jill](/boats/jill.md)", gained a full **36 in (91.5 cm)** of sleeping length — as much as could be taken without interfering with the sail rig — with the caution that any extra length gained this way must still be trimmed to fit the bulkhead correctly; failing that, a tall sailor can simply extend the feet into the forward compartment while sleeping.[11] On another boat, "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)", built as close to the plans as possible, the clearance under the closed hatch measured **930 mm (about 36.5 in)**, and the same feet-in-the-forward-compartment trick works when a sleeper shifts down the bed; the roof could go up a little, or the floor down, if more clearance were ever wanted.[12]

## Pilot weight, height, and stability at stock length

**Weight is not a limiting factor** at stock length: the boat carries a couple of hundred pounds of removable lead ballast that can be reduced to compensate for a heavier sailor, so there is no fixed "max pilot weight" as such.[13] Height is bounded instead by the cabin's fixed interior geometry — sitting headroom of **860 mm (about 34 in)** and a stock sleeping length of **1.8 m (about 5 ft 11 in)**, extendable by a further **0.8 m (about 31 in)** with a minor modification (the same bulkhead-trimming approach already described above under the measured cabin dimensions).[14]

A **6 ft 4 in, 280 lb** prospective builder who had already ordered the plans reported being told separately that the stock-length boat would be **unstable** for someone that size — a stability caution distinct from the simple fits-inside-the-cabin question, and not further explained or quantified in the archive.[15]

The caution was disputed directly, with a website "Stability" page — showing a 180 lb sailor struggling to capsize "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" by hand — as evidence the hull itself is not the limiting factor.[16] The likely source of the warning clarified it was never really about **stability**: a brother built to the same 6'4"/280 lb dimensions raised the doubt that there was room for a man that size to move around comfortably with the cabin hatch shut, independent of whether the hull could support or balance his weight. Before the cabin went on "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)," that brother had seen the bare hull and likened it to one of his own shoes: "I'd need two of those. Do they come in a left and right?"[17] A further distinction is drawn between raw mass and how it is carried: at 6'4"/280 lb a sailor is "close to the comfortable limit" on mass but may be saved by being short, whereas a solo sailor near 280 lb loses the option other larger crews have had of splitting that mass into two people who can counterbalance side to side.[18]

The point that [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) had himself sailed two-up on several occasions was raised alongside a benchmark for how little boat a determined solo sailor actually needs: **Hugo Vihlen**'s solo Atlantic crossing in *Father's Day* — 105 days aboard a boat just 5 ft 4 in long.[19] A second, closer benchmark followed: **Stephen Ladd** spent three years cruising a boat "a little shorter than a Paradox."[20] Ladd, seen firsthand at the Port Townsend Wooden Boat Festival, is about 5 ft 6 in and medium-built, and his boat — shallow draft, rounded chines, only a token keel if any, unpainted fiberglass — looked deliberately rough, in keeping with his account of traveling simply and without room for much else. A 6 ft 1 in, 250 lb sailor judged he would fit in Ladd's boat but "feel like I was in a coffin," and wondered whether this was a case for a **"Big Paradox," roughly 20% larger** — an independent, later echo of the already-circulated "Big Paradox" concept (see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)).[21]

A further technical answer for accommodating longer sailors reaffirms the same bulkhead-trimming fix described above under the measured cabin dimensions: **dropping the floor between bulkheads 1 and 2** to the level of the main floor lets a longer sailor lie down without difficulty, though turning over without hitting fittings such as a bulkhead-mounted kettle remains the harder problem regardless of floor height. Against a stretched "Big Paradox," one preference ran the other direction — toward a **reduced Madrigal**-style design instead, with a full-length aft cabin and a lug rig, possibly with a mizzen.[22] The thread closed with encouragement to pursue the Big Paradox idea and report back on the result.[23]

Independent corroboration of the stability claim came from an hour-long test sail aboard "Little Jim": at **6 ft, 260 lb**, a sailor found himself comfortable both sitting cross-legged on the cabin floor and in the helmsman's seat, judged the 3-ft-wide floor wide enough to sleep on slightly on the diagonal (with the option of extending the floor platform forward of the mast, or simply bracing the feet against something), and — despite the sail taking place in calm water and light wind — described boarding the boat as feeling "like stepping onto a pontoon" compared with any other 14 ft boat, crediting the hull's weight and enclosed design with a feeling of security judged to extend to rougher conditions. The control layout also drew praise, with every line falling to hand while facing forward.[24]

## Fitting a couple aboard a stock or slightly enlarged Paradox

A recurring question from prospective builders planning to sail two-up: how a Paradox handles a couple of noticeably different sizes, both underway and sleeping aboard, given that most photos and reports on record show the boat sailed solo. ## Reference sailor heights and weights, and a two-up alternative: the Bolger Micro

A large sailor comparing a 10% and a 20% enlargement judged the 10% step would build a boat about [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own size, and asked for reference height-and-weight figures from other Paradox owners, giving his own as 6'4" and 270 lb.[27] Photos in the group's Interior files album were pointed to as the visual answer, alongside a concrete figure: [Glen Maxwell](/people/glen-maxwell.md), who sails Paradox solo only, is 5'9" and 185 lb.[28] A sailor of about the same size as Glen Maxwell, but who sails with a crew rather than solo, judged the stock boat too small for that purpose and chose a **Bolger Micro** instead, which has two 7 ft berths.[28] Weighing the same tradeoff, the sailor who raised the original height-and-weight question was separately working out buying a completed secondhand Micro nearby — if that purchase went through, the plan was to keep a Paradox for sailing solo and use the Micro whenever taking others along.[29] Two more owners gave their own figures for comparison: one at 5'8" and 149 lb, for whom the stock boat is "absolutely right"; another, sailing [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md), at 5'6" and 180 lb, for whom Paradox "fits...like a glove," though squeezing right to the fit's limit is best done in quiet conditions.[30][31]

Checking back more than half a year later, the same large sailor restated his plan to build stock-length after all rather than wait for a scaled-up version, choosing instead to close the size gap by reducing his own weight — a third figure for himself, **260 lb**, down from the 270 lb given above.[32]

A further reference point came from a builder nearing completion of his own boat: **5 ft 11 in and 205 lb**, finding her not small at all.[33] An established owner sailing his finished boat regularly gave his own figures as **5'10" and 160 lb**, finding the stock size suited him without any changes needed, but suggesting sailors over 6 ft think twice — see [Raffles — sailing record and an owner's assessment](/boats/raffles.md#sailing-record-and-an-owners-assessment).[34]

## A further height/weight question, answered (December 2009)

A newcomer at **6 ft 3 in and 220 lb** asked whether he would comfortably fit the Paradox without substantial alterations, inviting other tall or large sailors to share their own experience.[35] Two replies pointed to alternatives if the stock boat proves too tight: the group's own file-section article on a 20% [larger Paradox](/design/larger-paradox.md) enlargement, already worked out with full dimensions;[36] and Jim Michalak's **Robbsboat**, a Paradox "clone" sharing her lines in a slightly bigger 16 x 4 ft package whose main interior room is 4 x 8 ft with no shelves — substantially roomier than Paradox's own interior, "much like the Enigma460 is today" — though judged a plainer boat, one [chine runners](/chine-runners/index.md) could bring closer to Paradox's own performance on the water (see [Jim Michalak's Robbsboat, and Phil Bolger redesigns](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#jim-michalaks-robbsboat-and-phil-bolger-redesigns)).[37] A third, more direct answer held that height alone is unlikely to be the problem: if the sleeping space proves too short, the opening through to the forward storage compartment can be leveled with the cabin sole so the sleeper's feet extend through it — the same fix already reported [above](#pilot-weight-height-and-stability-at-stock-length), here independently confirmed by a sailor who had made the identical modification to his own boat, "Little Jim," for unrelated reasons. Suppleness matters more than raw size — in bad weather, sitting and moving about inside may call for some contortions — and no owner has been heard to complain the cabin is too cramped; at **5'6"/180 lb**, that same sailor finds it "quite roomy."[38]

## Extended two-up cruising — undocumented

Whether two people can travel aboard together on **longer trips**, in terms of **space**, is a distinct, more specific question than the general "wearing the boat" ergonomics discussion above. It narrows the existing two-up data point from [the Sebastian, FL weekend](#a-real-two-up-weekend-glen-and-nancy-at-sebastian-fl) (a single weekend, sleeping ashore in a tent) to the harder question of **extended, multi-day** two-person cruising entirely aboard — a case not demonstrated on record. The question drew only good-natured joking about needing to know one's companion "really well" and pets being impractical company, with no substantive answer; the underlying space/logistics question remains open.[39][40][41][42]

## Takeaways

- The stock Paradox comfortably fits at least one sailor of **6 ft / 210 lb** (a cardboard mockup finding), with headroom the tightest margin. A 6 ft / 260 lb non-owner's test sail aboard "Little Jim" independently corroborated the hull's stability and comfort for a sailor of that size.
- Two adults of ordinary stature (5'6"-5'9", 185-190 lb) cruised together successfully for a weekend, cooking aboard while sleeping ashore (the Sebastian trip, above).
- At least one larger sailor (**6 ft 2 in / 240 lb**) judged the stock cabin **too small** to maneuver in safely, and named concrete minimums (48 in sitting headroom, 72 in beam) that would work for him — a data point for anyone considering a stretched/widened Paradox derivative.
- A third sailor (**6 ft 3 in**) proposed a detailed set of targeted modifications (sole-lowering with steel-strap replacement ballast, cabin-roof raising, slight mast-height increase) to gain headroom without a full uniform stretch — an alternative approach to the [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) uniform-scaling answer.
- Raising the cabin top has real mechanical costs elsewhere in the boat — a longer boom strop, a longer mast that may no longer stow inside the hull for trailering, and a higher risk of head/boom contact on an unexpected gybe — though at least one owner raised the whole structure **50 mm** with no structural issues (see [a 2014 exchange: the boom, mast, and hatch consequences of raising the cabin](#a-2014-exchange-the-boom-mast-and-hatch-consequences-of-raising-the-cabin)).
- No design response reconciling these individual proposals into an adopted "big-sailor" variant exists; this sits alongside the still-open [newcomer FAQ](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#open-newcomer-questions-cabin-comfort-capacity-and-perceived-safety) questions about cabin comfort.

## Designed for one, room for a guest — the Bolducs' two-up reality (microcruising.com)

A firsthand account of the design intent matches the picture above directly: [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s boats were "originally designed as solo boats with room for the occasional guest." The owners who cruise [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) two-up as a matter of routine are both small in stature — about 5'5" and smaller still — a real factor in how comfortably two people fit the cabin, consistent with the body-size discussion above.[43]

Little Cruiser can carry **up to two months' stores for two people**. Even so, the one improvement its owners would make to the design is to **lengthen the boat a few feet to give a separate sleeping area**, since the cabin floor doubles as the bed — a real-world, long-term-cruising counterpart to the stretched/widened variant discussion above.[44] Outside press coverage independently confirmed the same two-up cruising practice, with two months' provisions and a pet parrot aboard.[45] See also [Mindy Bolduc](/people/bolduc/mindy.md) and [provisioning and water](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md).

## "Size matters?" — a 6'4" newcomer, and David Beard's practical fix

A **6 ft 4 in** sailor who bought the plans without first checking his fit found only afterward that he was taller than the berth (just over 6 ft) and that sitting headroom, too, was short. Opening up the bottom of the bulkhead #2 hatchway and raising the cabin roof were both considered and set aside, on the reasoning that enlarging the cabin isn't like "letting out a pair of pants" and risks compromising the design's integrity — "Paradox's compactness is part of its beauty."[46] This predicament — too tall for the stock boat but reluctant to alter it — sits between the two "too big" verdicts already on record above: the outright rejection at 6'2"/240 lb, and the detailed modification proposal at 6'3".

A practical fix comes from direct experience aboard "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)": cutting a little out of the **bulkhead #2 hatchway** and **lowering the top of the floor storage bin** between bulkheads #1 and #2 (see [interior bin panels and hinged floor](/components/interior.md)) gives enough room to stretch out — "I sleep like that on Whisper."[47] This is a real, as-built fix, used successfully on a boat well under the taller sailor's own height, and is a lighter-touch alternative to the earlier, more invasive roof-raising/ballast-swapping proposal for a similar few inches of headroom. Raising the hatch cover itself was expected to add a little more headroom on top of that.[48] Construction to this design has spread as far as **Sebastopol, California**, on the Pacific coast north of San Francisco Bay — one data point for how widely Paradox building has travelled beyond its original builders.[49]

## "Room for 2": a firsthand two- and three-up data point

The most direct verdict on record on the recurring two-up question above, a question fielded "many" times over the years: **yes, there is room for two aboard Paradox, but it is not spacious**. [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s boats were "designed primarily to sail 1 person full-time," with some accommodation for an extra crew member on **short cruises** only. A genuine firsthand **three-up** data point backs this up: cruising in company with Matt Layden in the Bahamas, the group would cook aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), then **all three** would **eat aboard Paradox** together. The overall verdict matches the established findings closely: a Paradox can fit "a few close friends aboard for short stints," but the available space is "similar to that of being in a tent" — a vivid, independent restatement of the same conclusion reached above from mockups and the owners' own Little-Cruiser experience.[50]

Photographs from this account — Matt aboard Paradox with a companion; Matt in the Bahamas aboard Paradox; Matt sailing Paradox in Georgetown, Bahamas — give the first confirmation on record that Matt's then-girlfriend sailed **aboard Paradox itself**, not only the earlier Balboa 20. See [Matt Layden (biography) — "Room for 2"](/matt-layden/biography.md) for the fuller biographical context.

A separate verdict reaches the same conclusion by a different route: as designed, Paradox has plenty of room for one person of medium size, and is really only suitable for two when day sailing — though others, Matt Layden included, have lived aboard two-up without incident.[51]

## A day-sailing two-up data point: Alastair and his son

Nearly two years after the Sebastian, FL weekend (above), the next concrete **two-up** data point comes from sailing "[little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" on Poole Harbour with a **son**, home from finishing his school exams. Like the Sebastian outing, this was a single **day sail** rather than extended cruising, but unlike that trip it involved actually **sailing together** rather than one person being dropped ashore — the son went out briefly in a dinghy with a camera to photograph "LJ" under sail, before rejoining.[52]

"LJ" **handled fine with two on board**, and for a day sail it was "ok" — but the single seat is not big enough for two to sit comfortably side by side unless "you want to be really cosy," so one of the two has to sit **inside the cabin** instead. One good suggestion on record for a sailor planning to sail two-up **regularly**: gain a second usable **outside** seat by extending the opening hatch further forward, concertina-style (see [Little Jim — "Little Jim, 2 up"](/boats/little-jim.md)). The two aboard did not get in each other's way at all, manoeuvring around each other in the cockpit/cabin area without difficulty for the day. The verdict was that two could not **sleep** comfortably aboard "unless they were very friendly," comparing it to a two-man mountaineering tent — reaffirming, from a second independent data point, the same day-sailing-yes/sleeping-no split already established by the other two-up findings above — though a seriously cruising couple could **work watches**, with only one needing to lie down at a time.[52]

## Sleeping two aboard a Paradox-family cabin

Two independent, practice-tested approaches address the specific problem of **two people sleeping** in a Paradox-family cabin overnight, distinct from the day-sailing- and berth-length findings above.

One approach, from years of tight-cabin cruising aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) (in reply to [Aussie Paradox](/boats/aussie-paradox.md) two-up sleeping-test photos), is to sleep **head to foot** when a little more elbow room is wanted (workable "just as long as one of you doesn't have stinky feet"), and sometimes **side by side**, though the arm underneath "sometimes falls asleep." Either way, comfort comes down mainly to the **mattress** — a foam pad reused across most trips had grown thin enough to warrant replacement before the next voyage.[53]

A different, gear-based solution used aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," adapted from camping and diving experience: a thin (0.5 in) camping sleep mat with two shaped **foam blocks** fixed to it, one under the shoulders/head and one under the hips — targeted support at the body's actual pressure points, taking up far less room than a full mattress. It is rated very comfortable, while individual results will vary.[54]

The stock Paradox cabin's clear sleeping space measures **930 mm (37.2 in) wide by 1900 mm (76 in) long**. Two normal-sized (170 lb) people can sleep in it if they are close, but the harder problem is living aboard together rather than merely fitting: with two aboard there is barely room to move around each other for something as simple as fixing dinner in bad weather. One couple has addressed this in practice on their own cruises not by avoiding the tight cabin but by **pitching a tent ashore** as a base camp, sailing and exploring by day and returning to the boat, rather than trying to live together in the cabin's full confines without ever getting off; solo, by contrast, one sailor has spent up to **five days aboard** quite comfortably without leaving the boat at all.[55]

## The as-designed floor's exact dimensions, and how much more can be gained

Completing the exchange above, the stock Paradox's precise **flat floor** dimensions as drawn are **1.9 m by 0.94 m**, tapering to **0.78 m** at the narrow end (where the mast step intrudes on the space).[56] A builder willing to sacrifice the **side-bin storage** can gain roughly another **100 mm**; sacrificing the [flotation](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md) as well can gain **200 mm** — the most precise figures on record for how much of Paradox's stock two-up sleeping space is fixed by the floor plan itself versus recoverable by giving up storage or flotation volume. Two independent, later data points from builders' own boats corroborate this figure closely: **1.9 m by 0.9 m** for "Little Jim," and **74.5 in by 36 in** (about 1.89 m by 0.91 m) for "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" — see [first-boat suitability — build-time figures and difficulty rating](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md#build-time-figures-and-difficulty-rating) for both builders' full answers to a newcomer's build-time and dimension questions.

For a structural approach to gaining further sleeping room by reworking bulkhead 3 itself — narrowing its webs and reinforcing them in steel or doubled plywood — see [bulkheads — modifying bulkhead 3 to gain room for sleeping two](/components/bulkheads.md#modifying-bulkhead-3-to-gain-room-for-sleeping-two).

## A newcomer's basic-interior-dimensions FAQ, answered

A prospective builder considering a Paradox for cruising occasionally with a son, at 6 ft 2 in and 200 lb, asked for the stock cabin sole's length bulkhead-to-bulkhead, its maximum width, whether removing the side bins would meaningfully widen it or the center frame would still be in the way regardless, headroom sole-to-ceiling, and how a 20% enlargement would change the trade-offs. The stock plans themselves, posted to the group's files section under "Study Plans," give any of these dimensions directly.[57][58]

The clearest concrete numbers came from a Paradox/Enigma-hybrid builder's own boat, built to the same sleeping-area length as stock Paradox: **1900 mm** for the sleeping area, and **920 mm** between the bins at frame 3 — closely matching the stock clear-sleeping-space figures already on record above (see [sleeping two aboard a Paradox-family cabin](#sleeping-two-aboard-a-paradox-family-cabin)). See [Noli — NOLI's own dimensions, and a frame-2/frame-3 modification for extra sleeping room](/boats/noli.md#nolis-own-dimensions-and-a-frame-2frame-3-modification-for-extra-sleeping-room) for the fuller technique this answer drew on, including a frame-2 opening modification that lets a sleeper's feet extend into the forward stateroom and a frame-3 narrowing option to ease pressure on that bulkhead. Cruising two aboard a stock-sized cabin was judged more realistically solved by camping ashore than by both actually sleeping aboard, in contrast to Matt Layden, whose own slight build let him and a partner sleep aboard together.[59]

A separate, already-built modification reached a concrete outcome of its own: [Stephen Yahn's widened bulkhead 3 aboard "Ironsides"](/boats/ironsides.md#a-widened-steel-reinforced-bulkhead-3), built without bins at all, yields a **41 in** cabin sole width.[60]

Thanking the group, the newcomer was left needing only headroom, and was directed toward the NOLI builder's own boat, a Paradox/Enigma hybrid, for further detail.[61] Headroom itself drew a fresh data point consistent with the figures already on record for "Little Jim" (see [interior bin panels — cabin floor-to-roof height](/components/interior.md#cabin-floor-to-roof-height)): **930 mm** between the floor and the closed hatch — a figure that matters less in practice than it might suggest, since a sailor steering from inside is rarely sitting bolt upright and is much more likely to be slouching in a corner.[62]

## A very large sailor's fit: 6'8"/315 lb, and a cardboard-mockup suggestion

The tallest and heaviest prospective builder on record to ask about fit — **6 ft 8 in and 315 lb** — drew the fullest single round of replies on the topic. Several answers converged on the same underlying point already established above: raw size matters less than **how** the height and weight are distributed, and comfort is ultimately **relative to what a person is used to**.[63]

One data point comes from a sailor comfortable at 6'1"/225 lb aboard his own build: no problem was expected fitting a much larger sailor's *weight*, but **height** was flagged as the harder issue, likely calling for creative sleeping arrangements — an inflatable mattress, as used elsewhere in the fleet, would elevate a sleeper enough to let the lower legs hang past bulkhead #2 and rest on a cushion. Sitting on the thwart with the hatch open should pose no problem; with the hatch closed, a more reclined posture (aided by folding canvas/foam chairs) would likely be needed. The case for **stretching the design** was renewed for anyone concerned (see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)), alongside a caution against a cardboard cabin mockup for judging a very tight fit, since a flimsy box moving underfoot while entering/exiting a boat this size "can put one off" — even though a cardboard mockup is exactly the successful technique used above.[64]

Comfort is relative, likening the cabin to being in a car or a two-man tent, and — as with earlier size questions — the recommendation was to see the boat in person before deciding, rather than relying on any mockup or measurement alone.[65]

The same point already demonstrated with numbers above: raw height is not, by itself, the problem — what matters is how it is **distributed** between torso and legs. A 6'2" friend with a long torso and short legs found the cabin, hatch shut, too cramped, whereas the same height with long legs and a short torso would pose no problem. The cabin was still judged more comfortable than sitting exposed in an open boat.[66]

The same underlying concern — whether a 10-15% overall size increase to Paradox or [Micro](/matt-layden/index.md) would fit a larger sailor (6'5"/180 lb) better without claustrophobia or head-bumping[67] — drew a **convertible-car analogy** as the answer: hatch open, sitting on the aft deck, only the head is exposed but the sailor feels as if in the open; hatch shut, sitting on the sole, it is like being in a car with the top up — a small space that does not, for most people, feel confining, just as most drivers do not feel claustrophobic in a car despite its limited room. Seeing a boat in person before deciding was again the recommendation.[68]

A concrete, as-built comfort feature from "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)": her **removable rear window** allows sitting on the rear deck with feet on the seat, and closing the hatch in that position turns it into a reading or chart table (mindful of the boom) — see [Whisper — outboard bracket, cabin headroom, and the trailer](/boats/whisper.md#outboard-bracket-cabin-headroom-and-the-trailer) for the window's origin.[69]

A different suggestion for evaluating fit before building: build a **cardboard mock-up of the cockpit and living area** and "sail" it in the living room, free cardboard being available from big-box stores' overnight stockers — a technique already proven with a cardboard PDRacer mockup used to judge sizing before committing to air-box dimensions.[70] This independently reprises the same cardboard-mockup method already used above (see [a builder concludes the boat is too small for him](#a-builder-concludes-the-boat-is-too-small-for-him)) — the flimsiness caution above notwithstanding, cardboard mockups recur across the archive as the community's default answer to "will I fit."

A more rigorous scaling method proposed for the same problem: verify comfort in a stock prototype across the three likely sailing postures — on the helm seat, on a 4 in cushion on the sole, and lying on a mattress on the sole, for one or two crew — before enlarging anything. If a mockup shows a genuine size problem, the recommended approach is to build a cardboard mockup on the main cabin sized to one's own dimensions, measure the resulting length/beam/height upscale factors against the stock drawings, apply a single uniform scaling factor to all three dimensions, replan the plywood cutting layout, and adjust ballast to bring the enlarged hull back down to its own upscaled design waterline — the same conclusion the archive had already reached, that a Paradox can be built to 16 ft with no rig-dimension or performance penalty (see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)). The design was judged worth the extra effort at 14, 16, or 18 ft alike.[71]

A qualification to the removable-rear-window answer above: sitting on the rear deck is a **fair-weather-only** technique — in bad weather the hatch closes and the boat is watertight, with the rear window simply a nice option to have when conditions allow it. "[Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)" carries the same removable rear window without any reported problems.[72]

A further reservation about the removable-rear-window idea: it forces an all-or-nothing choice between being fully inside or fully outside, with no middle position. On the raised-weight/stability question, a firsthand comparison from two legs of the same passage: one day sitting down for **15 hours**, the return leg the next day taking **7 hours**, most of it spent **standing**. The conclusion drawn was that if the extra height of a standing sailor's weight were really a stability problem, discomfort would force a retreat below well before it became dangerous — since acquiring better cushions, no such retreat has been needed.[73]

A separate headroom fix, worked out for a tall ("beanpole") workmate who took an interest in the design but could not sit inside without severely denting his head on the cabin top: a shallow, roughly **4 in deep footwell** cut into the cabin sole gives the extra headroom needed, without raising the cabin top or lowering the whole sole. The workmate never built a boat of his own.[74]

## Series drogue vs. sea anchor geometry, and testing a rear window against green water

Combining the removable rear window with a properly sized heavy-weather interior was floated as a further step, with a caution attached: anyone planning to stream a warp, series sea drogue, or similar device from the stern in a gale should first pressure-test the rear window against **hydro-pistoning** — spraying it with a 15 ft/second water jet to confirm it would stay watertight under a breaking-wave strike while lying to a series drogue.[75]

A reluctance to tow any drogue from the stern in the first place, since the **tiller passes through a hole in the transom**: even small waves slapping the transom while landing on a beach let a little water through, and while that is only a few cupfuls in that setting, sustained stern-on wave action over many hours could add up to a significant amount — a concern that carries directly into the tiller-port/vent-baffle watertightness question addressed in full at [transom deck plate and vent baffle](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#a-proposed-permanent-tiller-boot-and-smiths-rebuttal-with-sea-anchor-vs-series-drogue-doctrine).[76]

## Why the cabin is enclosed: a convertible-car analogy

Even once [chine-runner windward-performance evidence](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#pointing-angles-owners-report) has convinced a newcomer that the design sails to windward well, Paradox can still look **"awfully closed up"** compared to a more open cockpit.[77] The same **convertible-car analogy** given for the claustrophobia question above answers this too: think of Paradox as a convertible — top up, the sailor is cozy and sheltered from the weather; top down, out in the wind and sun. As with a real convertible car, this is something of an illusion either way, since the sailor is really still "inside" the same small hull regardless of hatch position — but, also as with the car, "it is a pretty good illusion."[78] No alternative, more-open chine-runner layout is on record; the enclosed helm is treated as integral to the design rather than an option to build around.

## Sitting two side by side on the seat, hatch open

Two smallish people can sit **side by side on the seat** with the hatch open, though comfort scales inversely with size — "the smaller they are the more comfortable they will be."[79][80] A caveat comes with actually sailing that way: it works, but is "a bit of a squeeze," and under way one occupant is continually squashed against the other as the boat heels — a seated-two-up data point distinct from the day-sailing and sleeping-two findings recorded elsewhere on this page, since it concerns two people sharing the single seat itself rather than one on the seat and one inside the cabin.[81]

Whether a **second, lower seat** mounted below the forward end of the hatch might work instead remains an open question in the archive: no reply addressing whether such a seat would leave enough knee-room for the person at the normal seat is recorded.[82]

A separate, practice-tested alternative to sharing the single seat is simply not to share it: on one boat, a wife has sailed comfortably seated **athwartships on the forward berth cushions**, back to the windward side — an arrangement expected to feel stuffy but which in practice gets plenty of air through the vent box, with no claustrophobia reported.[83] On the low-second-seat idea, a considered answer, worked out only after already building and fitting the as-designed solid roof, was that a **soft-top roof running the full length of the cabin**, paired with a second bench seat forward of bulkhead 3, would have been the better solution — letting part or all of the roof be removed to open up that forward seat when wanted, at the cost of a little security (theft) and seaworthiness, though still leaving the boat safer than a conventional open dinghy.[84]

## Long-passage seat comfort: cushion practice

Sitting at the helm seat for hours at a stretch is a distinct comfort problem from the cabin-fit questions above, and the practice-tested answer is simply to rotate cushions rather than rely on any single one. A discarded wheelchair demo cushion, sourced free through a home-health-care contact, eliminated seat fatigue entirely on a weekend's sailing — a genuinely effective solution, though the cushions themselves are normally too expensive to buy new and are worth seeking out secondhand instead.[85] Another approach keeps a hard foam pad, a soft foam pad, and a small inner tube aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)," switching between them every couple of hours, finding the rotation itself — not any one cushion's material — is what actually reduces the pain of a long spell at the helm.[86] A further, independently reported version of the same rotation practice on the same boat: seating was initially uncomfortable simply because no cushion had been thought of at all, solved once a number of different cushions were kept aboard and swapped through the day to shift the seat's pressure points.[87]

The wheelchair-style cushion's design is what makes it work: a robust plastic envelope, inside a fabric cover, filled with polystyrene-type beads suspended in a gel, which moulds to the sitter's shape and spreads the load evenly enough to prevent pressure sores — the same principle used in helicopter pilots' dinghy-box seats, whose buttock-shaped tops and thin fabric pads have had considerable ergonomic development behind them, since a pilot fidgeting in the seat is undesirable. A loose cushion is also used doubled as a kneeling pad or shoulder padding when working in the forward cabin, with a cheap folding beach seat with a back rest sought without success — useful for sitting on the after deck with legs dangling over the stern, a comfortable enough position in itself but one that leaves the back unsupported, since leaning against the cabin side there is both too low and sloped away too much to help.[88]

A cushion of closed-cell **Airex foam** — denser than open foam but with a similar "memory foam" feel — has been used for years of long winter passages aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), sourced originally from Defender Industries; Airex foam remains available from specialty foam suppliers even after Defender stopped carrying that particular product.[89]

Even an already-established rotation does not fully solve the problem: a further owner, cycling through a number of cushions of different construction — foam, inflatable, and gel among them — every couple of hours, still ends up tender by the end of a long passage, leaving open whether shedding some of the sitter's own weight would matter more than any cushion choice.[149]

A further owner reports the same difficulty tracing to the low, transverse stock seat itself: sitting higher feels more comfortable, and heeling around 20 degrees adds to the discomfort, since it presses a shoulder against the cabin side; moving to the cabin sole for a change of position helps only briefly, since frequent kneeling is needed to look around for other boats — themselves hard to keep watch for through the cabin windows once salt spray covers them.[150]

For a proposed purpose-built adjustable seat rather than a stack of cushions — the "Quintessential Seat" specification, a hammock-style bicycle-saddle proposal, and the stability objection it drew — see [interior — Quintessential Seat](/components/interior.md#quintessential-seat).

A rotation of throw pillows and strapped or overstuffed marine cushions, chosen to suit whatever seating arrangement the moment calls for, also incorporates a raised radio: a roughly 16 in tall work radio, powered by its own charging batteries, mounted in an aluminum tubular frame with a cushion set on top, used for a better view while motoring.[90]

For a speculative lift-off roof-and-hatch modification aimed at the same two-up ventilation problem — never built, but debated at length against a canvas top and other alternatives — see [hatch — a removable roof and hatch, proposed but not built](/components/hatch.md#a-removable-roof-and-hatch-proposed-but-not-built).

## Why Paradox is fundamentally a solo boat, and one builder's case for a removable cabin instead

Carrying a second person comfortably is a harder problem than the stock cabin's roughly 3 ft sleeping width suggests: even two people on good terms find that width cramped for sleeping compared with a modern double bed (about 4.5 ft), and two adults sitting together in the cabin or hatchway for a full day's passage need to be genuinely compatible companions — a child or grandchild as crew may fare better. A **20% scaled-up Paradox**, built off the same base drawings, gains about 10 in of extra bunk width over stock — see [Building Paradox larger — a worked 20% scale-up](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement) for the full dimensions, sail area, and displacement; a further modification eliminates the scarfed overlap of the side-panel joints in favor of a plain butt-and-block joint, adding length concentrated mainly in the living cabin rather than distributed evenly along the hull (compare the standard scarf-jointing practice at [scarf joints](/construction/scarf-joints.md)).

As designed, the stock-length Paradox is judged among the smallest reasonable deep-sea boats afloat — better suited to genuine ocean work than many micro-voyagers chosen for ocean crossings, close to fully self-righting (a property especially valuable at this tiny size), sailable equally from fully inside, head-and-shoulders out, or fully open, and, with its simple rig and small appendages, resistant to structural damage even when rolled. It is a poor choice, on the same reasoning, for family day-sailing or beach cruising with children, sand, and coolers aboard — sociable, multi-person daysailing calls for a different, larger design entirely, several of which still tow behind a compact car without needing Paradox's lead ballast or comparable structure.

A **removable cabin unit** was proposed as the best available compromise for a builder wanting to carry an occasional passenger without committing to a full stretch: a low coaming or carlin ledge around the cockpit, with a cabin unit that lifts off and secures to the deck with bolts or dogs, opening up the people-space for fair-weather day sailing while still permitting solo voyaging when the cabin is refitted — falling back on a tent ashore to solve overnight crew accommodation rather than trying to sleep two aboard. A milder alternative simply spreads bulkheads 2, 3, and 4 apart by 2-4 in total for a slightly longer sleeping area, without increasing the beam itself, since [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) lose effectiveness as hull-side curvature increases with added beam. Because the stock hull is fundamentally a simple plywood skiff, such modifications are mechanically easy to make, but each change has knock-on effects elsewhere in the structure, calling for real related experience or problem-solving skill, and a correspondingly modest, conservative approach to any one change.

This case for staying close to the stock design was, in the end, self-directed rather than a rejection of anyone else's boat: the stock Paradox was judged very close to one builder's own needs and limits for making passages on big open water among wild coasts and islands, calling for a good seaboat and genuine self-sufficiency — "Paradox will do it."[91]

## A firsthand comparison: a Paradox owner who also bought a two-berth "Peep Hen"

**Glen Maxwell**, who built and sailed the Paradox "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" for five years, weighed the same problem from lived experience rather than proposed modifications: a young, agile, adventurous couple willing to make real sacrifices can cruise a 16-18 ft Paradox-family boat together, but anyone with more sailing behind them will generally prefer more comfort than such a small enclosed cabin affords for stretches longer than 4-5 hours. Rather than stretch or modify "Zoë," Glen bought a second, different boat sized for two: a **Peep Hen**, about 14 ft long, drawing 10 in, weighing 650 lb, and towing easily behind a small car. The Peep Hen has two berths with enough knee clearance to avoid hitting the cockpit sole, a porta-potti stowed out of the way that doubles as a step into the cabin, a cockpit convertible into a double berth, and a galley usable in nearly any weather. She is gaff cat-rigged, launches and gets sailing faster than the Paradox, and — while not as good a sailer as "Zoë" — sails well; named "**Hedwig**," she cost a road trip from Live Oak, FL to the Philadelphia area and back plus **$3,000**, about the same money "Zoë" took in materials alone but far less building time. A weekend sailing "Zoë" by day and camping ashore, compared against four days and three nights cruising aboard "Hedwig" around Cayo Costa with a group, settled the comparison decisively in the second boat's favor for two-up cruising: given the choice, "there is no way she would have chosen 'Zoë' over 'Hedwig' for that adventure." The resulting practice is running two boats for two different purposes — the Paradox solo, the Peep Hen two-up — with a stated preference, in hindsight, for acquiring the two-person boat first and building the solo Paradox afterward.[92][93]

## The Peep Hen's designer, family fleet, and some open questions

By 2008, the Peep Hen — designed by **Rubin Train** — had become something of a family boat: four were owned across the extended family, including one built by a brother, the first Peep Hen Glen Maxwell ever sailed, an experience that left him surprised at how well so broad-beamed and short a hull could sail; measured against Paradox's cabin, the extra room felt like "a palace."[94] A separate, standing request for a photograph of a Peep Hen actually under sail, rather than motoring with the sail merely flapping, drew no photograph directly — only pointers to where one might be found, a folder in an outside Yahoo group showing a Peep Hen under a single reef in the Gulf, and the `peephens.org` web site, with more images promised on a personal site soon.[95][96] Whether the design bears any relationship to the Bolger Micro and whether it remains in production are open questions raised on the list but never answered on record — the boat was advertised in Small Boat Journal in its day.[97] Rubin Train himself, though, is known to still be active in powerboat design, believed to be overseeing production of one of his own designs.[98]

## Alternatives researched for two-person coastal cruising in cold climates

Following up on the Peep Hen comparison, a search for that design or an equivalent turned up few used Peep Hens for sale (long out of production) and only a handful of comparable small plans-built alternatives with similar two-berth features: the Mertens-Goossens **Adele 14 and 16**, and Midwest Engineering's **Kingston 15 Pocket Yacht**. For sailing in a cool, high-latitude climate (Whitehorse, Yukon, and Skagway, Alaska were given as reference locations, where summers stay cool and boaters typically dress warmly), the stock Paradox was judged to remain the better fit despite the two-berth research, given that solo sailing was still expected roughly 80% of the time.[99] A further, independent search for a similar solo-with-occasional-second-crew boat turned up the **Norwalk Island Sharpie 18** as a strong two-person alternative (costing more to build than Paradox), alongside the **B&B Yacht Designs Core Sound 19** and the **Adele/"Adelie" 16** already noted above.[100]

## A second asking of the 6'4" fit question, a year later

The same 6'4" fit-and-clearance question recurred verbatim a year after it was first asked and answered above (see [pilot weight, height, and stability at stock length](#pilot-weight-height-and-stability-at-stock-length)) — this time from the same sailor who had asked it originally, still without having built.[101] He preferred to stay under 14 ft rather than build a larger version, and was less worried about the sleeping position than about clearance while actually sailing. A first answer, from a sailor five inches shorter, reported plenty of room in every position.[102]

A fuller answer worked through the geometry rather than a single data point. Height is only a problem sailing with the hatch shut, or sleeping; it would be a simple matter to modify the access to the forward stateroom so a sailor's legs could pass straight through, accommodating someone up to 8 ft. One concrete data point: sitting with the back flat against a wall, the top of a 5'6" sailor's head measured 890 mm off the floor; sitting upright in the Paradox cabin left 35 mm of clearance to the 20 mm foam lining the closed hatch, and about 100 mm when slouched into a normal sailing posture. Deepening the hatch by 50-100 mm, or lowering the floor, were both offered as simple fixes if more headroom were ever wanted. A firsthand secondhand data point backs the general reassurance: someone "over 6 ft" sailed "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" and did not find the height a problem. The only real limiting condition is sailing with the hatch shut in genuinely rough or wet weather — judged an acceptable trade against being wet and cold in an open boat.[103]

A cardboard mock-up of the interior, built from the dimensions and clearances given above, gave the asking 6'4" sailor a better sense of the boat's actual size and settled his own clearance worry.[104] A fellow builder's dry wit named the technique **"CAD — Cardboard Aided Design."**[105]

## A re-measured headroom figure, and the standard build-time caution

A prospective builder long in the torso, weighing a build against a fixed deadline the following summer, took up the recurring floor-to-ceiling question once more. A fresh as-built figure for "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" came back at **940 mm (about 37 in)**, floor to the underside of the closed hatch — matching exactly the same boat's own earlier-recorded figure for the same spot (see [interior bin panels — cabin floor-to-roof height](/components/interior.md#cabin-floor-to-roof-height)), though close rather than identical to a separate 930 mm re-measurement given above in the interim; the small spread across the three readings most likely reflects measurement precision rather than any change to the boat itself.[106][107] Most builders need **18 months to two years** to finish a Paradox (see [build effort and workspace](/construction/build-effort.md)), a schedule worth weighing against any fixed target date rather than assuming a build can be rushed to meet one.[107]

That same prospective builder confirmed the build-time caution had settled the question: without the time for such a commitment, he put a number on his own headroom need instead — about **39 in**, being long in the torso despite standing a full six feet tall and already finding it hard to fit most cars — leaving the stock **37 in** figure just short of what he would need, and leaving him weighing a pre-built **Pelican** as a quicker alternative.[108] He closed the thread undecided, thanking the group for the alternative-design pointers but still weighing a build against simply buying a small sailboat instead.[109]

## Interior-beam geometry, Paradox vs. Enigma 460 (Dave's Wiki)

A [reference wiki](/references/daves-wiki.md) works through the *usable* interior beam. Paradox's 4' beam is eaten into by **built-in side shelves (~3–4" per side)**, leaving roughly **3' 6" of human room**; the [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) 460's 3' 9" beam, without those shelves, gives **comparable or slightly more** space. The crew compartment (between the fore and aft bulkheads) is **7' 7"** long, with sitting headroom for a six-footer ("I can sit upright athwartship with several inches of clearance overhead (I am 6 ft.)"). Sleeping remains snug: the Enigma 360 sleeps one, the 460 "may have enough room for 2 smaller people to sleep snugly," and a wiki photo shows "2 people crammed into Paradox in a sleeping position" — consistent with the mockup-based headroom/fit findings above. One builder floated adding a Paradox-style **lift-out rear deck-house window** (like the one on the Paradox "Enuf") so the sailor could sit on the aft deck with legs inside the cabin — the same removable-rear-window idea seen on [Whisper](/boats/whisper.md).[110]

## A second owner's verdict: too small for two, with one modification that helps

A second owner's verdict on two-up sailing, reached independently of the day-sailing findings above: **"Paradox is too small for a crew of two. I know that people have done it, even for up to a fortnight, but how that was achieved without tempers being frayed I can't imagine."** A single afternoon's sail with a grandchild aboard a stock-cabin boat found "barely room to move around." A later, second Paradox with a removable washboard fared better for the same purpose, since the washboard lets one person sit out on the aft deck rather than sharing the single helm seat or the cabin floor — the same removable-washboard advantage already recorded in the "Room for 2" findings above (see [designed for one, room for a guest](#designed-for-one-room-for-a-guest-the-bolducs-two-up-reality-microcruisingcom)). A speculative further step, never built: a soft, roll-away top over the coachroof, letting a forward crew member sit on a transverse seat clear of the aft one, with either person able to steer using the tiller lines.[111]

## A restated verdict: unsuited to overweight, large, or inflexible sailors (December 2008)

A restated December 2008 verdict holds Paradox unsuited to an overweight or otherwise large sailor, or to anyone who cannot kneel or bend easily: reaching into the forward compartment, and especially retrieving the stowed battery, can call for near-"Houdini" flexibility. See [interior bins and floor — easing access](/components/interior.md#easing-access-eliminating-bulkhead-2s-raised-edge-and-a-wider-entrance-on-enuf) for the modifications this drew.[112]

## A further newcomer's fit question, and a stretched-Paradox/Swaggie pointer (February 2009)

A further newcomer, at **6 ft 4.5 in** and wanting **39 in** of sitting headroom, asked whether he could realistically fit a stock Paradox or should look elsewhere.[113] The practical answer: simply add **three inches** to the cabin's vertical dimensions, and consider bringing the forward stateroom doorway's lower edge flush with the cabin sole, a modification others have used with good results — "**Ironsides**"'s own first sail left the opposite impression from what her size on the water suggests: small outside, "very roomy on the inside."[114] A second reply, from a correspondent himself tall and drawn to Paradox as a candidate large micro-cruiser, pointed to the designer-sanctioned stretched version (see [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md)) as something he had already weighed for his own fit, alongside two already-covered larger alternatives — John Welsford's Swaggie and a smaller Welsford "Sundowner"-class 21-footer. See [similar boat designs — John Welsford's Swaggie](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#john-welsfords-swaggie) for the fuller detail this reply added.[115]

## Further replies: a Yrvind pointer, a recovered measurement, a pop-up-top idea, and a standing-room objection (February 2009)

The same exchange drew a further alternative-design suggestion: [Sven Yrvind's](/references/yrvind-website.md) newest boat is only a little bigger than Paradox, but her deck-stepped mast and lack of a deck vent leave considerably more usable interior space — floated as "a direction to go" for anyone who finds the stock cabin too tight.[116]

A further, independent answer to the original fit question survives only as a quotation inside a later reply, its own message having gone missing from the archive.[117] It restated "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)"'s already-recorded cabin figures — a **37 in (940 mm)** maximum from sole to the underside of the closed hatch, and the forward-stateroom access already cut level with the sole to stow a folding bicycle (see [boats/little-jim.md — the forward-stateroom access cut down for bike stowage](/boats/little-jim.md#the-forward-stateroom-access-cut-down-for-bike-stowage)) — and added two further points: sitting upright without a cushion needs about **35 in**, in principle risking a banged head, though lounging rather than sitting bolt upright avoids it in practice, and the flush forward-stateroom modification gains a full **30 in** of leg room when sleeping. Raising the cabin roof slightly (at some cost to sail stowage) or, more safely, lowering the sole were offered as further fixes if more headroom were ever wanted, with a final caution that the only sure way to judge personal fit is to go and look at a real boat.

A different reply reframed the whole question: sitting headroom only matters underway, since a simple pop-up top over the cabin's center section provides standing room at anchor — the sides made of any water-shedding material, held up by as little as a single central stanchion or two at the sides or ends, with the material's own tension (once the top is raised) preventing any racking load. Standing, even hunched over, matters in practice for basic tasks like dressing or sponging off.[118]

The original asker, weighing the trade-off, judged it worth accepting regardless: already owning a shallow-draft West Wight Potter but wanting more strength and offshore capability than she offers, he found Paradox's own shallow draft and centerboard-free windward ability worth the fit compromise, and hoped to find one to see in person near his home in Michigan.[119]

A further objection raised the same question from a different angle, independent of headroom: Paradox's combination of features is otherwise unmatched (barring Sven Yrvind's own boats), but the total inability to stand or stretch one's legs at sea for weeks at a time — unlike even a small cockpit boat, where at least one can stand and work the kinks out of a bad back each morning — gave real pause.[120] The direct answer: sliding open Paradox's own hatch lets a sailor stand up, and video of "[Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)" under way shows standing and sailing together is genuinely feasible aboard one of these boats.[121]

## Sailing performance with an extra crew member aboard (February 2009)

A newcomer's question about how the boat performs with two people aboard, rather than the usual headroom/fit angle above, drew a direct answer from firsthand cruising authority: **more ballast is better**, since Paradox is a cruising sharpie that benefits from being loaded up to her cruising waterline, and an extra crew member's weight most likely improves performance in most cases by digging the chines in deeper.[122][123] [Dave Bolduc](/people/bolduc/dave.md) has sailed [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own Paradox three-up without noticing much real difference beyond some crowding below, and has sailed [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) three-up with the same nice sailing characteristics observed — these boats are not planing racing designs, where an extra crew member's weight adversely affects performance; they sail at displacement speeds and carry cruising loads well.[123] A joking suggestion that a third crew member must have been lashed to the mast, or else was a small child, drew only amusement rather than a real design concern.[124]

The most people ever carried aboard Little Cruiser was **four, including one child**, briefly, for picking up a friend and her daughter from a nearby beach. Sharpies historically served as fishing and cargo-lightering craft, and remain especially well suited to carrying heavy loads as a result; even the smaller Enigma seems to sail better to windward with an extra crew member aboard.[125]

## A UK owner's firsthand comfort verdict, and the case against needing a dinghy (February 2010)

A large sailor — 6 ft tall and 18 stone — who had sailed as a guest aboard a stock Paradox found no trouble getting into, out of, or otherwise accommodating himself within her, judging the design more spacious than a similarly-sized small cruising sloop (an S3 Silhouette) he had also sailed, since Paradox is an uncluttered rectangle free of a bridge deck or fitted cabin furniture and mouldings, with a near-identical waterline length.[126] The same account credits Paradox's very shallow draft with removing any need to carry a dinghy at all — recalling, by contrast, a frightening night-time fall between boat and dinghy transferring off a larger cruiser on a tidal river.[126]

## A Great Lakes fit question (October 2010)

A **6'3"/250 lb** sailor in the Great Lakes region, having found no completed or nearly-completed Paradox nearby to see or climb aboard, wondered whether the boat would suit his frame — unanswered on record.[127]

## The thread continues: a Taiwan-based sailor's own fit account (October 2010)

A Taiwan-based American sailor, 6'3" and about 210 lb, went aboard a Paradox built in Taipei — [Auspice](/boats/auspice.md), built by [Jimmy Lu](/people/jimmy-lu.md) — and found her "small for someone like me... it feels like I'm sitting in a toy"; the main cabin's 190 cm length was barely tolerable for even one night at his own roughly 191 cm height, bulkhead two's 7 in lower lip made the forepeak hard to reach, and manoeuvring inside felt "like being locked in a refrigerator and trying to turn around." He was careful to call this his own reality at his size rather than a criticism of the design, and was weighing a larger Paradox — his first choice — against a different design entirely, for a planned build once he relocated to Thailand.[128][129]

The Great Lakes sailor whose own fit question had opened this thread (above) replied that, having by then sat down with the plans and mocked the cabin up in cardboard, the fit looked tight but workable provided he could reach and stow everything without contortion; already owning both the stock plans and a build manual, and by then deep into the archive's back-posts, he judged a 10% scale-up worth considering.[130]

A reply drawing on the same forward-stateroom modification already documented on [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md#a-taller-sailors-comfort-verdict-and-dropping-the-cabin-sole-considered-october-2010) offered a firsthand counterpoint: cutting bulkhead two's lip down (there, to stow a folding bike) also let its owner sleep further forward with his feet in the forepeak instead of the main cabin, and he reported feeling noticeably more supple after a couple of days aboard than when he started — agreeing manoeuvring inside with the cabin shut is harder for a taller sailor, but not disqualifying.[131]

A related question about why an enlarged Paradox needs to carry as much weight as it does, raised in the same thread, is covered at [Building Paradox larger — a further 2010 recurrence](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-further-2010-recurrence-a-weight-question-and-another-files-section-repost).

## A photograph of two people fitted before the deck was installed (2011)

A construction-stage photograph — two people lying stretched out inside a Paradox hull before her deck was fitted — is a further visual record of how two people fit the boat's floor space, consistent with the dimensions already established above.[132]

## A further 2011 recurrence: weighing stock against extended for occasional two-up sailing

The same absence of documented two-up experience recurred once more, from a prospective builder weighing the stock boat against the [enlarged variant](/design/larger-paradox.md) specifically so a usually-solo sailor could occasionally bring a partner along — citing easy launching, [chine runners](/chine-runners/index.md) suited to lagoon sailing without a centerboard or keel, and a shoal draft that removes any need to haul out for winter as reasons for choosing Paradox over other designs in the first place. The underlying question of how well two people actually live aboard, beyond occasional day sailing, remains as open as it was above.[133]

## A 2011 answer: canvas roof and a second seat for occasional day-sailing two-up

One answer to the same recurring question proposed trading away some of the sealed cabin's ultimate seaworthiness for daysailing comfort: replace the plywood cabin roof and hatch with canvas, and fit a second seat at bulkhead 3 so a guest could sit forward of the skipper once the roof is removed. This reduces the boat's ultimate seaworthiness, but would still likely be safer than any open dinghy.[134] A follow-up idea proposed opening, securely closable windows on the cuddy's port and starboard sides instead, intended to keep the boat as seaworthy as the stock design when closed — unbuilt and unconfirmed on record.[135]

## A 2014 recurrence: chine runners and an enclosed cabin as a further draw, and a preference for keeping the hard roof

The same question recurred once more, from a prospective owner finishing a first, simpler boat and reading up on Little Cruiser as an ideal two-up cruising platform: besides the two-up question itself, gear kept out on deck rather than underfoot in the cabin, and chine runners freeing the interior of a centerboard trunk, were cited as particular draws toward Paradox specifically.[136] The stock boat sails perfectly happily with two aboard; the [canvas-roof/second-seat idea already on record above](#a-2011-answer-canvas-roof-and-a-second-seat-for-occasional-day-sailing-two-up) was restated as one way around the single-seat constraint, framed this time against simply accepting that only one person can be at the seat under way while the other stays below — a trade-off judged to cost the boat's ultimate safety margin only a little, if at all.[137] Weighing the idea, a preference for keeping a hard cabin roof rather than switching to canvas emerged, leaving ventilation for two people in hot weather an open problem still to be solved rather than adopting the canvas-roof answer outright.[138]

## A tent-camping comparison, and Mike Beebe's removable cabin top (2014)

The current owner of "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)," drawing on long tent-camping experience, judged the boat could physically sleep two — two standard Thermarest pads lie side by side on the sole — but doubted the experience would be a good one: a two-person tent at least sits on open ground with room outside to undress and organize gear before crawling in one at a time, where the cabin offers no such staging area, making it awkward for two to get settled for the night or to deal with toilet matters. Under way, the crew member off the seat is cramped and gets in the way each time the sail is furled or hoisted — a difficulty eased, on [Michael Beebe](/people/michael-beebe.md)'s own Paradox, by a removable cabin top that lets that boat sail double more easily. Restating the solo-Paradox-plus-family-boat pattern already established above (see [a firsthand comparison: a Paradox owner who also bought a two-berth "Peep Hen"](#a-firsthand-comparison-a-paradox-owner-who-also-bought-a-two-berth-peep-hen)), he sided with Glen Maxwell's own view: a Paradox for solo sailing most of the time, and a different boat — a Peep Hen or a Siren 17, say — for when family wants to come along.[139]
