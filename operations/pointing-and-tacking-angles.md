# Pointing and tacking-angle expectations

How well a [Paradox](/paradox.md) points to windward depends heavily on trim; disappointing pointing is often down to ballast placement rather than the hull or rig. The boat has concrete tacking-angle figures, drawn from sailing experience, along with a simple practical check for ballast trim.[1][2]

## Two tacking-angle regimes

Sailed to **foot well** (prioritizing speed over pointing), the boat will **tack through about 110 degrees**. Sailed hard on the wind to **point as high as possible**, she will **tack through about 90 degrees**, but "plod along with disappointing performance."[2]

The trade-off comes down to a rule of thumb: a sailor doesn't need more than about a **10% increase in boat speed for every 10 degrees fallen off** the wind to make footing off worthwhile — i.e. a small speed gain from sailing a little freer usually beats squeezing for the highest possible pointing angle. These figures put concrete numbers to the general [chine-runner windward-performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) finding that Paradox sails to windward "passably well" without a centerboard, and are the first specific tacking angles (as opposed to VMG/speed figures) recorded in the archive.[2]

## Checking ballast trim afloat

A simple, practical trim check can confirm whether poor pointing is a trim problem:

- With **no one aboard**, the boat should sit **bow-down**.
- To check trim with a **crew aboard**, have someone ashore observe how the boat is floating while the sailor stands at the helm station; if she trims **bow-up**, move weight **forward**.
- Alternatively, use a **level placed on the floor boards**, since the floor boards are level with the design waterline — a way to check trim without needing an observer ashore.[2]

This afloat check is a distinct, complementary answer to the separate [ballast-rebalancing question](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) of **how much** ballast to move to compensate for a heavier outboard; that question addresses adjusting for an added weight, while this check tests whether the current trim is already off.

## A forward-weight-shift technique for completing a stubborn tack

A recurring tacking failure — stalling head to wind and falling back onto the same tack — is most troublesome in restricted waters where every foot of progress counts, and is made worse by reduced (reefed) sail area. The reliable fix, tested repeatedly during a hard 2.5-mile beat up a quarter-mile-wide channel in a full Force 5 (with Force 9 forecast) aboard ["Little Jim"](/boats/little-jim.md), never failed: just before putting the helm over, duck down into the cabin to shift crew weight forward, which brings the boat through head-to-wind "with great alacrity"; once she has fallen off onto the new tack, regain the helm seat.[3] The same trip also demonstrated the technique's limit: in a narrow, winding stretch of river too tight to tack up, with sail dropped for sculling, the boat proved briefly unable to make any headway into the same strong wind, even to hold a heading into it, until a lighter patch allowed her to continue.

## A chop-tacking technique: release the sheet and move forward

A second, independent account of tacking difficulty in chop, distinct from the Poole Harbour lee-shore beat above (see [a forward-weight-shift technique for completing a stubborn tack](#a-forward-weight-shift-technique-for-completing-a-stubborn-tack)), gives both a firsthand description and a repeatable fix. "A noticeable characteristic of Paradox was her slowness when tacking, particularly when the water was choppy." She needs to be sailed round in a smooth arc; a sudden change of rudder angle does not bring her round the way it would a lightweight dinghy, and instead "would simply act as a brake."[4] Because the mast sits to port of the centreline with the sail hung to the port side of it, drag on that side "could make tacking from starboard tack to port tack problematical" — success is more likely if the boat is kept moving at speed before the tack begins, rather than attempted from a near-stop. A technique for a stubborn tack in chop, learned from a fellow owner: release the sheet at the moment the boat is headed into the wind, and at the same time move forward into the cabin — the sail feathers into the wind as the sheet frees, and the forward weight shift brings the centre of lateral resistance forward, helping the tack complete. This weight-forward fix independently echoes the forward-weight-shift technique on record above, from a different boat and a different owner.[4]

## A quantified tacking-angle trial by GPS

GPS-track analysis of that same windward beat produced the most detailed tacking-angle dataset on record for a Paradox: over **19 tacks**, sailed with the sail reefed to about 50%, the average change of heading through the tack was **130 degrees**, ranging from a **worst of 150 degrees** (near the choppier mouth of the channel, in roughly 2 ft of wind chop) to a **best of 98 degrees** (at the more sheltered head of the channel, in flatter water). Heel stayed steady at the usual **20 degrees** throughout.[5] An earlier attempt with a couple more feet of sail up had left the boat overpowered; the front of the sail beginning to back or flutter serves as a practical guide to the closest safe angle to the wind, with a full sail seeming to point higher than a reefed one, though without hard figures to confirm it. These figures represent at least what a relative novice can expect to get from the boat, with room for a more practiced sailor to do better still.

The same channel has since been named directly: beating up the **Wareham Channel** in **Poole Harbour** presents no problem, but continuing on into the river beyond it is impossible. The general lesson drawn from it is that relatively slow going through a tack, combined with slow acceleration back up to speed on the new course, makes the design unsuitable for tacking up a narrow channel.[6]

## An independent trial at Chew Valley Lake

An independent sail trial reported in ["Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md), sailed aboard ["Little Jim"](/boats/little-jim.md) at Chew Valley Lake in October 2006, reached much the same verdict from scratch, on a separate occasion from the Poole Harbour GPS-tracked beat above. Sailing hard on the wind, the boat made good roughly **45 degrees to the true wind**, at close to her hull displacement speed of **4.5 knots**. She **tacked through about 90 degrees**, picking up speed again with very little helm input beyond a gentle adjustment of the steering line, and **never failed to complete a tack** on the day of the trial.

But the same trial exposed the same limitation found elsewhere in this record: she enters a tack quickly, then as wind pressure comes off the sail she **drops level onto her flat bottom and slows sharply**, her momentum only just carrying her onto the new tack — judged unpromising for beating up narrow rivers. That is an independent, non-owner confirmation of the disadvantage the boat's own owner had already listed in the companion article in the same DCA Bulletin issue.[7]

The trial's 90-degree tacking angle sits at the high-pointing end of the two regimes described above, and lines up with the GPS trial's best single tack of 98 degrees rather than its 130-degree average — consistent with the light-air, flat-water conditions of the day: mostly **0-4 mph** wind, with only one spell of about **8 mph** after lunch, meaning the windward verdict rests on a single short period of usable breeze.

## A 2010 owner's pointing figures, and a heavy-weather failure to make headway

A stock boat fitted with the taller, shorter-footed sail could point to about **55-60 degrees** off the true wind by compass in flat water and light-to-moderate wind, worse still over the ground once leeway is allowed for.[8] In more testing conditions the same boat's windward ability fell away sharply: beating without a break for **two days** in **20-25 knot** winds and a **2-metre** sea gained only about **3 nm** of net progress in the intended direction before the attempt was abandoned for port.[8]

## Reefing timing, and why a heavily reefed sail points less well

A practical reefing schedule ties directly into windward ability: reefing typically begins around **15 knots**, deliberately over-reefed at first, then eased out a few inches at a time until it feels right, an adjustment that can be made mid-tack when working up a channel; a heavily reefed sail simply points less well than a fuller one, a trade-off accepted as the price of manageable loading rather than treated as a fault.[9] A further owner's own figures for the same taller sail put roughly **65%** of the sail area still set at 20 knots, falling to about **50%** by 25 knots, with 25 knots treated as the practical ceiling for attempting to sail to windward at all; beyond that, the only real options are to run off or to heave to, which works well under the small triangle of cloth this sail's peak leaves standing once fully rolled (see [furling system — stowing a taller sail once furled](/components/rig/furling.md#stowing-a-taller-sail-once-furled-a-storm-trysail-like-partial-reef) for the same leftover-triangle behavior documented from the construction side).[11] The more a sail is reefed, the less neatly the yard draws in close to the mast; a parrel fitted at the yard was floated, untried, as a possible fix.[11] Ballast trim was also raised as a possible explanation for weak pointing;[10] one stock boat's own ballast, confirmed sitting at the designed waterline (sometimes a little over), ruled out gross under-ballasting as the cause in that case.[11]

## A chop-and-tide-limited beat: the state of the water, not the wind, sets the limit

A further firsthand account illustrates how sea state, more than wind strength alone, can cap windward progress: beating about a mile against a force 4 wind and a 1.5-knot foul tide, and staying in water under 2 metres deep to dodge the worst of the current, put the boat into short, confused chop that constantly washed over the deck; speed over the ground held below 2 knots for several hours before the passage was finally made.[9] The standing conclusion this and similar experience support: a short, continual chop knocking the way off the boat, rather than the wind strength itself, is usually what makes windward progress genuinely difficult.[9]

## An upper limit: no windward progress attempted above about force 5

Paradox is not expected to make good windward progress at all above about force 5; rather than keep trying, the practical response most owners settle on is to shorten sail drastically, or to give up on sail entirely and motor instead (see [auxiliary outboard power — a force-5 windward limit and a give-up-and-motor policy](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-force-5-windward-limit-and-a-give-up-and-motor-policy)).[12]

## A tack asymmetry reported on "Odyssey" (2011)

A further owner-reported data point, distinct from the trials above: ["Odyssey"](/boats/odyssey.md#windward-pointing-and-a-comparison-against-other-boats-sailed) points markedly better on a starboard tack, an estimated 60 degrees off the wind, than to port, at only about 45-50 degrees — an asymmetry judged by eye rather than measured.[13]
