# Sailing characteristics and rig balance

See also [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) for deliberate storm-handling questions (sea-anchor rudder position, lying a-hull, maximum heel) distinct from the day-to-day tacking/trim issues covered here.

How the [Paradox](/paradox.md)'s [lug rig](/components/rig/index.md) balances on different points of sail — whether beating to weather generates significant weather helm, whether that can be counteracted through sail trim rather than by holding the tiller over, and how balance shifts as the boat heels — remains an open question in the archive, alongside a related, also-open [ballast-placement question](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).[1] It sits next to the [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md) finding that in **light air** the Paradox holds a course well enough, by tiller-rope friction alone, to leave the helm briefly — a light-air data point that does not by itself settle how she balances **to windward** or **fully powered up**.

## A firsthand tacking/grounding lesson

A Paradox can fail to complete a tack under real conditions, with real consequences. A three-day solo winter river cruise — overnight lows in the mid 20s°F, daytime highs in the mid 40s°F, sleet and snow beginning at departure — proved the boat "pretty comfortable" and handling "ok" even for a sailor still learning her. A close call yielded an explicit handling lesson: trying to claw off a lee shore, the boat came about too late and would not complete the tack, was pushed onto a 20 ft rock ledge, and grounded — requiring a push-off in bitterly cold water. The lesson: "be careful of a lee shore if you have to claw off into the wind."[2] This is the earliest recorded account of a Paradox failing to complete a tack in a lee-shore situation and grounding as a result — a data point on real-world handling limits that the weather-helm/heeling question above still leaves open.

Cabin comfort and ventilation notes from the same trip: rain fell for most of the three days, and the cabin kept the crew "high and dry" throughout, though it was a little uncomfortable for extended sitting. The ventilation system worked "too well" for cold-weather use — it needs to be covered for winter sailing, a practical complement to the design's vent-trunk/mast-partners arrangement (see [mast partners and vent trunk](/components/rig/mast.md)) and to the general [cabin heating](/operations/cabin-heating.md) discussion, which had not previously identified over-ventilation (as opposed to heat source) as a cold-weather problem.[2]

The same failure-to-complete-a-tack symptom has shown up on another boat, stuck head-to-wind in irons repeatedly one weekend in the Salcombe estuary, recoverable only with the yuloh — see [rig and sail — a speculative small jib](/components/rig/index.md#a-speculative-small-jib) for the fuller account and the small-jib idea it prompted.

## Real difficulty completing tacks, and successful (if inconsistent) jibing

The most detailed real-world account on record of Paradox tacking behavior comes from days of hard sailing aboard "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)". Her **pointing ability** was not a concern — she kept up with bigger boats, if not pointing quite as high — but her **bow would not reliably come around into the wind to start a new tack**, to the point of never completing a tack without using the motor to help bring the bow through. By contrast, jibing worked, though not reliably ("very few" successful attempts) — a marked contrast with a sloop rig, which gave no such trouble.[3]

This is a second, independent data point (after the lee-shore grounding above) of a Paradox failing to come about under a given set of conditions — attributed here to some sailing-technique or trim issue rather than a lee-shore/timing problem, but not reconciled with the generally positive tacking-angle figures in [pointing and tacking-angle expectations](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md) (90-110°, from the same "Whisper," two months earlier). Whether the cause is technique, ballast trim, sea state, or rig set-up is not resolved.

## Technique suggestions, a weight-distribution theory, and a full helm-balance debate

Tacking trouble like "Whisper"'s has drawn a range of practical technique tips and the fullest theoretical debate on record on hull trim and helm balance.

**Light-air technique.** Tacking in **light air** is sometimes frustrating even on a well-sorted boat, and can require **backwinding the main**. The standing pointing advice is that the [Paradox](/paradox.md) dislikes being sailed too close to the wind, and falling off a little to build speed before tacking usually lets her come around fine — a problem mainly in **chop or a running sea**, which slows her markedly. If caught **in irons**, the recommended recovery is to backwind the main and **motor/paddle in reverse** until back on the desired tack — slow, but potentially saves a boat from going ashore.[4] The same two standard suggestions recur elsewhere: try **falling off** to gain speed before tacking, and check whether the boat is trimmed **down by the head** (too much weight in the bow), which "can cause a boat to miss stays."[5]

**A full loaded-weight breakdown** for "Whisper" ran to **400 lb lead ballast** (most of it where the water tanks were meant to go), **65 lb outboard**, **190 lb** of the sailor's own body weight, **6.5 gal fuel**, **5 gal water**, and roughly **75 lb** of gear/food/ground tackle — an estimated **750 lb total**.[6] See [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for the boat's other ballast/trim data points this adds to.

**The weight-forward/weight-aft diagnosis.** A boat that won't come about is "almost always a victim of too much weight forward or too much rig aft" — a lesson drawn from a catamaran that would not tack until crew weight moved aft. The broader theoretical point: a sailboat should be **"astable"** — not a weathervane that points itself into the wind unassisted — and while a **neutral-to-weather helm** is correct once under way and gaining speed, the same is not true when the boat is stalled and pointed close to the wind. A Paradox is otherwise "known to function properly when trimmed and sailed by others," pointing to a trim/loading issue specific to a given boat rather than a design flaw. The suggested diagnostic experiment: temporarily load 500 lb first in the bow, then in the stern, and compare tacking behavior in each condition.[7]

**The counter-view, on lee helm and safety.** A boat's helm "should be neutral towards weather," so that letting go of the tiller lets her head up (slowly) into the wind rather than risk a **knock-down** — a boat needing much rudder deflection just to hold off the wind suffers "excess drag and accelerated wear on rudder fittings."[8]

**The fuller defense of the weight-aft diagnosis** holds that a boat trimmed head-to-wind-stable with the tiller free simply **will not tack** — it will only drift backward downwind (the image offered: a well-designed **badminton shuttlecock**, whose favorable weight-to-lateral-plane distribution is exactly why it "comes about really well"). Established rules for designers are cited as never recommending a sail plan set aft of the boat's combined center of displacement and lateral plane. Two regimes are distinguished: at speed and under way, a neutral-to-weather helm is correct, but getting a stalled or anchored boat to **fall off onto a new tack** is the opposite problem, needing the reverse weight bias. A **lee helm is not necessarily less safe** than a neutral one in a knockdown ("you're still going over" either way) — as in the well-known storm tactic of letting go the tiller and running off downwind under bare poles in a gale, a maneuver that depends on mass positioned well **upwind of windage**, i.e. functionally a strong lee helm. A secondary mechanism is also on record: **weather helm is partly produced by hull-shape asymmetry under heel** (the bow driving down / stern lifting), an effect judged **less pronounced on the Paradox** given its modest beam.[9]

The theoretical disagreement between the neutral-helm and weight-aft/lee-helm positions was aired but not settled, and the practical question of why "Whisper" specifically won't complete a tack remained unanswered beyond the general weight-forward/weight-aft diagnosis.

## A further firsthand account: aggressive trim, forward ballast, and wave timing (2017)

A newly launched boat's second sea trial reported the identical symptom: unable to complete a tack from starboard onto port, catching in irons every attempt, while gybing onto port instead cost very little ground — settling, from that trial on, into tacking only onto starboard and gybing onto port.[10] The remedy offered runs counter to the weight-aft diagnosis above rather than confirming it: sail the boat more aggressively, letting out a bit more sail and heeling harder to build speed before the turn; add two or three gallons of water as temporary ballast in the forepeak, or simply move body weight forward during the turn; and time the turn to coincide with the top of a wave. With experience, tacks are rarely missed at all once these are applied together.[11] A further owner's own long-standing habit agrees with the forward-weight half of that remedy: he sits well forward through a tack at all times, easing the boat round in light air and throwing it round hard in a moderate breeze, particularly in choppy water.[12]

## A second round: weathercocking, ballast, and rudder stall

The same weight-forward/weight-aft question resurfaced when "Whisper," sailed in light air, again stopped dead, nose into the wind, on a tack.[13] From experience aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)": too much weight forward makes a Paradox hang nose-to-wind on a tack, while too much weight aft keeps her from coming up into the wind at all.[14] Moving ballast aft after launch fixed the same problem on that boat, apparently settling it for a season, but the trouble recurred after adding more gear and a second anchor over a winter layup — pointing to too much ballast overall, correctable by experimenting with adding and removing ballast to isolate the cause.[15]

The same weight-aft diagnosis recurs elsewhere under the term "weathervaning," with two standard fixes: trim more weight into the boat's stern, or move the sail forward (raking the mast, or relocating the sail's attachment point).[16]

A complementary theory, drawn from published writing on sharpies: the lighter a boat's displacement, the less pronounced its weathercocking tendency, since low momentum lets the hull stop dead when tacking. A related **rudder-stall risk** is also on record — a large-rudder-area boat tempts the helmsman into applying too much rudder to force the bow through the wind before way is lost, turning the rudder into a brake; the recommended technique is to use as little rudder as possible to carve the turn while retaining way, falling off to regather speed if the boat slows too much, or kicking the stern over with an oar stroke or two.[17]

A counter-view holds the sharpie comparison overstated: at roughly **1,400 lb** and 14 ft, a Paradox is "hardly a lightweight Sharpie," and the behavior is more likely a balance issue — a sailing canoe shows the same weathercocking if trim is neglected. See [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-sharpie-theory-explains-her-handling) for the fuller question of how far sharpie theory actually applies to Paradox. The rudder is not a foil suited to high angles of attack and stalls easily.[18] Taken together the record airs competing theories (weight trim, sail position, hull momentum, rudder stall) without a single resolution specific to "Whisper," and no follow-up report survives of an on-the-water experiment to isolate the cause.

## Glen's CE/CLR diagnosis for lee helm, and a motor-weight tangent

The **opposite** symptom from the tacking trouble above is also on record: "Whisper" at one point needed constant **negative (lee) rudder** just to hold her on course, the amount varying by point of sail, with no room on the spars to shift the sail fore-or-aft to compensate.[19]

The most direct textbook-style explanation on record of helm balance: **lee helm** (needing negative rudder) means the boat's **center of effort (CE)** sits too far **forward** of its **center of lateral resistance (CLR)**, causing the bow to fall away from the wind. **Four possible fixes** apply, usable alone or in combination: **rake the mast aft**, if the rig allows it; **tie the halyard to the forward-most point on the yard**; **move weight aft**; or some blend of the three.[20] This is a more theoretical, textbook-grounded diagnosis than the practical weight-distribution/trim debate above — and notably diagnoses the **opposite** helm condition (lee helm, too little weather helm) from what that earlier debate mostly concerned.

A motor-weight theory runs alongside the sail-balance one: a big motor mounted on the stern is "especially bad" without enough load to sink the boat to her lines, echoing trouble on another micro caused by a heavy outboard hung too far aft.[21] "Whisper" carried a **55 lb motor** on the stern, called "part of my problem," with a search for a suitably light replacement turning up a **Yamaha 2.5** (about 37 lb) of uncertain adequacy and **Honda** as the only other option under consideration.[22] Neither weight is "that light either" — a **27 lb, 2.5 hp short-shaft Nissan** (used on a dinghy and a Sea Pearl trimaran, mounted low to the water) is a one-person-liftable motor that starts easily and pushes the trimaran to hull speed (5.5 kt); two-stroke motors run lighter than four-strokes, "though dirtier."[23] Whether the lee-helm symptom was actually a sail-balance, ballast, or motor-weight issue was not settled. See [Whisper](/boats/whisper.md) for the boat-level record, and [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for "Whisper"'s other loaded-weight and outboard data points this adds to.

A further diagnostic **experiment**, distinct from a direct fix: furl the sail and go under **bare poles** in a breeze — if the boat **rounds up into the wind**, that indicates **too much ballast forward**; then try again with the sail raised but not sheeted in tight, rudder neutral, and see whether she still rounds up (she should not, if trimmed correctly). The suggested remedy, if the bare-poles test rounds up, is simply to **move ballast aft**.[24]

### Glen's method for finding CE and CLR by hand

A halyard-position change is on record as having fixed helm balance aboard "Zoë" (see [boats/zoe.md — a Cumberland Sound cruise](/boats/zoe.md#the-skylight-and-chart-table-in-practical-use-a-cumberland-sound-cruise)), raising the question of how far in front of the CLR the CE actually sits, if at all.[25]

No numeric CLR has been worked out for "Zoë," but a **low-tech method** for finding both figures by hand is on record in full:

- **CLR**: cut a scale drawing of the underwater profile (hull plus any appendages — rudder, keel, etc. — with prop openings or similar cut away), fold it so it becomes stiff, and balance it on a knife edge; the balance point is the CLR.
- **CE**: cut a scale drawing of the sail, pin a weighted string (a ballpoint pen works) through each corner in turn and let it hang freely, tracing a line down the string each time; on Paradox's four-sided sail this yields a four-sided bounded area, and the CE lies at its center.[26]

The exercise has not actually been run for Paradox with the **rudder in the down position**, and the simple method has one known gap: it does **not** account for the [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md), which add roughly **2 sq ft** of lateral-resistance area of their own.[26]

The sail plan can be shifted fore-and-aft by repositioning the **halyard attach point** on the yard and by changing the **tack-fitting length**, to balance the helm or deliberately add weather helm. A safety point not recorded elsewhere in the archive: **lee helm is dangerous for a single-hander**, because if the helmsman falls overboard, the resulting loss of weight aft throws the boat into lee helm and she will **jibe off downwind away from him** — a reason some single-handers, following a Coast Guardsman's aside on the theme of drowned men being recovered with their fly open, make a habit of **urinating into a jug** rather than leaning over the side.[26]

No reader is recorded actually measuring "Whisper"'s or "Zoë"'s CE/CLR against each other, but this stands as the fullest general **method** the archive records for a builder to find both figures for their own boat.

## Dave Bolduc: directional stability as a deliberate trade-off, not a flaw

Long firsthand experience sailing **[Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)**, together with familiarity (short of ownership) with **Paradox** and **[Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md)**, generalized about trim and tacking across all three of Matt Layden's small-cruiser designs — the fullest cross-design comparison of tacking/maneuverability behavior in the archive.

**Trim practice on Little Cruiser.** The boat is ballasted "carefully": most heavy gear is kept **amidships**, with lighter items toward the ends — except when sailing to windward all day, when weight shifts slightly **forward** (mainly by having the second crew member "sit farther up"), and when running downwind in heavy air, when both crew shift **farther aft**. This is a concrete example of active, point-of-sail-dependent crew trim on a Layden boat, complementing the theoretical bow/stern-weight debate above for Paradox specifically.[27]

**Motor weight hurts tacking/performance.** A stern-mounted outboard **"definitely affects the boat's performance adversely."** A **3.3 hp Mercury (28 lb)** was, in this judgment, probably too heavy for Little Cruiser's **narrow transom**; for shorter trips a lighter 2 hp motor, or leaving the motor off entirely and using the [yuloh](/components/yuloh.md) instead, worked better. This is an independent, cross-design echo of the outboard-weight/tacking concerns raised for Paradox specifically (a 65 lb four-stroke outboard) in the debate above and in [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md).[27]

**Paradox is "very stiff tracking" — a deliberate design trade-off.** The clearest design-level explanation on record for the tacking difficulty debate above: Paradox **"is indeed very stiff tracking and at times hard to tack at slow speeds,"** a consequence of her **wide transom** and **large rudder** — slamming the rudder hard over "acts like a big brake" rather than turning the boat. The recommended technique is to **nudge the tiller over gradually**, a little at a time, rather than putting the helm hard over, to complete a tack. This is not a flaw but a direct consequence of [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s design priority for Paradox specifically: **"Matt's emphasis in Paradox was to get as much directional stability as possible."**[27]

**Contrast with Little Cruiser and Swamp Thing.** Layden's other two designs, **Swamp Thing** and **Little Cruiser**, "tack more easily," but as a trade-off "tend to wander more" (i.e. sacrifice some of Paradox's straight-line directional stability for easier maneuvering). **Swamp Thing** — a favorite-sailing Layden design (see [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md)) — is, "believe it or not," the **most maneuverable of Matt's designs** thanks to her [chine runners](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md): she "tacks on a dime at almost" [the remainder of this message was not recovered from the archive].[27]

**A 2015 recollection: the technique traced directly to Matt Layden.** The same ease-the-tiller method was retraced, more than a decade later, to its origin. Slamming the rudder over hard, the way one would in a small dinghy, caused the same trouble at first; it cleared up only after watching Matt Layden himself sail his own original Paradox from inside her cabin and noticing he moved the tiller far more slowly — copying that alone made the boat sail much better. The same technique has since worked on both Little Cruiser and Enigma, failing only when the boat is already moving too slowly, or in a strong headwind with a short chop.[28]

**A direct, four-design ranking.** The same source went on to rank all four of Matt Layden's small-cruiser designs against each other for the first time. For tacking speed, Paradox is the **slowest**, Little Cruiser next, and Enigma and Swamp Thing the **quickest**; for holding a course completely hands-off, the ranking reverses — Paradox **"wins hands down,"** Enigma and Swamp Thing are "pretty good too," and Little Cruiser needs a lot more constant attention. Matt Layden himself once suggested fitting Little Cruiser with chine runners for the best of both worlds, a change never made given a microcruiser's already-scarce interior space; **[Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md)** made the equivalent change on his own **[Hogfish](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)** designs and has been happy with it.[29] This is the fullest direct confirmation on record that Paradox's own tacking reluctance sits at one end of a spectrum the whole Layden family shares to different degrees, rather than a fault peculiar to Paradox or to any one of the hydrodynamic mechanisms argued over above.

**The trade-off restated in the designer's own terms.** The same source put the trade-off plainly: every boat reflects the compromises its designer makes, and Paradox is no exception — Matt Layden's original intent for this microcruiser was a simple, seaworthy craft that excels at holding a course to help a single-handed sailor cover long distances, an aim judged completely achieved, so that the occasional slow tack is "simply part of the boat's handling characteristics" rather than a flaw, managed by keeping speed up and putting the helm over more delicately through the turn. A tentative explanation, offered without a designer's certainty, was floated for why Swamp Thing and Enigma — which also carry chine runners — tack more swiftly than the bigger boat: their lighter weight, narrower transoms, and proportionally smaller rudders, though which of those factors actually matters, if any, was left open.[30]

Taken together, this reframes the tacking debate above: "Whisper"'s reported difficulty completing tacks is not necessarily a sign of a boat mistrimmed or an aberrant hull, but at least partly an inherent, designed-in property of Paradox's hull form (wide transom, large rudder) traded deliberately for directional stability — a property shared to a lesser degree by all Layden boats but most pronounced on Paradox, and least pronounced on Swamp Thing. This does not resolve the specific tacking trouble reported aboard "Whisper," but supplies an independent, credible explanation (design intent, not necessarily poor trim) alongside the individual weight-distribution/helm-balance theories above.

## How a standing lug rig tacks

A Bermudan or Gunter rig has a pivot at the gooseneck and the foot of the yard that lets the sail swing around the mast when tacking. Paradox's [lugsail rig](/components/rig/index.md) works differently: the rig is **designed to stay on one side of the mast permanently** — the boom and yard do **not** swing around to the opposite side on a tack at all.[31] This confirms, from the design's actual behavior, the standing-lug configuration described in [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md).

## Center of effort, rudder stall, and telltale placement as the real tacking variables

Whether the lug rig's own geometry — rather than trim or hull form — explains reported slow tacking turns on whether the forward part of the sail (the portion ahead of the mast, standing in for a jib) is simply too small, and whether moving the halyard attachment further aft (see [rig](/components/rig/index.md#quantifying-the-new-sailplans-effect-on-boom-furling-geometry)) would help.[32]

The more useful mental model, though, is the sail's overall **center of effort/center of sail area**, not a fore/aft split around the mast — proper balance is found empirically by experimenting with halyard placement once the boat is correctly trimmed to her lines (see the [hand method for finding CE](#glens-method-for-finding-ce-and-clr-by-hand) above). As with most catboats, coming about struggles if the **rudder stalls** from being pushed to leeward too quickly — a particular risk on Paradox given how large a fraction of the boat's total lateral area her rudder represents — and light air makes the problem worse. Over-sheeting causes the same struggle. The recommended technique is to sail a catboat rig **through the tack more slowly** than a marconi fin-keeler, tending the sheets throughout so the luff is just curling or fluttering, and to add **telltales** at the luff, leech, and draft, plus a second set just aft of the mast to read flow where the sail bears against the spar. The balanced lug is capable of "fantastic shape" with less active control than other rigs, being naturally low-aspect with both CE and center of lateral resistance sitting low — noting also that a sail cut by a loft needs noticeably more draft than an equivalent sail on a lighter boat, per Matt Layden's own original "new sail" specification.[33]

A complementary view puts the tacking-speed question in perspective from the hull-form side: compared with a centerboard dinghy, Paradox is **sluggish coming about**, a consequence of being a **heavy-displacement craft** (14 ft, over half a ton) with the functional equivalent of a **full-length keel** — likely tacking just the way the numbers would predict for that hull type,[34] an explanation that sits alongside, rather than replaces, the weight-distribution and rudder-stall diagnoses given elsewhere in this article, and echoes the "stiff tracking, hard to tack at slow speeds" verdict [above](#dave-bolduc-directional-stability-as-a-deliberate-trade-off-not-a-flaw).

Windward passage speed lags a dinghy's in the same way, distinct from the tacking maneuver itself: sailing in company with other boats on a course to windward, the boat is usually the last of the fleet to arrive — "but when I do I am usually on my second or third mug of coffee."[35]

Separately, "Whisper" **will tack without the outboard motor** aboard (68 lb — a different, heavier motor than the 65 lb one referenced elsewhere in the weight breakdown above; the archive does not reconcile the two figures). Tacking **with** the outboard fitted proved harder to work out, trim again being the key variable, echoing the same underlying motor-weight-vs-trim problem generalized about for Little Cruiser above.[31]

A related pair of handling questions remains open: how a Paradox handles **large motorboat wakes in shallow water** — whether a change of course is needed to deal with them or a steady course can be held — and how she behaves running in a **following sea**.[36] See [heavy-weather tactics — an open question on wakes and following seas](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#an-open-question-on-motorboat-wakes-and-following-seas) for the related storm-handling material this sits alongside.

## A jet-ski collision at anchor, and praise for the hull

At anchor around 11:30 one night, a **drunk jet-skier collided with the side of "Whisper."** The jet-ski's speed could not be judged directly, but based on how much the boat rolled and the scratches left behind, the rider "wasn't idling." The jet-skier, sensing the reaction aboard, "took off." **Nobody was hurt**, and Whisper suffered only **cosmetic damage** — a positive data point on the boat's having weathered the impact without structural harm: "I can verify the sound design of the hull!!!!"[3] This is a rare, incidental real-world data point on hull toughness under an unplanned impact, distinct from the deliberate rock-ledge grounding recorded above.

## Pounding: rare at anchor, occasional underway

Asked directly whether Paradox pounds, either at anchor or under sail — the question prompted by [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s newer [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) design, which departs from Paradox's flat sharpie bottom with a **"V" bottom at the bow** — three separate owners reported the same experience: pounding at anchor is essentially unknown, and underway it is rare, at most occasional spray tossed rather than a hull slamming into waves. One owner speculated the V-bow on Enigma was Matt's way of cutting down that spray and picking up a little extra speed, rather than a response to any real pounding problem on the flat-bottomed original.[37][38][39][40] Sailing experience with both [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) and [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) as well as Paradox confirmed the same experience across all three heavy-displacement, wet-forefoot hulls, with at most an occasional slap at anchor in real rough conditions — but noted the one exception found: motoring straight into a short head sea could set up pounding in Little Cruiser, a problem corrected simply by having both crew sit on one side of the boat, heeling her enough to restore a "V" to the hull's presentation to the waves.[41]

The comparison to Matt Layden's newer, lighter 12 ft design was worked out in more detail: Paradox's greater displacement and lesser rocker keep its forefoot submerged more of the time than the newer design's, which is one reason a pounding problem is more likely on the lighter boat and why its bow needed the "V" that stock Paradox does not. Phil Bolger's 15 ft "Micro" was cited as the cautionary case for the same failure mode — enough rocker to pound badly, prompting Bolger to retrofit his own V-bottomed bow to several of his flat-bottomed designs in later years. A further, more speculative case was raised for the newer 12 ft design specifically: pressed into service towed as a ship's lifeboat or tender astern of a larger vessel above hull speed, its V bow was thought likely to help with tracking as well as reducing pounding.[42][43] The original question was considered settled by these replies.[44]

## A 2013 recurrence: trim, stern-anchoring, and towing as the real variables

Asked again in 2013 whether a flat bottom makes a Paradox more prone to slapping at anchor than a V-bowed hull would be, the settled account above gained further detail rather than a contradiction. With the boat properly trimmed — the stem an inch or so below the waterline — slapping essentially does not occur, since keeping the stem submerged keeps air from getting under it in the first place; what reliably brings slapping on is anchoring **by the stern** rather than the bow, since the transom then only kisses the water and small waves can find the gap needed to trap air beneath it. The worst slapping on record came not at anchor at all but under tow (and, by the same logic, likely under motor), and was cured simply by leaning over the side to heel the boat and break the flat presentation to the water.[45] A separate flat-bottomed boat, trimmed down to her own waterline, reported no slapping whatsoever under any of sail, anchor, or engine, even motoring through choppy inlet chop.[46]

A further firsthand comparison, from an owner who has sailed Little Cruiser, Enigma, and Paradox alike, put a number on which boat actually slaps: Little Cruiser rarely slaps at anchor, only in genuinely big waves, while Enigma's V bow slaps occasionally both at anchor and underway — put down not to the bow shape itself but to Enigma's shallower immersion, since she is lighter and rides higher than a heavier boat like Paradox. The V bow's real practical benefit, in the same account, was sculling rather than pounding: it keeps the bow from blowing off to leeward as easily while working to windward. Its most likely original purpose was simply to stiffen Enigma's already-thin 3/8 in bottom panel, a second, competing explanation alongside the spray/speed theory already recorded above.[47]

## Handling and feel from microcruising.com

A first-person "what it feels like to sail her" layer complements the tacking/trim debate above. The pilothouse arrangement is central to this account: sailing from inside "gives one the feeling that they are driving a 'convertible'" — when it gets rough, the hatch simply closes and the boat keeps going, carrying no foul-weather gear as a result. The boat's short waterline gives a genuine windward advantage in a seaway: "because we are so short, we can often fit in between the wave troughs that larger boats aren't quite able to bridge." Once heeled, the narrow beam and hard chine "present a sharp V to the oncoming waves," which along with low windage and a heavy loaded weight lets the boat "maintain momentum while still sailing nicely upwind."[48]

On surfing: in 8-ft seas with 25 knots blowing from astern, "Paradox and Little Cruiser can probably surf at 8 or 9 knots in short bursts," applying corrective rudder as the stern lifts to counter the boat's tendency to yaw.[48] Groundings are routine rather than dramatic, given the roughly 9-inch draft — "a simple push" is all that is usually needed to get going again — and the mast lowers on a tabernacle to duck under low bridges without having to wait for an opening.[48][49] The same shallow draft let Little Cruiser negotiate shallow water and bump over sandbars between the Exuma islands, and both boats crossed the Gulf Stream to Bimini in sloppy 5-7 ft seas.[50] See also [pointing and tacking angles](/operations/pointing-and-tacking-angles.md) and [hull speed](/operations/hull-speed.md).

## Why the seat's position damps the felt motion

A striking feature of the pilothouse layout is how little of the boat's motion the helmsman actually feels. A film of "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" beating to windward in a force 5 shows her pitching quite violently on screen, yet none of that motion was felt aboard, because the single helm seat sits close to the boat's **centre of pitch rotation**. The corollary is that a second person, sitting further forward in the cabin, feels far more of the motion: on a two-up Helford-to-Falmouth passage, whoever sat forward kept getting seasick, prompting the crew to repeatedly swap places.[51] The same felt-versus-filmed gap has been noticed for **rolling** as well as pitching, watching back a video of beating into a force 5 with no sense, at the time, of how much the boat was actually moving.[52]

An independent report from a different Paradox confirms the same disconnect and extends it further. A short video of that boat sailing downwind under a scandalised rig left her sailor surprised by how much rocking it showed once filmed — "you don't notice it when you're on the boat" — and, weighing the two accounts together, called it simply one of the trade-offs of a boat this short: a hull this size pitches and rolls even in quite small chop, a fact not appreciated before the build began. The motion is not confined to sailing: it happens at anchor as much as under way, which is reason enough to seek out the most sheltered nook available rather than accept it as unavoidable. A bigger boat's own steadiness can mislead in the other direction — taking morning tea aboard a friend's 32 ft keeler moored in the same anchorage, that sailor assumed from below decks that the wind had dropped, only to find on deck that it had not; the calm felt below was the bigger boat's own greater stability, not a change in the weather.[53] This complements the wider two-up finding that the forward berth is the uncomfortable one (see [crew size and cabin fit](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md)) and the "convertible" pilothouse feel documented above (see [AYRS meeting talk](/references/ayrs-meeting-video.md)).

## An independent trial: hove-to by default, and self-steering by sail balance

An independent sail trial aboard "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" adds an outside data point to the [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md) finding noted in the introduction above — evidence gathered on a first acquaintance with the boat rather than built up over years of an owner's own accumulated habit. With no sail set and the rudder cleated up, she was found to lie **hove-to by default, side-on to the wind** — not a deliberate manoeuvre, but simply her resting state. Under way, pointed where the sailor wants to go and trimmed to best advantage, she **holds a straight line unattended** and keeps holding it until the wind itself shifts, the sail's own balance acting **"like windvane steering"** with no constant tiller correction needed.

Tacking needed almost no helm: she picked up speed on the new tack while the helmsman did little beyond a gentle adjustment of the steering line — no **"Lee-Ho!,"** no ducking, no risk of damaged shins or cracked skulls, and no risk of capsize. Off the wind she proved **very stable**. The **99.75 sq ft lug sail** hoists from inside the cabin so easily it looks like sleight of hand, and both steering line and sheet fall readily to hand from a relaxed seat, lounging equally easy to windward or to leeward; the helmsman sits **facing forward** — no trouble for the young or supple, but a real boon to anyone with an arthritic neck.

The evidential weight sits in who is reporting it: this is not an owner's accumulated trim skill, refined over seasons, but a sailor new to the boat getting the same hands-off, self-correcting behaviour immediately, on a single day's acquaintance — independent confirmation that the trait belongs to the design itself rather than to any particular owner's practice. See ["Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md) for the fuller trial, including its windward-performance verdict.[54]

## Sailing standing up in the hatch

Because the cabin *is* the cockpit, the sailor can work the boat **standing in the hatchway**: "The sailor can also stand while sailing, with his arms and torso extending through the cabin hatch. Some sailors actually read a book while standing in the cabin hatchway." A Paradox owner elaborates: "in all but extreme conditions it is quite possible to sail standing up. I quite often stand leaning with my elbows on the hatch cover with a book in front of me… I have even been known to climb onto the aft deck and dangle my feet in the water over the transom, during a quiet downwind run."[55] This complements the "convertible"/pilothouse feel documented above.

## A one-tack-fluttering puzzle, and a contrary experience

"Whisper"'s sail set well on every point of sail except one: on a **starboard tack**, or any point where the boom and yard lie **against the mast**, getting the tell-tales to fly correctly left a slight **flutter on the leading edge**; tightening the sheet enough to stop the flutter then threw the tell-tales off, with no detectable difference in speed either way.[56]

Flutter is more usually seen on the **leech** rather than the luff, possibly from a stretched luff or a halyard needing tightening — though it need not be a cause for concern. A broader observation from years sailing both [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) and Matt Layden's own Paradox: **one tack is always better than the other**, because the mast disrupts airflow over the sail on whichever side it sits against — "just the tradeoff you have to live with" on a balanced lug rig with no parrel.[57] This matches, from a different angle, a **full-knot tacking-speed asymmetry** found aboard "Zoë" (see [rig and sail — a tacking speed asymmetry](/components/rig/index.md)) — between the two, independent builders confirm the lug rig sails measurably or noticeably differently depending on which side the sail lies against the mast.

A contrary data point runs the **opposite** way to that general rule: on one boat, the **best sailing performance comes on the port tack** (sail against the mast), with no trouble tacking from that position, whereas on the **starboard tack** (sail in free air) she still occasionally gets "stuck" partway through a tack and has to try again. The same whole-sail flutter described above shows up there too, and actually gives the **best performance**, sailing with the luff "just occasionally fluttering" — though the sail may simply need attention.[58] This is the first report on record of a builder finding **better**, not worse, performance and easier tacking with the sail held against the mast, complicating the simple "one tack is always better" rule the other two data points had suggested.

A 2011 exchange added independent, cross-design corroboration to the port-tack observation above. One owner, thinking aloud, wondered whether the boat sails better to windward on the port tack, unsure if it was real or imagination;[59] a second owner confirmed the same impression from his own boat, speculating the point at which the sail starts to stall is simply more clearly defined on that tack.[60] A newcomer sailing a high-peaked lug sail on an Oughtred Puffin — not a Paradox — reported the identical asymmetry: better, more controllable windward sailing in strong winds specifically when the gaff/yard lies against the mast, theorizing it comes from more induced twist in the sail rigged that way.[61] The exchange concluded the effect is most likely a general feature of the standing lug rig itself, common to Paradox and other lug-rigged boats alike, rather than anything peculiar to Paradox's own hull or sail.[62]

A 2012 recurrence gave the asymmetry a mechanical explanation distinct from any of the above: on one boat, much better performance on the port tack traced at least partly to a sheeting angle that encourages over-sheeting specifically on the starboard tack — recognizing this, the fix was simply to release the sheet further whenever tacking onto starboard.[63] A further owner reported "a very noticeable difference" between tacks aboard his own boat without offering an explanation, a further data point for how widely the asymmetry recurs across separately built boats.[64]

A 2016 correspondent, partway through his own 10% scale-up build, proposed eliminating the asymmetry altogether by sighting the sail on the mast's starboard side so it always lies along the centreline, reasoning this would equalise pointing on both tacks at the cost of a couple of blocks to route the tack line and releasing it whenever the furled sail is stowed on the side deck.[65] The idea drew a skeptical technical answer: a lug sail can be re-rigged to the other side of the mast, but the boat already carries asymmetric performance with the sail fixed on one side, with better performance expected on whichever tack keeps the mast to windward — normally the starboard tack, already the preferred long tack under the right-of-way rules — and shifting the sail to leeward of the mast on every tack might be workable only in light winds.[66] A second reply was frankly skeptical of the whole idea: switching the sail's side risked a banged head, since dropping the sail to the side deck instantly is a valued feature of the fixed rig, and the correspondent did not recognize the underlying problem at all on his own boat.[67]

The same correspondent returned with a related worry — the boat's own reluctance to tack from port to starboard, previously answered by shifting weight forward through the tack — and asked whether a centered sail might cure that too.[68] The answer was more discouraging than for the pointing question: a centered sail was expected to make tacking worse rather than better, since the boat can usually be persuaded to fall off onto the starboard tack regardless; the difficulty is rarely serious and mostly shows up when not concentrating, needs a bit of speed carried into the tack in choppy water, and above about Force 6 is better handled by wearing round than by tacking at all.[69]

A further data point ran opposite the general port-tack-is-better finding recorded above: one boat sails distinctly better to windward on the port tack, mast to leeward, crediting a better-supported luff, but doubted moving the sail to the mast's other side would change anything at all, reasoning the same center of lateral effort runs down through the mast foot regardless of which side the cloth flies — much as a biplane flies evenly on a single wing.[70]

A different fix altogether emerged from a sail replacement. One boat used to show the same tack-to-tack imbalance, but a newer sail cut with a slightly concave luff — self-supporting, needing less halyard tension — brought both tacks to about even performance, a further, independent mechanism alongside the sheeting-angle fix already on record above; no advantage was seen in moving the sail to the mast's other side regardless, since it would only complicate furling and give up the fixed rig's instant stow to the side deck.[71] The concave cut drew a further, unresolved theoretical objection: a standing lug, the type Paradox carries, would be expected to do better with the mast to leeward and a balanced lug the opposite, with the mast to windward, and a straight and a concave luff would be expected to sail identically, which this case did not bear out; how much concavity a luff can carry before the sail's own performance degrades was left open, floated as a possible route to curing lee helm.[72] The concave cut traced to its own sailmaker's own judgement, an old hand at lug and junk rig rather than the computer-cutting programs tuned for a Bermudan sail's pointed head; full detail on choosing a sailmaker for a rectangular sail is at [components/rig/sail.md](/components/rig/sail.md#buying-vs-building-a-sail-recommendations-and-firsthand-accounts).[73]

## A new owner's lee-helm and immersion questions

A newly relocated owner sailing "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" — bought secondhand and shipped to Norway, see [people/robert-biegler.md](/people/robert-biegler.md) — reported the same lee-helm symptom on her first two short outings: compensating with the rudder produced a lot of leeway, roughly **25 degrees** on the first sail. On the second outing, with a bit more wind (5-8 kt) and sitting to leeward, the combined effect of immersing the [chine runner](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) more deeply and shifting the sail's center of effort further to leeward eliminated the lee helm and cut the leeway to roughly **10 degrees**. At the time the boat carried only **140 kg** of ballast and no cruising stores, well short of a full loaded cruising trim; the open question left was whether the deeper immersion a full cruising load brings would finish the job of curing the lee helm and improve pointing further.[74]

Two independent replies confirmed deeper immersion and correct trim are indeed the fix, converging with the CE/CLR diagnosis already on record above. Sailing "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)" to windward needs only a very tiny amount of weather helm, on any point of sail; the same trim that gives that light weather helm also gives less leeway, and the key is not to sheet the sail in too tightly — treating it, especially close-hauled, as if it were a junk sail with the wind striking the cloth at barely 2-3 degrees of incidence rather than hauled hard in. Lee helm signals a bow riding too high: trimming with more ballast to sink the stem further is the fix, and a correctly trimmed boat both carries less leeway and lets the designed waterline exit cleanly at the transom without drag, with the stem sitting some 3-4 inches under water. Built to plan, a Paradox carries **180 kg/397 lb of lead ballast and 70 kg/154 lb of water ballast** — a specific target figure against which the 140 kg reported above is markedly short.[75]

A second reply gave a concrete figure for how much ballast a Paradox the size of "[Zero](/boats/aussie-paradox.md)" needs to sail well on all points with stores aboard: **200 kg**, an amount that still demands some agility to work the foredeck when setting the anchor.[76]

## A 14-degree heel target, and near-neutral helm running

A design-level heel-angle figure, distinct from the maximum-safe-heel data recorded on [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#rudder-position-under-a-sea-anchor), sets the boat's optimum working angle for windward performance: chine runners are most effective at about **14 degrees** of heel, ballasted so the windward chine runner just breaks the surface — at that angle the transom, apart from a "smidgen," clears the water entirely.[77] A closely matching, independently measured figure gives **14.5 degrees**, at a wind speed of 10 kt (Force 3) and a displacement of 640 kg (1,410 lb), as the point where "her windward chine runner will skim the surface of the water. She is at her optimum."[78] For the underlying lateral-resistance mechanism, see [chine runners: how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md).

A related tenderness comparison, made against a different small boat trimmed the same day: "by comparison with 'Faith' I found she was more tender, but like the former she hardened up when heeling at about **15 degrees**" — a close, corroborating figure alongside the 14/14.5-degree chine-runner target above, taken from a stiffness rather than a lateral-resistance perspective.[79]

Running before the wind produces a further, counter-intuitive balance finding, echoing the general self-steering-while-running record already documented (see [self-steering — an Autohelm 800 fitted to two boats, and dated firsthand course-holding instances](/operations/self-steering.md#an-autohelm-800-fitted-to-two-boats-and-dated-firsthand-course-holding-instances)): although the sail's centre of effort sits far off the centreline running, which would suggest weather helm, a Paradox in fact carries "virtually neutral helm" and can hold a course unattended for minutes at a time in a smooth sea and steady wind, "even if the crew changes position."[78] The same neutral balance is stated as the design's own working target on the wind too, independent of point of sail: **"She will sail best with a neutral helm."**[80]

## Weather helm strengthens with wind and sail carried, and reefing is the standard fix

The [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md) finding that a Paradox typically holds herself hands-off in light air does not extend unmodified into a breeze: carrying too much sail for the wind actually blowing brings on a marked weather helm that turns the boat sharply up into the wind rather than letting her hold her course. The fix on record is simply to reef — taking in sail restores the light, easily-held helm rather than fighting the round-up tendency by force on the tiller.[81]

## Reefing downwind for comfort, distinct from the weather-helm fix

A separate, comfort-driven case for reefing off the wind: in a following wind it is often more relaxing to reef, giving up as much as half the sail area; the loss barely shows in boat speed, but it removes the stress of carrying full sail hard downwind — with the sail simply rehoisted again on heading back up onto the wind.[82]

## A 2018 recurrence: tiller fatigue running, and two rudder-position ideas

An owner sailing informally in a local wooden-boat regatta — expecting to be the slowest and smallest boat entered, but instead keeping up easily and passing two others — reported a symptom distinct from the tacking/weathercocking trouble discussed above: the tiller stayed "always active," rarely still especially at speed, tiring to hold and constantly pulling one way or the other, sometimes by large amounts. An interim fix extended the tiller into the cabin on a crude pole rather than using the plans' rope steering, avoiding the sore hands the ropes caused, though not a final solution; left open whether the boat's balance was simply incorrect, or too much weight sat aft.[83]

The diagnosis offered: probably not well trimmed. Two Paradoxes sail long tacks reaching or beating entirely hands-off the ropes; running downwind used to mean sweating and working hard, until learning to reduce sail fixed it — the boat goes just as fast downwind under less canvas and handles far more easily, digging in less at the bow. This restated and extended an already-established forward-ballast tacking tip (see [above](#a-further-firsthand-account-aggressive-trim-forward-ballast-and-wave-timing-2017)) with further detail: a heavy battery and heavy spare anchors are already carried forward on both boats, and more weight forward also improves tacking, rarely falling into irons anymore. Tightening the steering ropes helps too, aided by a short elastic tied on to take up slack, and a tightening clamp holds the ropes temporarily for a break.[84]

A second reply came at trim from a different angle: a correctly trimmed Paradox looks slightly bow-down, not level or bow-up as many photographed boats — including this respondent's own, on his first few outings — appear; a boat sitting level or bow-up "won't sail well." Read the tiller fatigue as a specifically running-downwind symptom, since the boat "tends to roll" running, with the effect at higher, surf-capable swells still untested. Floated an untried idea: raising the rudder to about 30 degrees to move the hull's lateral resistance aft slightly.[85]

An outside comment, from an owner of an unrelated small cruiser (a Potter) built to carry weight in its own cuddy, offered the textbook mechanism behind both replies at once: a bow riding high moves the centre of effort aft and gives lee helm; on his own boat, not getting enough weight far enough forward kept the chine out of the water and made her sail markedly worse to windward.[86]

Photographic evidence followed, confirming the bow-down diagnosis with a concrete figure: transom just kissing the water, the waterline sitting right at the rudder bolt, and the bow fully immersed is what correct trim looks like, achieved in this case by moving about 30 kg of lead forward of bulkhead 2.[87] The owner who had raised the original tiller-fatigue question reported the difficulty ran well past mild irritation: running nearly downwind at a sustained 7 knots — the fastest sail on record and well over hull speed — waves washing under the hull made her hard to keep on course, and three hours of that left him needing several days ashore to recover, "exhausting" even as "the best fun I've had in years."[88]

A theory followed for why running downwind produces this particular fight, distinct from the reaching/beating trim question above: at speeds well over hull speed the chine runners may generate large, alternating vortices — one runner riding deep and dragging the boat toward its own side, then detaching as the boat slews, with buoyancy and inertia carrying the roll on over to the opposite runner in turn — a rolling, slewing motion unlike a conventional hull's clean surf down a wave face. The prescription drawn from the theory restates the forward-ballast/reduced-sail solution already established above: reduce sail as much as possible running downwind, since the alternative is arriving at the destination barely any sooner but physically exhausted.[89] See [chine runners — analogies and speculation](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#analogies-and-speculation) for this theory alongside the tree's other unproven chine-runner hypotheses.

A further hypothesis followed from the owner experiencing the difficulty firsthand: his own chine runners are sanded slightly concave on their top edge, shaped that way for what looked like better hydrodynamics, and this may itself be contributing to the problem. His own plan going forward matches the one recommended above — reduce sail and move more weight forward — using the anchor chain already carried aboard as the added forward ballast.[90]

## No Portsmouth Yardstick rating on record

No Paradox has a registered **Portsmouth Yardstick** rating — the handicap system used to let dissimilar one-design boats race against each other on a level footing — and no attempt to obtain one for the hull and standard sail plan is recorded.[91] The idea drew a considered decline rather than an answer: competitive sailing runs against the whole point of owning a Paradox, since arriving quicker or slower, or not at all, is beside the point of a boat built as a comfortable home rather than a racer.[92]

A 2011 recurrence of the same underlying question asked specifically about a **PHRF** rating for informal club round-the-cans racing rather than Portsmouth Yardstick;[93] the answer was the same — no one is thought to have worked one out.[94] A firsthand reply a few days later made the question moot regardless of any handicap figure: the PHRF fleet nearest one owner's own home carries a **20 ft minimum length**, excluding a stock 13 ft Paradox from competing at all.[95]

## Draft, heel, and speed across conditions, from a stock owner's firsthand account

Asked broadly how shallow a Paradox can sail, what heel angle is typical, and how she performs across a range of wind strengths,[96] an owner answered from direct experience: she has sailed in under a foot of water, though the chine tends to rub bottom if not kept flat; close-hauled in a good breeze, about 20 degrees of heel is optimum, and more than that feels overcanvassed; in light air, off the wind, she outsails many conventional boats of a similar size, but loses that edge in fluky conditions, where her weight keeps her from accelerating as quickly as a lighter boat; hard on the wind she is generally slower than everything else on the water, bar a West Wight Potter; and in 15 knots of wind she reaches hull speed easily.[97] A separate reply to the same question reported a GPS observation of one particular Paradox running just past her own hull speed; see [Ironsides — a GPS-measured brush with hull speed](/boats/ironsides.md#a-gps-measured-brush-with-hull-speed).

A further recurrence of the same draft-at-heel question drew concrete figures: at about 20 degrees of heel — judged the best sailing angle — the chine runs roughly 18 in below the waterline, though the rudder, extending some 30 in down, hits bottom first. If the chine runner does touch bottom while sailing shallow water with the rudder partly raised, spilling the wind from the sail immediately gives 9 in of clearance under the hull — a technique that has taken a Paradox down a channel on a broad reach in under a foot of water.[98]

## Reefed-sail tacking trouble, and a chine-runner-as-keel theory (2015)

A 2015 recurrence returned to the tacking question from two directions at once. One owner reported that reluctance to tack is, for him, the single most frustrating thing about sailing his own Paradox — she tacks fine in a moderate wind and smooth water, but always refuses once well reefed in stronger conditions; unsure whether hull shape or the sail's centre of effort sitting far aft was to blame, he tried moving the halyard back along the yard a few inches, limited by how little clearance is left near the masthead once it is hoisted, and noticed no difference in her handling.[99] A separate exchange the same week, prompted by a photographed Paradox variant built with no chine runners at all, floated the theory that the runners themselves may function rather like a long keel for tacking purposes, following a report that [Minnow](/boats/enuf.md)'s new owner found her reluctant to tack without help from the sculling oar — see [people/john-starkie.md](/people/john-starkie.md#chine-runners-and-hull-geometry) for the fuller exchange, and [the catamaran-and-leeboard comparison it also drew](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy).

That report grew into its own thread, "Paradox tacking difficulties," which ran through several competing explanations without settling on one: the chine-runner-as-keel theory above, a submerged-forefoot theory, and a vortex-inhibition theory — see [how chine runners work — a third strand: vortex inhibition](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy) and [sharpie lineage — whether sharpie theory explains her handling](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#whether-sharpie-theory-explains-her-handling) for those in full. On technique rather than cause, the thread converged more plainly: keep the speed up through a tack, since in choppy water way is lost coming up into the wind and the boat simply stops, and in a river or other constricted water there may not be room to build speed between tacks before way runs out altogether — in calm water, even in very light wind, tacking is never a problem.[100] Tacking in rough water can still be done, but it needs preparing for — bearing off first to build a bit of speed, especially when heavily reefed.[101] One untested suggestion was to keep the body toward the outside of the turn rather than the inside, heeling the boat further for a deliberate "roll tack" to help her round up, though Paradox's hard chines and narrow hull were doubted to leave much room for that kind of weight shift to work.[102] A separate, untried idea borrowed from junk-rig practice: run a control line to the boom's inner end at the tack and pull the tack aft through the turn as junk sailors do, easing the sail forward again once on the new tack, on the reasoning that this alone should make Paradox tack almost automatically.[103] This converged technique-based diagnosis echoes, and gains a firsthand Layden provenance from, the older account above of easing rather than slamming the tiller through a tack (see [Dave Bolduc: directional stability as a deliberate trade-off, not a flaw](#dave-bolduc-directional-stability-as-a-deliberate-trade-off-not-a-flaw)).

The same thread carried a firsthand illustration of the difficulty rather than a fix: riding out four days of 30-40 mph wind at anchor in the Newtown River, Isle of Wight, waiting for a favourable slant to continue west, one owner watched, enviously, a small junk-rigged Corribee tack her way out of the river's narrow stretch in a Force 6, in bursts of 5 to 10 seconds each — a maneuver he judged a Paradox simply "can't do... so easily."[104]

A further reply offered a fourth account of the same trouble, from the owner most affected by it. Doubted the submerged-forefoot theory on his own boat, where the forefoot immerses only about 4 in and, in any real waves, goes in and out several times through the course of a single tack; doubted too that the hull's own directional stability was itself impeding the tack, since the boat is quick enough to round up promptly into the wind if the helm is let go while beating. The trouble instead is getting her to pay off onto the new tack once rough water or a deep reef leaves too little speed to carry her through: with the sail's centre of effort sitting so far aft, a strong tendency to weathercock into the wind takes over, and a tack left uncompleted leaves the boat simply sitting head to wind, awkward to get going again in choppy water, with the yuloh itself tricky to use from that position. A related but separate problem is stalling outright — sitting with some wind in the sail on either tack but no forward progress at all — which needs to be broken by bearing off downwind for steerage way, or by working the yuloh, before she will sail again.[105] Pressed on whether this amounted to excessive weather helm, drew a sharper distinction: the strong weathercocking tendency shows only once the boat is stopped, and is not the same thing as weather helm, the force needed on the tiller to hold a course to windward while under way — the rudder is powerful enough to steer easily there, with the amount of weather helm adjustable by altering the length of the line that raises the rudder, and not, in his own judgment, excessive. Good as the rudder is once sailing, though, it does nothing for a boat that has already stopped.[106]

A further exchange turned from cause to build variance. One correspondent, accepting the various explanations already offered, wondered why the boat's own weight (inertia) does not simply carry her through a stalled tack, and asked whether the boats reporting the trouble were ballasted to specification.[107] The reply pointed to a confounding factor of its own: nearly every Paradox in service has been modified from the original to some degree, deliberately or not, and one owner recalled a modified boat seen years earlier in Auckland whose chine runners had had their aft portion cut off after she proved unbalanced (lee helm assumed) — a striking contrast against this thread's own report of excessive weathercocking on an unmodified boat. Concluded that, given how much balance seems to vary boat to boat, the soundest plan for a new sail is to trim it empirically: cut a polytarp sail down bit by bit until the boat's own balance is found, before committing to a proper one.[108]

A further question, so far unanswered on the record, asks whether reefing itself moves the centre of effort forward rather than aft — since the sail's head is cut shorter than its foot — and whether that would add lee helm and make tacking harder still.[109]

The theory drew a direct rebuttal from the owner it was addressed to: the sail's head is only a little shorter than the foot, so reefing should shift the centre of effort only slightly; testing the opposite fix, moving the halyard attachment aft along the yard by 4 in, produced no noticeable improvement.[110] Pressed further, the theory was refined rather than dropped: the sail itself sits tilted forward, so reefing furls away its lower, more-aft part while the more-forward part stays set — a shift masked in everyday sailing by the large rudder but showing up once heavily reefed — offered as the likely reason [Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md)'s chine runners were cut down aft (see the build-variance account above); a second Paradox nearing the chine-runner stage was, on the same reasoning, being considered for runners set a little further forward than stock.[111] A further reply set out the underlying textbook principle plainly: moving the centre of effort further aft helps tacking, not further forward — the further forward it sits, the harder the boat is to turn into the wind.[112]

The owner most affected by the trouble replied to both open points in quick succession. On the junk-rig mast-rake comparison, he offered a different reason than the centre-of-effort explanation above — gravity helping keep the sail full in light air and a choppy sea, rather than shifting the centre of effort aft — and, lacking enough of his own understanding of how the chine runners function to feel confident changing them, said he would build a Paradox's runners exactly as designed.[113] On the weathercocking mechanism itself, replying directly to the centre-of-effort principle just above, he restated his own account in sharper terms: turning **into** the wind is never a problem, the tendency to weathercock being quite strong, but turning **away** from head-to-wind is what proves difficult, the boat often losing steerage way before she is established on the new tack and sticking head to wind; given the right combination of moderate wind, smooth water, and enough speed carried into the turn, though, the tack does succeed, matching Dave Bolduc's own account of the technique above.[114]

A further correspondent, drawing on a previous, non-Paradox balanced-lug boat with a similarly forward-sloping mast (though carrying only 15-20% of the sail forward of the mast against the usual 30-35%), offered firsthand support for the gravity/mast-rake theory rather than pure conjecture: the sail stayed full in light air, helped by sitting to leeward, and — a further, unlooked-for benefit — the boat never once suffered the "death rolling" some hulls show running hard downwind, and her boom never rode up when the sail was let right out; a capsize suffered once was put down to letting her get side-on to the waves, not to rolling. Offered as analogous evidence from a different rig on a different hull rather than a Paradox-specific finding.[115]

Between the chine-runner-as-keel, submerged-forefoot, vortex-inhibition, and centre-of-effort/weathercocking accounts, and the CE-forward-when-reefed theory above — doubted by the owner it was aimed at, refined rather than confirmed, and met with a general sail-balance principle pointing the opposite way — the debate over *why* Paradox tacks reluctantly remains unsettled, with no single hydrodynamic cause agreed. What has converged, from an owner who has sailed all four of Matt Layden's small-cruiser designs, is that the reluctance is the trade-off side of the same hull-and-sail character that gives Paradox her class-leading directional stability — in the designer's own apparent intent, a boat built to hold a course for a single-hander over long distances rather than to turn quickly — and that the working fix is technique rather than modification: keep way on, and ease the tiller through the turn instead of forcing it (see [Dave Bolduc: directional stability as a deliberate trade-off, not a flaw](#dave-bolduc-directional-stability-as-a-deliberate-trade-off-not-a-flaw) above). In practice the trouble costs the most ground beating up a narrow channel, where losing way mid-tack means waiting for speed to rebuild before making further progress; the usual light-air fallback is the yuloh rather than a string of short tacks, though that grows to hard work against a brisk headwind for an older sailor.[116]
