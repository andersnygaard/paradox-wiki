# Rudder and chine experiments

See also [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) for how to position the rudder while lying to a sea anchor in bad weather.

A **quantified sea trial of a modified chine runner**, with real VMG/windward-performance data from Matt Layden himself, is documented at [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md); an earlier, less formal **forward chine-extension experiment** — a hull-shape change rather than a rudder one — is covered at [chine runners — origin and evolution](/chine-runners/origin-and-evolution.md#a-forward-chine-extension-on-paradox-tried-and-not-recommended).

A refit-thread debate over what plastic to machine a **lower rudder fitting (bearing/gudgeon)** from — nylon, Tufnol, Delrin, or UHMW, settling on UHMW as the best of the four — is documented separately at [lower rudder fitting — bearing material options](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md).

## Rocky-bottom and beach-protection ideas

For a Paradox sailed off **rocky beaches**, the bottom half of the rudder **below the swim step** takes a real beating in that environment — one such rudder had pieces "the size of Dimes" knocked out of it. The fix is to add **fiberglass roving** to that area for extra abrasion protection.[1]

Two further, untested ideas have been proposed for hulls used in rocky/shallow conditions:

- **Splitting a length of copper pipe** lengthwise and capping the rudder's **leading edge** with it, as a wear-resistant sheathing.[2]

- Adding **longitudinal steel straps** (as thick as could be bent easily to the curve of the hull bottom, roughly 8-10 ft long, epoxy-glassed over) to the bottom of the hull itself — giving both extra impact protection **and** doubling as additional **outside ballast**, low down where it helps stability most.[2]

This last idea is reinforced by the fact that some Paradox hulls have needed to be **loaded heavily** to bring them down to their design waterline — a data point suggesting added outside ballast might be welcome rather than merely tolerated.[2] The steel-strap idea is not known to have been built or tested and remains speculative alongside the already-adopted rudder-bottom roving fix; the copper-pipe idea, however, has since been built and proven in service — see [below](#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative). See also [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md) for other data points on how much ballast Paradox hulls have needed in practice to reach their lines.

A further variant of the same bottom-protection idea proposed **wooden stringers along the outside of the hull bottom**, meant to keep the plywood itself off the ground when beaching — raised together with its own likely drawback, added hydrodynamic drag, by the same builder who proposed it.[120] It drew two negative answers: a Paradox is heavy enough already that the extra drag would only pile onto what the chine runners already cost, on top of making the boat harder to winch onto a trailer;[121] and, separately, that the boat is better thought of as "a miniature keeler, not a dinghy" than as a light dinghy hull that might need such protection, with anchoring out preferred to beaching in any case once the risk of vandalism to an unattended beached boat is weighed against the convenience.[122] Weighing both replies, the idea was dropped in favor of building to plan.[123]

A separate, unanswered suggestion proposed sheathing a Paradox's **bottom** in strips of **Ultrahigh Molecular Weight (UHMW) Polyethylene**, described as having "many of the characteristics of steel," citing a supplier (Interstate Plastics) selling it in sheets from 1/16 in to 4 in thick; not UV-stabilized, but understood to perform well in underwater applications. This is a distinct proposal from the copper-pipe and steel-strap ideas above (aimed at the rudder and hull-bottom impact protection respectively), instead suggesting a full-bottom wear-resistant sheathing material — and it echoes, from a different angle, **UHMW** as the preferred bearing material (ahead of Delrin, nylon, and Tufnol) for the [lower rudder fitting](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md). The suggestion remains untested, like the steel-strap idea above.[3]

### A proven copper-pipe leading edge, and a dacron-rope alternative

The rudder's leading edge and bottom show wear where they rub against the bottom if the blade is not hauled up promptly enough — separate from the rocky-beach wear described above, this is ordinary bottom contact on any seabed. One fix seen recommended on Duckworks cuts a groove into the leading edge and fills it with a length of dacron rope soaked in epoxy; not confirmed built on a Paradox. The copper-pipe idea above, by contrast, has now been built and proven: splitting a length of copper water pipe and hammering it to shape around the leading edge and bottom, bonded with epoxy, has shown no signs of damage after three seasons in service, though the surrounding paint keeps rubbing off.[4][5] Still sound more than a decade on: the same technique was later extended to the foot of the rudder stock, another grounding-wear point, and a further extension to the chine runners' own edges was under consideration too — see [chine runners — a full bronze bottom plate proposed](/chine-runners/construction.md#a-2019-recurrence-a-full-bronze-bottom-plate-proposed-for-ballast-and-beach-protection-together) for the wider 2019 exchange this arose from.[111] The original adopter, replying independently to a fresh November 2019 thread on the same problem (below), put a direct figure on that durability for the first time: about ten years by then, without needing any attention.[118]

A further November 2019 exchange, opened by a complaint that an unprotected edge too often becomes "the default depth finder," gathered six more techniques without settling on any one of them.[112] Gluing rope into the leading edge with epoxy, as a sacrificial edge that can be ground off and replaced, was offered as one option.[113] A length of PVC electrical conduit (schedule 40), split or given a single slice, heated and formed to the edge's curve, then ground down, filleted in, and flared out as needed, was reported to slide over almost any shape with ease and prove very durable; black ABS pipe was suggested as a possible alternative material.[114] An old piece of half-round bronze, salvaged from a dinghy's bow fitting, was screwed to the leading edge and bottom with the voids filled in thickened epoxy — its owner adding candidly that he had separately had trouble both from bottom contact and from forgetting to raise the rudder before hauling the boat up a ramp.[115] A length of Teflon rub rail wrapped around the leading edge was offered as a further, simpler option.[116] Weighed against rope, heavy fiberglass, and split copper pipe, all separately tried, the best result on this record came from a webbing strap wetted out in epoxy and folded lengthwise around the edge, held with masking tape until cured, the tape then sanded away — with the same wry verdict on how well an unprotected edge still finds the bottom: "great depth finder."[117] A sixth reply, two days later, took a different approach again: taping the leading edge and glassing the whole rudder blade, with a standing annual routine of adding another coat of epoxy resin and sanding it back — paint was tried too but never lasts, given the shell-grit beaches sailed from, though the technique itself has caused no trouble.[119] None of the six is preferred over the others on record; each stands as an independently reported fix alongside the already-proven copper-pipe method above.

## Beaching stern-first: a sleeping-comfort technique, and why it doesn't damage the rudder

Beaching stern-first is sometimes done deliberately, to angle the cabin sole so a sleeper's head lies higher than the feet for a comfortable night — going in bow-first on a steep beach would instead mean sleeping with the head toward the bow, where the base of the vent box gets in the way. The technique carries a real wear risk of its own: the transom's corners on either side are vulnerable when beached stern-first, although adequately strong to withstand quite a battering, and grounding stern-first on a pebbly beach specifically risks thumping the base of the rudder. The better practice is to find a gently shelving beach and get the hull as close to horizontal as circumstances allow. The rudder's own structure, though, tolerates ordinary beaching contact without damage regardless of the angle of approach: an upward force on the blade, as when beaching, simply pushes it up until it hits the underside of the boat rather than loading the pintles — the lower pintle alone is 15 mm — so bouncing on a beach while drying out or refloating, though it can sound and feel bad, does no harm to the structure.[6][7]

## Rudder blade stop: clonking the transom corner, and the haul-up rope as a check

Left to swing freely down, the rudder blade does clonk the corner of the transom on the way, but then moves away enough to allow the tiller's full swing — confirming, from direct experience, that nothing built into the stock design actually stops the blade's forward swing before it contacts the transom (see [rudder ballast without a lead casting](#rudder-ballast-without-a-lead-casting) above). The angle it settles at has not been measured, but the haul-up rope can constrain it to hang at any shallower angle wanted.[8][9]

Where the blade meets the upper rudder, the faces need a good flat fit against each other: left loose, the joint rattles and the string that lifts the blade can catch on it. The blade also has to swing down freely on its own, or the sailor is left leaning over the back of the boat trying to push it down by hand.[124]

## Rudder variety across Layden boats

Rudder design has varied across Matt Layden's boats and builds rather than being fixed, as shown by photographs of **four different Layden rudders** posted to the group's files area. One of the four is the rudder of a boat identified only as **"GJAC's,"** photographed half-submerged — the only picture of that boat available.[10]

GJAC herself — a distinct older Matt Layden design (14.5 ft x 4 ft x 9 in, no cabin top), based in Connecticut — has her own concept at [GJAC](/matt-layden/gjac.md), which consolidates this rudder photo with the resale listing and a comment on her chine runners' leeboards-vs-ballast trade-off.[11]

## Rudder-up sailing: a case for reduced rudder size

Sailing "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)," it was discovered about **45 minutes** into a sail that the **rudder blade** had never actually been **lowered**, having been left up the whole time; the boat had tacked "a few times" and reached without the oversight being noticed, prompting the tentative conclusion: **"I believe this boat sails better with the rudder up!!!!"** Further testing was planned in **stronger winds** and, if the result held up, **reducing the rudder's size by about 50% or more**.[12] This single-data-point finding is distinct from (but potentially relevant to) the [heavy-weather](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) question of the best rudder position while lying to a sea anchor, and from the separately documented **rudder-up-while-drifting** minimum-force principle described in that same period.

A **smaller rudder** is generally agreed to make Paradox **tack quicker**, consistent with the plan above to try reducing the rudder's size by about 50% or more.[13] As a practical cross-design reference point, [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s rudder — whose **balance point** differs from Paradox's stock rudder, the difference that first prompted floating a "Little Cruiser style" rudder for Paradox as an alternative[14] — was measured and traced for comparison.

### Why the rudder was built large, and the trade-offs of its size

Firsthand experience sailing alongside [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) himself for two months in the Bahamas, with **Little Cruiser** alongside Paradox, gives the fullest account of **why** Paradox's rudder was originally built as large as it is, and of the trade-offs that large size creates in practice:

- The rudder looks large at first sight, though **Matt had already trimmed it down** once before it was compared against Little Cruiser's in the Bahamas.[15]

- The original reasoning was to **optimize windward ability** and **enhance straight-line tracking**. The same large-rudder concept is believed to have been used on the **15 ft "Bris"** — though that boat reportedly **burned out two autopilots** on an Atlantic crossing (per a *Cruising World* article), a possible downside of a heavily-loaded, hard-to-hold rudder.[15]

- The large rudder **works as intended** for tracking, but creates handling problems elsewhere: crossing the Bahama Banks, Paradox tracked a fairly straight course unattended overnight, while Little Cruiser had to be steered in turns because that boat wandered more.[15]

- Paradox can get **caught in irons while tacking in light air**, needing practice to learn to "nudge" the helm over gently rather than slam it — "the big rudder acts like a break, and if you just slam it over while tacking you'll stop." A more practiced touch avoids the problem entirely, indicating it is technique rather than a hull/rudder defect; Matt himself was never seen caught in irons this way.[15]

- The same irons/tacking problem is not seen aboard **Swamp Thing**, even though she has only chine runners (no centerboard or keel) — indicating the lack of a centerboard is **not** the cause of Paradox's tacking difficulty, an observation that complements the cross-design trim comparison attributing the stiff tracking to Paradox's wide transom and large rudder as a deliberate directional-stability trade-off.[15]

- When Paradox's rudder **kicks up in shallow water**, it creates noticeably more **lee helm** than either [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) or Swamp Thing — a third reason, alongside tacking difficulty and the autopilot-load concern, for wanting to reduce the rudder's length or reshape it.[15]

**Little Cruiser's rudder, measured for comparison:** Little Cruiser's elliptical rudder was removed, its outline traced onto construction paper, and measured directly:[15]

- **Little Cruiser's rudder**: 29 15/16 in long (as traced) and 16 1/4 in at its widest point; from the **pivot hole**, its length is **25 3/8 in**.[15]

- **Paradox's rudder** (for comparison): **29.13 in** long from the pivot hole and **15 3/4 in** wide — i.e. Little Cruiser's rudder is **slightly narrower** but its usable (pivot-hole) length is roughly **3.75 in shorter** than Paradox's.[15]

- Construction detail: the rudder blade is built from **two pieces of 1/2 in plywood glued together** to a full 1 in thickness, tapered both top-to-bottom and fore-and-aft to produce a foil shape.[15]

- Rather than cutting the full 3.75 in off a Paradox rudder outright, a builder is advised to **whittle it down gradually** or **build a dedicated test rudder** to experiment safely. Matt Layden himself **preferred the shape of Little Cruiser's rudder** because it is **better balanced** — so adapting LC's shape (rather than simply shortening Paradox's own rudder) is a plausible alternative worth considering.[15]

This is the fullest account connecting **why** Paradox's rudder is shaped as it is, **what problems** that shape causes in practice (irons in light air, kick-up lee helm), and a **measured real-world alternative** (Little Cruiser's shorter, differently-balanced rudder) for builders considering the same 50%-or-more reduction described above.

A second, independent account of the same rudder-size rationale came from a builder who tested a Little Cruiser at Cedar Key and discussed rudder size directly with Matt Layden there: the rudder sits well aft as part of the boat's overall lateral-resistance equation, and Paradox's hull specifically needed the larger rudder for increased lateral resistance. See [junk rig — a daggerboard-forward CLR experiment, and why Paradox's rudder was built large](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md#a-daggerboard-forward-clr-experiment-and-why-paradoxs-rudder-was-built-large).[16]

**Offsets for building the Little Cruiser rudder** — instructions and a table of offsets for Little Cruiser's rudder were compiled and distributed to the list (see also [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md) for the sailmaking material sent in the same message), giving builders the concrete data to attempt the LC-style rudder Matt Layden preferred, rather than only a verbal description of it.[17]

## Rudder vulnerability, and how much load it carries relative to the hull

A 2015 exchange, prompted by the same [Small Fry capsize](/boats/small-fry.md#an-unconfirmed-2009-report-a-rolling-off-fremantle-and-a-presumed-news-source) then under discussion on the list, weighed how vulnerable the rudder really is. One view held it looks like the most vulnerable part of the boat, its weakest link — tempered by the reminder that Matt Layden designed the boat as a coastal cruiser meant to venture onto open water only in reasonable weather.[99] Asked directly what the rudder's weak areas actually are,[100] the underlying design characteristic was explained: the rudder is cantilevered from the rudder stock and unsupported for most of its underwater part — not a problem when used as designed, and plenty of other boats run unsupported rudders without trouble, but Paradox's rudder carries more load than most other rudder types, a load that only grows as conditions worsen, so the rudder and its fixings need to be built stronger than most.[101]

[Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md)'s own verdict runs more reassuring: he would not classify the Paradox rudder as weak, though he accepts it as the weakest part of the system, since it has moving parts and the inevitable stress raisers that implies — compared with most (all?) 14 ft production boats, it is immensely strong.[102]

A builder then fitting his own rudder raised the converse concern: could building and attaching the blade too strongly simply transfer the damage to the hull instead, in sufficiently extreme conditions?[103] No direct answer to that specific trade-off is on record, but the practical advice that followed treats the rudder and its fixing as one system rather than the blade alone: for a genuine ocean passage capable of meeting whatever weather comes, the recommendation is to reinforce both the rudder and the lower gudgeon, aiming to make the combination bulletproof — offered with the caveat that a boat bought already built, sailed only in weather its owner judges safe, and never yet troubled by a rudder problem, is no substitute for a builder's own hands-on sense of what the stock parts can take.[104]

A returning contributor to that same exchange pushed back on how large the loads involved really get: the forces on the rudder are bounded by the boat's own scale, and a roughly 1,400 lb boat is still a small, light one whose loads can never be huge.[105] A separate reply relayed Matt Layden's own view as a caution rather than a contradiction: because the rudder's large area works as part of the hull's overall lateral resistance, a wave slamming the boat's side loads the rudder more than a rudder built only for steering would see — not a criticism of the design, but a fact worth bearing in mind when sizing its fixings.[106] Reconciling the two, the same returning contributor agreed the loads in question are best described as moments — force multiplied by distance — a function of the hull's own size and weight (how easily it can be pushed around) together with the rudder's area and how far it sits from the boat's turning centre.[107]

Finishing his own rudder and stock for a full-sized Paradox after building a Michalak "Philsboat," one builder was struck by how much more massive Paradox's parts are — one and a half times the weight of the Philsboat's own solid stock and rudder — leaving only the lower gudgeon/pintle fitting, where the load concentrates, as a lingering worry. Reasoning through his own cast gudgeon, coated months earlier and left bare while he searched for the right cloth: six layers of woven roving wrapped fully around the fitting work the same way steel bands work around a barrel, so as long as the roving goes all the way round, the bolts and the friction against the hull hold it in place even once the epoxy bond to the gudgeon itself has aged past its freshest strength.[108]

## Rudder ballast without a lead casting

Photographs in the group's files section, in a folder titled "rudder blade," show a rudder being fitted with lead ballast in a method and placement that differ from the plans and Don Elliott's manual illustrations — a point of confusion for at least one builder comparing them side by side. The pictured rudder is [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s, not Paradox's own.[4][5]

An alternative to the rudder's **lead ballast**, considered for a [BRIS-method Paradox](/boats/bris-paradox.md), is a **bungee cord** running from the tiller down to the blade's leading edge — the idea being that it could hold the buoyant blade down, help snap it back to vertical once past a critical angle, and cushion an impact when the blade hits rocks.[18] In practice, though, the stock ballasted rudder already comes up **very easily** — more easily than expected — and a bungee-cord scheme would need to be strong enough to hold the buoyant blade down, which would in turn make the up-haul *heavier* to operate than simply lifting the ballasted blade already is; a separate downhaul would also be needed, since bungee cord does not hold up well long-term in a marine environment. There is, however, a proven lead-casting alternative: a [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) rudder has been built with **lead shot mixed into epoxy** and poured into the same screw-fitted cutout a hot-lead casting would use — no melting required, and the result was easier to fair than a poured-lead blade.[19]

A further caution applies to the stock design: as drawn, nothing stops the Paradox rudder from swinging forward until it strikes the bottom of the transom, so a bungee-downhaul scheme would need a built-in stop; once the bungee stopped pulling down, the blade would simply float back up unaided — an argument for the simplest working system over an added bungee.[20]

## A pivoting, spring-tensioned rudder blade on a sister sharpie, and why Paradox stays simple

A different rudder-blade philosophy altogether was worked out for [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md)'s 32 ft sharpie "Hogfish" — a boat close enough in concept to Paradox that its own rudder started out built the same way, offered here as a caution against assuming Paradox's weighted-blade approach would suit every hull of the type. On the reasoning that a weighted blade cannot give a balanced helm — the weight pulls the blade in the wrong direction, since a balanced helm needs it to pivot slightly forward rather than simply hang down — and that a weighted blade builds up dangerous angular momentum on the upswing working in chop, "making for an absolutely unmanageable helm," Hogfish's blade was reworked to pivot on a Dyneema line running through a groove cut into its upper part and over a pulley, spliced to a "fool's purchase": two stationary blocks joined by a thin nylon line looping between them to a jam cleat, acting as a springy tensioner that drives the blade fully down under normal load but simply stretches, rather than breaking anything, if the blade strikes bottom.[94]

Paradox's own simpler, gravity-loaded design was defended point by point against the same objections: because Paradox's rudder weight sits at the blade's rear edge rather than being arranged to pull it forward, the blade is actually driven *past* vertical rather than held back from balance — limited only by the blade fouling the underside of the transom, exactly the "nothing stops it swinging forward" caution recorded above — and because the blade is fastened flat to the side of the stock rather than hung on a separate free-swinging pivot, the friction between blade and stock, once the rudder is loaded, is by itself enough to keep the blade from moving unless it strikes something; no angular-momentum jump of the kind described for Hogfish has ever been noticed on a Paradox.[95]

## Rudder pivot point on the transom centerline

The rudder's pivot point is meant to fall on the **ship's centerline** at the transom: sheet #1 of the plans shows the centerline of the rudder's **upper half** falling on the vessel's own centerline, meaning the pivot axis is on-center, and nothing on sheet #11 or elsewhere contradicts that reading.[21][22] A sense of symmetry alone makes an off-center upper rudder half look wrong, but the **lower half** and the tiller both work perfectly well off-center regardless — the alignment question is cosmetic on the visible upper half, not functional. This on-center reading has been confirmed in practice by builders who hung their own rudders the same way.[23]

Builders have separately asked whether the **gudgeons and pintles** themselves (rather than the rudder blade's own pivot bolt) should be hung on the transom's centerline, or whether the blade should be offset instead. In practice, mounting the pintles on the centerline is confirmed to work: it puts the rudder blade itself very slightly to starboard and the tiller very slightly to port, a minor, purely cosmetic asymmetry that causes no handling issue — consistent with the plans' own on-center pivot-point reading above.[24]

## The pivot-bolt fixing, and its record under grounding loads

The rudder blade pivots on a single bolt, as drawn on the plans — a detail that raises the question of what happens if the boat grounds before the blade can be lifted, and an unfair sideways strain is thrown into the fixing by wind and waves working the hull.[25] In practice the fixing has proved sound: by late 2003 the original Paradox had roughly **10,000 miles** on this exact configuration under [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) himself, with no reported problem — evidence the designer would have changed the fixing had it given trouble — and "Zoë" had likewise logged several hundred miles on it without any issue.[26] A later question about the pivot design — whether, unlike most lifting rudder blades (usually sandwiched between two cheek pieces on the stock to take side-thrust), Paradox's simpler single-bolt fixing risks the washer/nut eating into the surrounding wood or fiberglass over time[27] — drew the same reassurance repeated independently two years later: no problems reported by owners running the stock fixing as drawn, consistent with Matt Layden's own many thousands of miles on Paradox without issue.[28]

The fixing's real-world toughness is also borne out by ["Jill"](/boats/jill.md#a-small-craft-advisory-and-a-rudder-blade-that-saved-the-trailer)'s own rudder blade, pulled off the boat and used as an improvised traction "bridge" to free a truck stuck in wet sand — recounted again a year later with two further data points: heavy subsequent motoring and motor-sailing produced no damaging wear on the silicon bronze washer, bolt, and nut, and the real risk to a blade is **rock rather than sand** — dragging the leading edge over rocks is where damage happens, making a case for Kevlar-reinforcing that edge (as later done in [Little Cruiser's 2005 refit](#the-big-rudder-as-a-leeway-reducer), above).[29]

### Bushing the pivot bolt against water ingress

A further pivot-design question concerns **waterproofing** the bolt hole itself, since the fixing sits below the waterline: the plans simply show plain holes through both the stock and the blade for the pivot bolt to pass through, with no guidance on stopping water ingress there, raising the question of whether bushings should be fitted in both parts.[30]

The recommended technique bushes the hole with epoxy rather than a separate metal or plastic sleeve: drill out the pivot locations in both the cheek block(s) and the rudder stock with an **oversized bit**, first brush on a penetrating coat of straight (unthickened) epoxy so the end grain and long fibers can "take up" some adhesive, then fill the oversized holes in both parts with **thickened epoxy**. Once cured, re-drill the epoxy plugs with the correct bit for the pivot bolt's actual diameter — for a 1/2 in bolt, drilling the original oversized hole at 3/4 in leaves enough thickened epoxy to rebore cleanly back down to 1/2 in. The result is a rudder that "will rotate butter smooth" while the surrounding wood and fiberglass stay fully protected from water. For a more deluxe version, a matched pair of **silicon bronze bushings** can be fitted to the outside diameter of the reboared epoxy, giving what's described as a long-lasting, "near bullet-proof" rudder pivot assembly.[31] This oversized-hole/epoxy-fill/rebore method was independently confirmed twice more: once as a brief restatement of the same 3/4 in-to-1/2 in figures,[32] and once as "a very good solution" from a builder about to apply it to his own rudder.[33]

A simpler alternative, used successfully on at least one Paradox, sets the pivot-bolt **washers flush with the surrounding surfaces**, bedded in epoxy, rather than bushing the bolt holes themselves — a technique that protects the wood at the bearing surface without the oversized-hole/rebore step above.[34]

### A further 2011 concern: pivot strength on an enlarged Paradox

Sizing his own 10%-enlarged Paradox, one correspondent flagged the pivot as a particular worry: the stock design already carries "a very big rudder," and enlarging the blade only sharpens the concern, recalling [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md)'s own work strengthening a rudder pivot on one of his own designs as a precedent worth studying. No reply settling the question for an enlarged hull specifically is on record; the established fixings above — the oversized-hole/epoxy-fill/rebore bushing technique, or flush-bedded washers — remain the only ones with any Paradox-specific track record.[89]

## The raised blade scraping against the upper pintle bracket

Raising the rudder blade fully vertical can bring it into contact with the **upper pintle bracket**, since the blade has to pass close by the fitting on its way up.[35] In practice this is cosmetic rather than structural: where the bolt heads rub, the paint scrapes off but the epoxy underneath stays sound — countersinking the bolts deeper than usual would avoid even that.[36] Rather than treating the contact as a defect to design out with a fixed pin (which would remove the ability to raise and lower the blade at both stock positions), it is accepted as normal wear that does not need a fix.[37]

## The big rudder as a leeway-reducer

At a Cedar Key small-boat gathering in May 2019, Matt Layden gave a dinner talk on his design lineage explaining the aerodynamic/hydrodynamic role of the chine runner — tied directly to rudder size: "the chine runner acts as a hydrodynamic fence with low pressure on one side and high pressure on the other which enables his sharpies to sort of 'fly' upwind through the water **with the aid of a big rudder to reduce leeway**." The oversized rudder is therefore not an incidental byproduct but a deliberate partner to the [chine runners](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) for going to windward — consistent with the rudder having been built to optimize windward ability and enhance tracking.[38]

The same point was made independently in 2004, when a Swedish-to-English translation of a book's Paradox chapter was reviewed: the translated chapter was felt to deserve more weight on the large rudder's importance to **lateral resistance** — see [Sven Yrvind's book](/references/yrvind-book.md#a-proposed-group-project-to-translate-sven-yrvinds-full-book-into-english) for the fuller context of that translation project.

In Little Cruiser's 2005 refit, the leading edges of both the **rudder blade and the centerboard** were reinforced with **1.7 oz Kevlar** cloth for impact protection — a concrete durability detail for the same kind of high-load foil edges discussed above.[39]

## Sudden, total loss of rudder effect mid-tack, and tacking technique

A control problem recorded aboard "[little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" shows the rudder's effect can be lost **entirely and suddenly**, mid-manoeuvre, rather than only as a deliberate rudder-up experiment. Close-hauled in a channel, heading SE into a F3-4 SE wind against 2-3 kn of tide (a genuinely disturbed sea surface), a tack away from some moored yachts went as expected up to head-to-wind, where the boat stuck — but trying then to **bear off back onto the starboard tack**, "the rudder had no effect, in fact it behaved just as if the blade was raised," to the point of checking whether the blade had physically fallen off. Drifting down onto the moorings with the tide, the sail was lowered and the boat **sculled clear**; once the sail was re-hoisted a little later, everything was **back to normal**. Two standing quirks on the same boat: she cannot be sailed at all with the rudder **fully raised** (pulling in the mainsheet just turns her into the wind; dropping the blade to horizontal restores control), and she tacks to **starboard** more easily than to **port**, occasionally sticking head-to-wind on that tack as she did this time.[40]

Trim was tried as a fix for the same boat's occasional tacking trouble — sticking head-to-wind, or failing to come up to the wind — with some success: experimenting with ballast trim mostly sorted it, though it may have been developing technique rather than the trim adjustment that made the difference. On every other point of sail the boat handled the same regardless of trim, even with a passenger's weight moved around deliberately to test it — tacking is the one manoeuvre sensitive enough to notice a difference. Gybing, by contrast, is consistently benign, so a boat stuck in the tacking failure mode described above can usually still be turned the other way around.[41]

## Fitting the lower pintle without drilling the rudder stock

A way of fitting the **lower** pintle to the rudder stock without drilling a hole for it has been devised, judged simpler and more accurate than drilling — a real concern, since a botched drilled hole means a major rebuild of the stock. See [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md#lower-pintle-an-alternative-to-drilling-the-rudder-stock) for the technique and photos.

A pintle bedded in epoxy alone, keyed only by roughening its surface with emery paper before it goes into an oversized hole in the stock, can still work loose over years of hard use: on one boat the lower pintle — a 12 mm stainless rod — turned up floating free inside the stock six years after launch, found only when the rudder came off for a fresh layer of bottom glass, with no damage done. Saw cuts filed into the rod's sides, giving the epoxy a proper mechanical key rather than relying on surface roughness alone, fixed it on re-bonding; an annual check of the pintle is now the standing precaution. See [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#a-rudder-stock-pintle-repair-and-the-folding-bikes-first-cruising-season) for the full account.[42]

A further alternative builds the pins into the stock from the start, rather than drilling or bonding them into an already-finished one: each pin, bent to shape with an oxy-acetylene torch, sits in a groove cut into the corresponding plywood half of the stock before the halves are glued together, bedded in thickened epoxy and wet out as part of the normal lamination — cheaper than the plans' own fitting, with a hole through the upper pin for a cotter pin to keep the stock captive. See [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#a-rudder-pin-design-built-into-the-stocks-lamination) for the full account, unproven at time of writing beyond limited time on the water.[43]

A lower pintle can also develop play through a different path: water working into the plywood stock itself, rather than into the bond around the pintle, showing up as several degrees of play and lingering dampness even after days drying in a warm room, with a longitudinal crack of unknown depth visible in the stock. One repair approach sands back to bare wood, speeds drying with hot-water bottles, then re-warms the wood with hot-water bottles and a hot-air gun immediately before pouring in epoxy, on the reasoning that trapped air contracting as it cools draws the epoxy deeper into the grain; unidirectional carbon laid across and along the crack, and wound in a loop around the pintle, is meant to stop the crack widening and stabilize the fitting, with a thin layer of fiberglass wrapped around the pintle's base first to keep the carbon from reacting galvanically with the steel. A separate case traced the same kind of play to the epoxy bond itself failing rather than the wood cracking — water tracked down the outside of an oversized, epoxy-lined hole without penetrating the wood — cured by cutting saw kerfs into the pintle and roughening its surface for a better mechanical key before regluing, with a regular winter inspection adopted afterward as a standing precaution; the suspected cause is the heavy load a lower pintle carries, especially under grounding, which can separate the joints between plywood laminates if they were not well made in the first place, for which flush copper rivets through the stock were suggested as extra insurance. Substituting a ready-made transom-hung rudder from a small catamaran or trimaran of similar span and chord — trading away the boarding step and shallow-water steering discussed above, and perhaps a little drag — was floated as an alternative to repairing a damaged stock at all, but judged unlikely to survive Paradox's own grounding loads unless the replacement's stock were short enough never to touch bottom.[44][45][46]

A separate, unrelated fouling problem — a tall-as-drawn rudder stock catching the mainsheet in the notch against the transom — and the fixes for it are covered in full at [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#rudder-stock-height-and-mainsheet-fouling-at-the-transom-notch).

## Molding a shaped lower rudder fitting: three techniques on record

Three ways of fabricating a shaped lower rudder fitting as a hollow epoxy/fiberglass shell are on record. One: build a male mold from foam, cover it in cloth wet out with epoxy, then dissolve the foam mold out with gasoline once the shell has cured, leaving a shell ready to fill with epoxy.[96] Another: form a sheet-metal shape on wax paper, then pack it with scrap fiberglass and epoxy thickened with a little structural filler, pulling the metal form free once cured.[97] A third points to two casting techniques centuries old — lost-wax casting, where a male mold of sealing wax or beeswax has a female mold cast around it, then is melted out and run off once that mold has set, and sand casting as a further alternative.[98]

## Lining up the top and bottom rudder fittings with a dummy jig

A dummy jig avoids drilling the bottom fitting on guesswork: cut two wooden off-cuts to match the spacing of an off-the-shelf top fitting, drill a hole through both along the line where the fitting will finally sit, then pass a stainless-steel rod — long enough to reach from the top fitting to the bottom — down through that hole and line it up with the bottom bracket. The bottom bracket is then drilled by sighting the back of the drill through the same dummy hole in the temporary top fitting. The exercise also caught a fit problem worth catching before drilling: the rudder blade must be cut back exactly as the plan shows, or it will bump against the hull as the rudder swings to the starboard side.[109]

## A lost rudder up-haul, and sailing on with reduced rudder area

The usual practice of running the mainsheet through the rudder up-haul line safeties the rudder against losing area if the up-haul itself comes loose or is left slack. When that step was skipped, a 50-mile round trip to the boat ramp turned up the lapse only on arrival: the up-haul had come loose, costing roughly **15%** of the rudder's total area. The exposed end-grain wood was coated with **Marine Goop** rather than aborting the outing, and the boat was sailed anyway in **16 mph gusting to 18 mph** — with little if any noticeable difference in the helm despite the reduced area, a real-world data point on how much rudder area a Paradox can lose before it is missed. See [boats/zoe.md — a rudder up-haul safety lapse](/boats/zoe.md#a-rudder-up-haul-safety-lapse) for the full account.[47]

A related but distinct fouling problem — the mainsheet catching on the **rudder and rear cleats**, worsened by a fitted **outboard** — has drawn a different family of fixes; see [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#mainsheet-fouling-on-the-rudder-and-rear-deck-with-an-outboard-fitted).

A cheaper preventive fix for the same up-haul-dropping-loose failure: hang a shackle on the up-haul line between the last fairlead and the cleat, stopping the line from jumping clear of the cleat's groove as the rudder kicks up.[48]

Tacking trouble is generally a matter of **technique** rather than a hardware fault:

- Tacking trouble mostly shows up in **light air**, with one tack always easier than the other — technique explains much of it, since it gets easier with practice, and the **cut of the sail** may also play a part. As a further data point on rudder-up sailing (echoing the "rudder or not" discovery above), "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" has been sailed with the rudder up in light air and seemed to tack fine — on one occasion a tack was completed before it was realized that the rudder had never been put down.[49]

- The most detailed tacking-technique checklist on record states flatly there is "nothing at all the matter with Paradox['s] lateral plane or rudder size." The advice: (1) always bring the boat to a **close-hauled** heading before attempting a tack — never attempt one from a close reach — and time tacks to the **crests of waves and strongest gusts**, not troughs or lulls, the most common cause of a failed tack; (2) don't stall the rudder by throwing it hard over too quickly or too far — no more than about **30 degrees either side** at a time, easing it over with a feel for its grip on the water; (3) check the **downhaul** lowers the blade fully, forward edge vertical, since Paradox's uphaul-only design (per the plans) needs a downhaul added for reliable, safe operation; (4) a rudder bolt hole not drilled exactly **perpendicular** will be slow to lower or may stick; (5) a poorly shaped blade "will not perform at all" — a rudder blade shape/pattern can be supplied if needed; (6) the main halyard must attach at the yard so part of the sail sits ahead of the mast; (7) in extremely light wind, use a **paddle** to force the bow around, since Paradox is a heavy craft that should not be expected to handle like a racing dinghy.[50][51]

## Keeping the blade down under way: gravity, a weighted uphaul, and a raised-height stop

Whether a weighted rudder blade really stays down as boat speed increases, not just at rest, is a question every builder eventually asks fitting the rudder for the first time — since the pivot has to be left loose enough for the blade to kick up on impact, it can look, on paper, as though the same looseness would let the blade swing back under way.[90] In practice gravity alone holds the blade down with no separate lock needed; the same uphaul-weighting fix already established above (see [a lost rudder up-haul](#a-lost-rudder-up-haul-and-sailing-on-with-reduced-rudder-area)) works equally well as a proactive safeguard, not only a repair: hanging a weight — a spare shackle serves — on the up-haul line where it runs horizontally along the tiller takes up any slack if the downhaul is cut too fine and the blade touches bottom, stopping the up-haul from jumping clear of its cleat groove.[91][92]

A further, independent fix caps the blade's raised height directly rather than managing the uphaul line: after decreasing the pivot bolt's tightness on its own failed to make the blade reliably drop, a rubber-padded lag screw fitted into the side of the rudder stock limits how high the blade can be raised in the first place, keeping enough of the blade's weight on the pivot's aft side at all times that it continues to want to swing back down.[93]

## A twin-rudder proposal for a centerline outboard, judged not worth the complexity

Fitting **two rudders working in tandem**, so that an outboard motor could be mounted on the boat's centerline instead of off to one side, has been proposed but not worked out as a design — a UK-made wind-vane self-steering unit exists that mounts readily to an outboard bracket, so that either the motor or the self-steering gear could be swapped onto the same centerline mount as needed, provided the mainsheet did not foul the wind-vane hardware. The steering-linkage mechanics were raised as an open question rather than a worked-out design.[52]

The idea has been judged not worth its complexity: on a hull as narrow across the transom as Paradox's, there is little practical advantage to a dead-centered, rarely-used outboard, while a genuine twin-rudder installation adds mechanical complexity and drag for the sake of an occasional convenience.[53]

The self-steering side of the same centerline-mount question, and how a fold-up rudder like Paradox's complicates fitting a trim-tab wind vane specifically, is carried forward at [self-steering — a trim-tab wind vane and Paradox's folding rudder](/operations/self-steering.md#a-trim-tab-wind-vane-and-paradoxs-folding-rudder).

## A proposed full skeg and barn-door rudder, and why it shifts the whole design

A drawn interpretation of Paradox, fitted with a **full-length skeg** carrying a large external **"barn door" rudder** hung directly off its trailing edge, drew a detailed technical objection rather than a straightforward critique of the hardware alone.[54] Moving the rudder onto a long skeg moves the boat's **center of lateral resistance aft**, which in turn forces the sail's **center of effort aft** to keep the boat in balance — meaning, in practice, that the mast has to move aft too, further into the cabin, disturbing the vent box and mast step. The skeg/rudder combination also alters the sail's **lead**, since the rudder now sits directly behind it. The verdict: unless the builder fully understands the knock-on effects, the change is not worth making — it adds draught without sailing any better, and may sail worse, since altering one element of a tightly balanced design turns into a redesign of the whole boat.[55] A lighter-touch alternative for the same underlying goal — sacrificing the chine runners' relative simplicity for a genuine windward improvement — would be a small forward **centerboard**, at the cost of the extra work and a hull opening that can jam with small stones.[55] This is a concrete instance of a concern already raised in the abstract: Paradox's sail lead, without a centerboard, already sits aft of amidships compared with [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s forward lead, precisely because her center of effort is set differently — see [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling — why the two boats' mainsheets lean opposite ways](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#mainsheet-fouling-on-the-rudder-and-rear-deck-with-an-outboard-fitted).

A further practical question raised about the same redesign: without a centerboard's ability to be raised for shoal water and reduced wetted surface, and given the deeper, fixed skeg-mounted rudder's own draught, an engine was judged likely to be necessary just to get the boat through an ordinary tack.[56]

## Sven Yrvind's own integrated rudder-transom system

The chine runner's foremost external champion (see [Yrvind and the chine runner](/people/yrvind/paradox-and-the-chine-runner.md)) developed a rudder system for a new boat, judged also suitable for Paradox: a single hinged assembly that folds several separate functions (kick-up protection, sea-anchor jigger, trim tab, tiller friction lock, and self-steering) into one mechanism rather than solving each with a separate fitting:

- The rudder mounts on a **false transom hinged to the actual transom**, which can be raised a full **90 degrees**. This lets the whole rudder assembly act as a kick-up guard against grounding, be lifted clear of the water while riding to a sea anchor, or be used, partially raised, as a steadying **"jigger"** while riding to the anchor.

- A **short mast rises from the rudder itself**, serving as a lever handle for raising the blade, a mount for a small steering sail that helps push the boat through a tack when the helm is thrown, and a sheeting point for self-steering.

- A **trim tab** carries a pulley that interacts with a second pulley on the rudder itself. Rigged with a figure-eight line, the trim tab swings through twice the rudder's own angle — a mechanical **"power steering"** effect. The same figure-eight rigging makes the rudder **"break down" like an airplane's Fowler flaps**, adding power at low docking speeds, and doubles as a **drag brake** to slow the boat quickly when thrown hard over.

- Alternatively, the trim-tab pulley can run straight lines back into the cabin, letting it serve either as an ordinary balance trim tab on a point of sail or as a sheet for **self-steering**.

- A short peg projecting ahead of the mast drags along the deck, its friction acting as a **tiller tamer** to hold a chosen helm position.

- A second, longer friction stick, mounted flush to the false transom and led into the cabin by its own line, bears against a perpendicular stick fixed to the actual transom. This adjusts the kick-up hinge's friction, lets the rudder be locked at an angle for **shoal cruising**, and can store the whole assembly in the raised position.

This is the configuration currently planned for that new boat, despite other rudder arrangements having been sketched as well, and it was judged suitable for Paradox too.[57]

A comparison from a different small-boat design, the **Bolger Weekender**, offers a related but separate thought on light-air handling: Weekender sailors had found that in **light winds**, crew weight sometimes needs to be shifted to the **leeward (downwind) side** of the boat to simulate the heel that a stronger wind would otherwise provide, since the chine "edges have to bite" to work properly.[58] Trying this aboard Paradox produced no clear result: moving about inside the boat has been observed to have **little effect on her heeling**, a small but concrete data point on how little the crew's position, by itself, changes the boat's angle of heel, distinct from the deliberate weight-shifting described for the Weekender's different hull form.[59]

Tacking failures and light-air handling in general (irons, stalling, heel) are distinct from the more specific, temporary total loss of rudder effect described above, which remains a single, unexplained data point.

## Foil section: not a standard NACA airfoil

Common rudder foil sections quoted in small-boat design reference material — **NACA 0012** and **NACA 0015** — are noticeably thicker in cross-section than Matt Layden's rudder, which is comparatively flat.[60] The archive does not record whether the design was worked from a specific named airfoil section or developed empirically; see [why the rudder was built large, and the trade-offs of its size](#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size) above for the rudder's overall sizing rationale, and citation [15] above for the only recorded construction detail on the blade's cross-section (two 1/2 in plywood pieces glued to a full 1 in thickness, tapered both top-to-bottom and fore-and-aft to produce the foil shape).

Working from the blade's own dimensions — 400 mm long and 2×24 mm plywood thick before glue — a builder can reach a thickness ratio close to **16.66%**, making **NACA 0015** (15%) a reasonable stand-in section if a builder wants to shape one from a named airfoil; in practice, a rudder has been shaped successfully by eye with an angle grinder alone, "till it looked right."[61] The flatter, less-tapered section is arguably not a compromise but an advantage for a boat operating mostly at slow speeds: too much taper toward the trailing edge reduces control at low speed and raises the risk of damage, and the small eddies a flatter blade sheds actually help swing the stern around during a turn.[62]

A picture superimposing a **NACA 0006** airfoil over the plans' own rudder outline shows it fits closer than NACA 0008, though still not exact.[63] The underlying arithmetic was later corrected: the blade's *total* thickness of 24 mm is made up of two 12 mm plies, not two 24 mm plies, giving a thickness ratio of 24/400 ≈ 0.06 — confirming **NACA 0006** as the closest standard section, rather than the thicker NACA 0015 figure worked from the earlier (mistaken) 2×24 mm reading.[64][65]

A practical shaping technique complements the angle-grinder-by-eye method above: photocopy the plan's aerofoil cross-section, enlarge it to full size, and cut cardboard templates directly from the copy to check the blade's shape as it is worked. A **square trailing edge**, rather than one brought to a sharp point, is reported to give **less drag** as well as being more robust. The blade's two faces are not shaped alike: the face bearing against the rudder stock is left flat as far as the shaping allows, then blended into the aerofoil face beyond that; on the outside, the blade is shaped down to just above the waterline, but the radius around the uphaul-line groove is left at full thickness and blended smoothly into the surrounding shape.[4][5]

A trailing edge finished as a flat about **1/4 in** wide, rather than tapered to a point, is reported on a further rudder built to the plans, which in practice comes out **"basically a flat blade,"** not a dynamically shaped foil — consistent with the flatter, less-tapered reading above. Its taper was worked using guide lines marked on the blade the way contour-depth lines mark a chart. The same rudder's leading edge was later built up with **G-flex epoxy**, thick enough to survive a hit on a rock without letting water into the wood — at the cost of the blade's own measurements no longer matching the plans' spec exactly, though with no noticeable loss of speed — a further wear-protection option alongside the copper-pipe and other techniques described [below](#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative).[125]

A router offers a further shaping method alongside the angle-grinder-by-eye and cardboard-template techniques above: a pair of guide fences cut from plywood, offset from a chosen NACA section — **NACA 0009** on one build — by the length of the router bit's own protruding cutting edge, then run the bit against the fences to cut the foil directly.[126]

## A builder defect at the brass leading edge, found and repaired

Close inspection of one rudder, after some years' service, found minute gaps between the fibreglass sheathing and the metal strip protecting the leading edge: rather than encapsulating the brass capping piece by folding the fibreglass cloth over its edge, the original build had taken the cloth only as far as the brass — workable when new, but opening tiny gaps over time through which water could reach the plywood and cause delamination. Cleaning up the leading edge and filling the gaps with epoxy, worked warm under a tarpaulin tent in cold weather, is the fix; the underlying lesson is to fold sheathing cloth fully over any metal edge capping, rather than butting it against the edge, wherever a rudder or similar foil carries one.[66]

## Raising the rudder fully vertical: gudgeon fouling

Fitting a rudder and tiller can reveal that the blade cannot be raised all the way to vertical because the **upper gudgeon** itself is in the way — a fit problem distinct from the cosmetic pintle-bracket scraping described above, since here the blade physically cannot complete its travel. A countersunk gudgeon fitting is the recommended fix.[67]

## Sailing to windward with the rudder raised, and boarding from a raised step

The rudder's role as a keel substitute extends further than simply providing lateral resistance at rest: a Paradox will sail to windward with the rudder raised to 90 degrees from vertical and less than half its area still submerged, letting her work to windward in as little as 2 ft of water.[68] Raised fully clear of the water altogether, the rudder finds its own natural position — useful if the boat is ever laid ahull in a gale — though the tiller port itself then needs a waterproof gasket, since the built-in [vent baffle](/components/vent-baffle.md) may not keep water out under those conditions, fine as it is in anything more moderate.[68]

The rudder should be **raised**, not lowered, whenever the boat is being propelled by the [yuloh](/components/yuloh.md), except perhaps with the wind astern: lowered, it tends to blow the bow away from the wind, and a boat left to her own devices with no sail up will settle downwind with her quarter angled into it, a set that pulling hard on the windward-side yuloh does little to correct — "For me, this was a hard learned lesson."[68] The single-bolt fixing to the starboard side of the stock (see [the pivot-bolt fixing, and its record under grounding loads](#the-pivot-bolt-fixing-and-its-record-under-grounding-loads) above) has nonetheless held up in service: "I've never heard of a Paradox that has lost a rudder."[68] That same starboard offset, though, works against the boat when tacking from starboard to port: its drag combines with the drag of the offset mast and sail, also set to port, to slow the boat through the tack and can put her in irons. Reversing the rudder, jigging it side to side, paddling the bow around, or jigging with the yuloh are the usual recoveries; in choppy water or against a foul tide with little boat speed, wearing ship instead costs negligible ground.[68] Corroborating the general design point, a large rudder acting as a keel substitute has been judged the reason a Paradox can carry a proportionally smaller underwater profile elsewhere than a comparable yacht, since the further aft a rudder sits from the centre of lateral resistance, the more leverage it has.[69]

A small step at the bottom of the stock, on the port side, is built primarily for boarding when the boat is launched from a beach; it is "almost useless" for boarding from the water after a fall overboard, since it is not possible to get a foot on the step while simultaneously hauling up and over the stern — a trailing rope with a loop is the better aid for that case.[68] The step has nonetheless been used successfully, with real effort, to reboard after going over the side unexpectedly, holding onto the boat and working aft to the stern first.[70]

A 2015 theory credits the boarding step with a second, hydrodynamic function beyond boarding: read as an upside-down foil, it was argued to keep the stern submerged running downwind, helping Paradox avoid the corkscrewing around a submerged forefoot that sharpies generally suffer running before the wind — offered alongside the chine runners' own course-stabilizing role. See [chine runners — a third strand: vortex inhibition](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-third-strand-vortex-inhibition-a-paper-dart-analogy) for the fuller theory.[110]

## Omitting the boarding step for an outboard's sake, and a harder lesson on reboarding

One hull was built without the stock boarding step on the rudder at all, a deliberate omission that drew a question from a fellow builder who finds the step useful when setting off from a beach.[71] The reasoning: the step adds drag while rowing with the rudder raised and only arms for propulsion, and its plunging up and down in swells was suspected as the cause of a "plunging sound" the boat makes at anchor — see [Little Jim — a transom-slap noise at anchor, explained by trim](/boats/little-jim.md#a-transom-slap-noise-at-anchor-explained-by-trim) for how that particular mystery was actually traced. A ladder or shelf on the transom's right side was planned in its place, useful for swimming and climbing back aboard and with an eye toward taking up free-diving — not yet built.[72] The drag from the step itself was judged unlikely to be measurable at 2 knots, and the shelf idea was judged interesting.[73]

A 2008 mailing-list message gives an earlier, contemporaneous account of the same reboarding difficulty later retold in the 2012 blog post above — sitting almost at water level, the step takes being "a freak" to get a foot onto while simultaneously grabbing the top of the rudder stock to hoist up, managed only "out of pure survival instinct" once the real difficulty of waterlogged, deadweight clothing was met firsthand. The same message adds a caveat the later retelling does not: for anyone planning to mount an outboard on the port side of the transom, don't bother building the step at all, since it becomes unusable once the engine is fitted there — and recommends a rope loop, positioned lower than the step, as the better first foothold for reboarding regardless.[74]

A 1996 published construction account gives a different name and a design rationale the mailing-list analysis above never worked out: it shapes the blade's face to a "**NASA airfoil section**" — a radiused leading edge, maximum thickness about a third of the way back, and not a sharp trailing tip but a small flat, whose slight drag "pulls the water onto the blade's surface" and prevents trailing-edge turbulence.[75] This published account and the mailing-list measurement work above do not agree, and the record does not settle which is correct, or even whether "NASA airfoil section" and the mailing list's NACA 0006 comparison describe the same underlying shape. Both are presented here rather than reconciled: one is a contemporaneous published construction description with a stated design rationale, the other a later, independent measurement against named standard airfoil sections.

## Tiller construction: laminated wood versus bent metal tube

Builders have made working tillers from both **laminated hardwood** and **bent metal tubing**. One tiller was laminated from **ash**, glued up with epoxy that generated enough exothermic heat to melt the plastic pot it was mixed in; clamped to a curved forming jig for 24 hours, the laminate held its bent shape once released.[76] **Ash** is a good tiller material precisely because it bends without breaking and returns to its original shape once released — the recommended method is to steam-bend the wood in a jig, let it dry fully in the bent shape, and only then epoxy it, since a large single-pour mass of epoxy kicks hot enough to burn the wood or the mixing container; mixing smaller batches at a time avoids the problem.[77]

As a separate, faster alternative, a tiller has been made from 1 in galvanized electrical conduit — scrap, free from a scrapyard — bent to shape in about an hour including collection time. The bending method: drill a hole in a piece of 4x2 lumber slightly larger than the tube's diameter (a 32 mm hole for 25 mm tube), pass the tube through the hole, and apply gentle, steady leverage while sliding the tube through, producing a fair curve without buckling if done with practice.[76] A second bending technique suits thin-walled tubing: pack the tube with sand before bending, which prevents kinking without a forming block, and slip a larger, stronger pipe over the tube's ends for extra leverage — old tire rims make effective bender heads for this method, and it likely works well on thin-walled tube generally.[77][78]

A third, simpler lamination method needs no separate forming jig at all: bang a row of nails into a board tracing the desired curve, then laminate the tiller's wood strips directly around the nails.[79] A fourth method reaches the same curve in one piece of metal rather than a lamination: take a length of **one-inch galvanized pipe**, drill a hole through a 4x2 block sized to the pipe, and bend the pipe by working it through the hole until it takes on enough curve, then cut it to length; once fitted, the bend can be touched up further in a vice to keep the tiller clear of the under-deck foam at the extremes of its swing.[80] The bent pipe itself, once shaped this way, has been used directly as the finished tiller — no separate wood lamination needed — chosen as handier and quicker than either laminating plywood strips or steam-bending ash.[81] The tiller's own length and amount of curve are not spelled out on the plans and were left an open question on the record — absent further guidance, a builder facing the same gap can only take a reasonable guess at his own dimensions (a cross-section on the order of 1 1/2 in square is one starting guess), and cardboard section templates are one way to work out a shape before committing to material.[82][83]

For a builder unsure of the shape needed at all, an alternative to figuring it out from scratch is to reverse-engineer the required clearance directly: clamp a length of stiff wire (a wire coat-hanger will do) to the rudder stock and bend it by hand, checking as it is swung from side to side, until it clears every internal obstruction the tiller would otherwise strike.[84]

Absent stated tiller offsets on the plans, one recorded approach is to extrapolate the shape directly from sheets #4 and #11 — the same sheets showing the rudder stock and steering-line geometry the tiller must clear. A builder taking this route should allow generous curve: one tiller made this way came out with too little curve, forcing a very close shave past the vent baffle and the last deck beam on every swing; the practical fix, short of relaminating the tiller, was to thin down that deck beam rather than remake the part.[85]

Asked separately whether the tiller's own length to bulkhead 4 (700 mm) was correct, and how many degrees the rudder blade should turn, a further builder confirmed the degree figure directly: having likewise scaled the tiller's shape from sheet 4, his own turns about 30 degrees each way — matching the same 30-degrees-either-side figure independently given above for easing the rudder gently rather than hauling it hard over[50][51] — without separately confirming the length dimension.[86][87][88]
