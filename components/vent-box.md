# Vent box

The ventilation box (vent trunk) that ventilates the Paradox's closed cabin while the boat is sailed hatch-shut. The cabin's normal ventilation is good enough to be comfortable even shut up tight in bad weather, with no separate stove-exhaust vent found necessary; on cold nights, stuffing a towel into the vent box is a simple way to cut the airflow down and stay warmer. See [Alastair](/people/alastair-law.md#construction-contributions) for the fuller cabin-ventilation answer this is drawn from.

## Solar-powered ventilation

A solar-powered vent fan — self-contained, needing no battery or wiring of its own — is a useful add-on for extra airflow, particularly with the cookstove running.[1] A Paradox-sized Enigma-style build mounts one on the foredeck in place of the stock vent trunk described above, joking that solar vents do little for a warm night once it's moonlight rather than sunlight doing the work.[2]

## Design rationale: ventilation for the Bahamas, and cold-climate adaptations

The vent box exists chiefly because [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) designed the Paradox for the Bahamas, generally much warmer than a temperate cruising ground, and wanted a steady supply of fresh air through the cabin.[3] Air enters through a large circular opening in the foredeck and is drawn into the cabin from there; a second, inspection-cum-vent hatch in the transom encourages the flow, so that with its cover removed air passes from the front of the cabin, through the lazarette, and out the stern — a path chosen because the plans originally sited a gimballed cooker in the lazarette, needing somewhere for fumes and heat to go.[3] In a colder climate the aft vent is opened far less often than in the Bahamas, since keeping the cabin warm outweighs the wish for airflow; one cold-climate owner reports fitting a small charcoal stove for cabin heating instead.[3] Where the transom vent cover is made of Perspex rather than the stock design's opaque plastic inspection hatch, repeated tightening of its retaining nut can crack the Perspex around the pin hole; a plywood replacement solves it, at the cost of the light a clear cover otherwise lets into the lazarette.[3]

## Internal fittings: furling-line and topping-lift blocks

Inside the vent box, blocks for the furling line and the topping lift are best mounted one behind the other rather than side by side, since a side-by-side pair leaves too little room for the mast itself to pass through on the way to its step.[4] Small screws alone are not adequate to retain these blocks against the working loads they see: one such block pulled clean away from the bulkhead, left attached "only by the grace of God," and both blocks are better refitted on stainless bolts, washers, and nuts through the bulkhead instead.[4][5]

## A vent-box leak, diagnosed across a multi-post repair sequence

A leak reported, but not pinpointed, by a previous owner as being "somewhere in the region of vent box where the mast is stepped" set the priority for a subsequent refit: find where water is entering the boat, and stop it.[6] The mast itself can complicate the search — on at least one hull its cross-section did not match the moulded socket at the bottom of the vent box, and a wad of epoxied woven roving blocked full insertion; wedging the mast rather than fitting it perfectly is the recommended cure, since a too-perfect fit lets a wet, swollen mast become almost impossible to extract.[7]

Finding the actual leak took a sequence of exploratory repairs, described by their own author as a series of "operations." The first cleared a lump of epoxied woven roving obstructing the mast step's recess, then sealed the drainpipe there with epoxy and epoxy putty.[8] The second opened the bilge around the block the drainpipe passes through, where a tangled, spike-covered mass of resin-saturated woven roving — meant to keep water out of the forward locker but instead letting it in around the drainpipe itself — was chiselled out and replaced with epoxy and epoxy putty.[8] A third "operation," on the vent box's own throat, sewed together a split in the fibreglass with new cloth to stop water entering the vent from above deck reaching the cabin or bilge.[9] A fourth and final step probed the drain where it exits the vent box through the bottom of the hull: scraping the joint with a penknife blade — a self-described "biopsy" — found no fractures or open crevices between the epoxied roving, the plastic pipe, and the plywood, and the area was coated with epoxy anyway as a precaution.[10] The eventual verdict was necessarily provisional: "In theory, the boat should no longer leak. In practice we shall see."[10]

## Access to the vent box: a removable side panel versus gluing it in place

Finishing a vent trunk raises a real design choice about its own side panel: leave it removable for access, or glue it in for good.[11] Builders have taken three different approaches. One left only the top half of the panel removable, with no gasket and no leakage — the halyard-turnaround block is mounted on the removable panel itself, and every line can be worked through the vent opening on deck without the panel ever needing to come off; access is valued chiefly for inspecting the inside and repainting it.[12] A second boat went further, leaving the whole side panel removable rather than just its top half, again with no leaks — the drain hole under the mast step sits above the waterline, and even in genuinely rough conditions, with waves regularly breaking over the foredeck and water reaching the vent box, this has caused no problems.[13] A third builder considered a removable panel but glued it in permanently instead, reasoning he would have sealed it with the same product used on the windows had he left it removable; has never actually felt the need to get inside since, though cutting an access panel later would be straightforward if ever needed.[14]

Weighing these three approaches for his own build, one builder settled on a simple **access hatch** rather than either extreme, planning to leak-test it by pouring buckets of water in once the epoxy had fully cured. Full account at [JoHanna — the vent trunk's starboard side panel: an access hatch built](/boats/johanna.md#the-vent-trunks-starboard-side-panel-an-access-hatch-built).[15]

## A 2009 drain-routing question, and a mast-step electrical-contact detail

A newcomer just starting his own build asked, among other plan-reading questions, whether the vent trunk's drain runs all the way through the hull to the outside.[16] It does: any rain running down the mast, or sea water washing over the decks, drains straight out through the bottom — "simple and elegant."[17] Building that drain from a copper tube rather than plastic pipe, so it can double as the mast's lightning-ground conductor (see [Ironsides — building the vent trunk drain](/boats/ironsides.md#building-the-vent-trunk-drain)), only works if the portion of tube standing in the water is left bare — no paint, no fiberglass — with the electrical connection made at the tube's *upper* end instead, where a tab or finger crosses the mast-step pocket for the mast's own foot to stand on.[18]

## A computational airflow study of the vent trunk, and where it falls short

A set of CFD (computational-fluid-dynamics) studies modeling cabin air flow through different port-hole placements for the closed-hatch condition, aimed at finding an optimum vent layout, was uploaded to the group's Files area in 2009 as a small folder of PDF files.[19] At least one correspondent found the files corrupted and unable to open, a problem not resolved on record.[20] A follow-up single-file study modeled cabin air flow with the hatch left in different positions instead.[21] Real-world experience pushes back on the model's completeness: it does not appear to account for the **transom vent**, and observed cabin air flow with the hatch closed does not, in practice, follow the pattern the model predicts.[22]

A comparable flow-through design seen on other small cruisers uses paired "Wiley ports," one at each end of the cabin, admitting air while excluding spray and water — the same basic goal as the vent box's own single foredeck opening — with airflow slowed on a cool night by stuffing something into the windward port.[23] Such ports are sometimes fitted with wedges that hold them open, shut, or at an intermediate position; where a design's own stock plans call instead for the baffle to be glued in permanently, building it from a clear material such as polycarbonate is one way to keep at least some light and visibility even without that adjustability.[24][25]

## A temporary summertime vent scoop, improvised from a pipe elbow

For extra ventilation and cooling while anchored out in hot weather, a scoop can be improvised from an ordinary **4 in pipe-size elbow**, whose roughly 5 in outside diameter is a tight, press-in fit for the standard 5 in forward vent hole — bought at a hardware store for about **$5**, with a slight taper ground onto one end and seated with a firm blow from the fist. Because it stands too proud to leave in place under way — it fouls the [tack strop](/components/rig/tack-strop.md) — it is fitted only at anchor and pulled before sailing.[26]

## A wind-sail alternative proposed, though not built

A cross-shaped fabric funnel — two pieces of cloth sewn into a "+", its base fitted to the vent opening and its top held up by a halyard or similar line — was proposed as a more efficient, easier-to-stow alternative to the pipe-elbow scoop above, catching wind from any direction rather than just one; sized to a 4 in opening, a catchment panel roughly 2 ft high was suggested.[27] Asked to sketch the idea in more detail,[28] the proposal instead drew good-natured skepticism — that it would likely work better as an exhaust than an intake, with a top cap supported by a hoop floated as a possible fix[29] — and no drawing or further detail followed on record.
