# Toe rail

The toe rail runs along each deck edge, at the join between deck and topsides. It is **part of the stock design**, drawn on the plans rather than an owner's addition.[1]

## What it is actually for

Two purposes are commonly claimed for a toe rail, and only one of them survives contact with the boat. The first is **channelling rainwater** off a smooth deck — a function a builder who intends to texture the deck surface anyway can reasonably question, since a non-skid finish does not shed water the way a smooth painted deck does.[2] The second, and the one experience settles decisively, is **footing**: a season sailed with no toe rail at all proved genuinely difficult for moving forward on deck, and fitting one afterwards "makes things much easier."[3] The rail is therefore worth fitting on a Paradox regardless of how the deck is finished, and it is a safety fitting more than a drainage one.[1][3]

That said, the deck is not somewhere a Paradox sailor spends much time. The rail's value is real but occasional, since the boat is sailed from inside the closed cabin and there is rarely a compelling reason to go on deck at sea in any small boat.[1] Separate **handholds** are correspondingly unnecessary: the hatch slides on the cabin top already serve as handrails.[4] See [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-catalogue-of-conventional-offshore-features-weighed-and-mostly-declined) for the wider offshore-fittings catalogue this judgement belongs to.

## Why the plans' shape, rather than a bought aluminium extrusion

The slotted aluminium toe rail sold for larger yachts is a natural thing for a builder to look for, and hard to source as a standalone part — the examples that turn up are pre-fitted to complete, expensive boats.[2] The stock design is the better answer anyway, and for reasons beyond drainage. The plans' shape is **cheap**, **cuts windage**, and **does not collect dirt** the way a right-angled extrusion does. A bolted-on aluminium rail would stand proud of the deck at the wrong angle, sit higher than the rail as drawn, and open a potential **leak path into the hull structure at every mounting screw**, mastic sealant notwithstanding. Its one distinctive advantage — holes to hang fenders from — is already served by the coachroof handrails. And a Paradox sits at just the right height to be sat on at the deck edge with your feet in the sand when she is beached, a use an aluminium rail would make painful.[5]

The practical upshot is that builders end up building the rail rather than buying it, whatever the material.[6]

## Materials builders have used

**Wood** is the default, and the choice a builder generally arrives at once the metal option is talked through.[6] For a rail that must hold a fair curve without warping, locally available **fir**, **split and laminated** rather than used in one solid piece, is a documented approach — the same treatment applied to the [yuloh](/components/yuloh.md) on the same build.[7] Being brightwork along the sheer, the rail is also a natural candidate for **varnish rather than paint**, finished alongside the hand rails, hatch board, cabin rails, and spars, with the hull's paint stripe run just below it.[8]

A non-wood alternative in use is **Trex plastic composite decking**, fitted as a combined **rub rail and toe rail** — a material chosen for a bow area that also carries an impact bumper.[9]

## Sheathing an awkward shape

The toe rail is one of the harder parts of the boat to fibreglass, since cloth does not want to lie into the angle it makes with the deck. Two approaches are on record from Layden-boat refits. Cutting several layers of light **2–4 oz cloth on the bias** lets the glass conform to the tricky shape, used alongside 6 oz cloth on the flat deck and sides.[10] Alternatively the **toe rail/deck joint** itself can be sealed with two layers of **4 oz glass**, distinct from the 6 oz cloth over the deck as a whole.[11]

A third, sequencing answer sidesteps the problem: **take the rails off**, sheathe the deck as a clean flat surface, then **reinstall the rails and reinforce them with a layer of glass tape**.[12] Where the rails are left on, finishing them is worth completing before the cabin trunk is started, since the cabin structure gets in the way of working along the sheer afterwards.[13]

## A variant considered but not built: the rail outside the hull

Fitting the toe rails to the **outside of the hull** rather than on top of the deck would let them double as a **rubbing strip**. It is listed among the things one long-term owner would try if building again, but it has not been built on a Paradox.[14]

## Under way: the rail going under

On a boat whose windward chine runner is meant to be near the surface, the leeward toe rail dipping is a normal sight rather than a warning. Sailed in an estimated 15–25 knots, the toe rail going under was "never an issue" — easing the sheet stands the boat right back up.[15] See [sailing characteristics](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md) for the angle-of-heel picture this belongs to.
