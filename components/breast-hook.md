# Breast hook

The breast hook is the structural knee tying the two topsides together at the bow, just aft of the stem.

## A part the plans call for, but the panel layout leaves out

The breast hook is one of a handful of plywood parts specified in the plans that do not appear anywhere on the ten-sheet cutting diagram — the same omission affects the vent trunk pieces (18 mm front, 9 mm sides) and the butt blocks.[1] The gap was noticed independently by builders years apart: it was flagged at the very first day of marking out panels in 2001,[1] and turned up again in 2005 in a first-time builder's systematic page-by-page parts list, which counted it among the parts that appeared to demand extra plywood beyond the ten sheets.[2] It is not a printing fault so much as a characteristic of the plans, a great deal of whose interior detail is left to the builder's own discretion.[3]

No extra sheet is needed for it. The working answer is to watch for the omitted parts when cutting out and to plan the sheets so the offcuts will carry them, rather than ordering more ply.[1] See [panel layout and nesting](/construction/panel-layout.md#layout-tips) for the wider nesting problem this belongs to.

## Listed at 12 mm, built from 18 mm offcut

The plans' own parts list gives the breast hook as a 12 mm plywood piece.[2] In practice at least one completed boat's was cut instead from a leftover piece of 18 mm — the thicker stock being what happened to be spare once the sheets carrying it had given up their planned parts.[4]

## Nesting it on the 18 mm sheet

Because 18 mm is the most expensive plywood in the bill of materials, whether the vent trunk sides and front *and* the breast hook can all be got from a single 18 mm sheet is worth settling before any cutting starts.[5] They can, provided the sheet is re-nested rather than cut as drawn. Taking the bottom rudder piece, flipping it about a horizontal axis and moving it as far to the left as it will go, so that it fits inside bulkhead 3, opens room in the scrap above it for the breast hook, and in the wedge at the lower right for the vent trunk front. The vent trunk sides then come off the 9 mm sheet carrying the foredeck instead, one from each of its wedge-shaped offcuts.[6]

The re-nesting pays a second dividend unrelated to the breast hook: laid out as drawn, the rudder's nose falls off the edge of the sheet, whereas the revised arrangement makes it easy to let in a small infill piece when the two rudder sides are joined.[6] Where no CAD system is available for juggling this, taping newspaper together and cutting the components out full size gives pieces that can be shuffled around the sheet until they fit — the same paper-pattern method recommended for the layout generally.[6]

## The bow insulation must go in before the breast hook

Fitting the breast hook closes off the space it spans, so the sequence around it is fixed: the insulation panels at the bow, between the stem and bulkhead #1, have to be installed *before* the breast hook goes in, and are glued down with contact cement.[7] This is the one point in the build order where the part's timing is not a matter of preference. It is distinct from the four hull-side insulation panels between bulkheads #2 and #4, which are deliberately left until the very last, since working around them punches holes in them.[7] See [hull assembly sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#recommended-order) for the stem-first order this falls within.

## Backing for a bow ring bolt

Beyond tying the topsides together, the breast hook is the natural backing for any substantial fitting through the bow. A half-inch screw-in ring at the stemhead, offered as strong enough to hoist a whole loaded boat and therefore beyond worry as an anchoring point, drew exactly that qualification in reply — that a robust breast hook behind such a ring bolt would not go amiss, along with a larger washer or a stainless strip to spread the load.[8] The exchange concerned an Enigma-derived build rather than a Paradox (see [Question](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md#question-an-enigma-ketch-of-his-own-design)), but the principle applies to the Paradox's own bow, where the question of what the ground tackle's working load actually bears against is treated at [attaching ground tackle at the bow](/operations/ground-tackle.md#attaching-ground-tackle-at-the-bow-cleats-a-stem-u-bolt-and-chafe-protection).
