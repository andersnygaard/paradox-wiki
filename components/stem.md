# Stem

The Paradox's bow stem: the timber it is made from, its notching, and how it is assembled into the hull.

The stem post is 100 mm (4") thick. A sculling oar has no reverse and no brake, so contact with an obstruction while sculling is unavoidable — the stem's thickness is what leaves the boat undamaged when it happens.[1]

## Timber grade for the stem

Laminating the stem where it runs back at roughly 29-30 degrees takes three laminations of about 700 mm each, cut from an 8 ft length of 2x8 (1.5 in x 7.25 in) stock. Choosing between clear vertical-grain Douglas fir and ordinary #2 construction-grade lumber for that stock — at 2022 US Pacific Northwest prices, roughly $150-200 for the clear-grain length against about a tenth of that for #2 — drew two opposing views and no resolution on record.[6] One held that inexpensive construction-grade material suits an amateur build throughout, since anything glued and nailed into the boat can be un-built again and repaired or upgraded later.[7] The other, drawn from experience building a different, non-Paradox design with cut corners on material choice, held that skimping shortens a boat's working life — that one lasted only about seven years — and recommended following the designer's own material list, thinking of the wood's cost as spread across the boat's whole working life rather than paid once.[8] A third view held that species and store grade matter less than the individual piece in hand: wood that is kiln-dried, straight, free of knots over about 1/2 in across, and free of twist is safe to use for the stem regardless of its official grade, since grading exists partly to protect a buyer from real defects and partly to serve the mill's own pricing; a piece salvaged from an old torn-down building — a floor joist, say — often comes already seasoned dry for decades and free of any real defect beyond a few old nail holes.[9] Sound fibreglass work over the finished stem keeps out moisture and prevents rot regardless of the grade chosen, provided the lay-up itself is done well; vinylester resin, priced and performing between polyester and epoxy and notably water-resistant, was suggested as a further option for that glass work.[10] One build under way in mid-2022 used construction-grade Nordic spruce for the stem and for most of the hull, reserving knot-free spruce specifically for the mast and avoiding pine as more prone to rot.[11]

## Protecting the stem from wear

After months of use, the stem's lower curve takes repeated contact with beaches and rocks. Building the stem thicker than the plans specify is not enough on its own to prevent this — one owner still found considerable wear after doing so.[4] Rather than repair the stem itself, a sacrificial piece of wood fitted over the wear area is simply replaced every few years instead.[2] Epoxying a strip of stainless steel over the same curved area serves the same protective purpose.[3] A third material tried later on the same problem: opening out a length of copper water pipe into a sheet, hammering it to shape, and bonding it on with epoxy — a fix applied where hard beaching and a trailer's snubbing block had worn the bottom of the stem, alongside the same treatment on the rudder (see [rudder — a proven copper-pipe leading edge](/components/rudder.md#a-proven-copper-pipe-leading-edge-and-a-dacron-rope-alternative)).[5]
