# Windows

The Paradox is glazed all round. Because the boat is sailed from inside a fully closed cabin, the windows are not a convenience — they are how the sailor sees out, and they are part of the structure that keeps the sea outside. The plans draw a **continuous side window** down each side of the cabin, plus a **forward** and an **aft (rear) window** wrapped around the ends.[1]

## The rear window is structure, not an opening port

The most consequential fact about Paradox's glazing is that the **rear window is an integral part of the cabin structure**, and it is what makes the boat much more watertight in the event of a severe knockdown or a complete roll-over. Removable or multi-position rear windows of the kind fitted to [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) are a different design proposition on a different boat; converting a Paradox's rear window to open is done at the owner's own risk.[2] This is the structural reason behind a preference recorded elsewhere in the archive on comfort grounds — that hinged front or side windows are too much of a leak risk to fit for extra ventilation, while a removable rear window is the one that could work (see [hatch — a 2011 canvass for a possible commissioned rebuild](/components/hatch.md#a-2011-canvass-for-a-possible-commissioned-rebuild-hinged-versus-sliding-and-a-washboard-alternative)). Real-world experience bears the warning out directly: a non-standard matchboard drop-board substituted for the fixed panel on one boat produced fine stress cracks in the unreinforced deck around its own hatch opening, and a similarly open rear cabin on a second boat proved too hard to keep genuinely waterproof and weakening enough to the cabin that its owner closed it up and rebuilt her to the stock fixed panel.[3]

## The end windows wrap, and seat on the corner posts at an angle

The forward and aft windows are not flat panels: each wraps around the end of the cabin, and the **forward window carries the more pronounced curve** of the two. The plans' corner-post detail on sheet 10 dimensions the two angles this produces — **30 degrees and 14 degrees** — which set how each end window seats once wrapped around the post, the forward window needing the larger of the two. Neither is critical to take exactly off the drawing; both can be tweaked at assembly with a sanding disc on an angle grinder.[4] The glazing material itself is flexible enough that the bend is not the difficulty it looks: a 20 × 300 mm polycarbonate offcut can be bent into half a reef knot without breaking.[5] See [deck and cabin beams](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#bending-polycarbonate-glazing-around-the-forward-and-aft-window-curves) for fairing the beam profiles the glazing must follow.

## Framed openings, or openings routed into the cabin sides

Builders have taken two routes to holding the glazing. One is to **rout the window shapes and their recesses directly into the cabin sides**, dispensing with separate frames and bedding the glazing into the recess — a method that needs a router bit long enough to clear the 9 mm plywood.[6] The other is a **built-up frame**: on one beamless cabin top the forward window frame was laminated from **two layers of plywood**, stitched and glued into position, and judged as strong as the framed original while lighter.[7] Where a wooden frame is used, the cabin's fibreglass cloth is run down the sides to the **edge of that frame**, so no plywood edge is left unsealed.[8]

### A smaller window opening cut into a non-standard cabin

The routed-in method above was used on a cabin that was not the plans' cabin at all. Its builder abandoned the drawn cabin and made the sides and front from **12 mm plywood** he already had, deliberately routing **smaller openings** than the plans call for, and glazed them in **3 mm polycarbonate at the sides and 6 mm at the front** — the rear window a **lift-out 6 mm pane** rather than a bonded-in one, despite the structural role the rear window normally plays.[9] Polycarbonate cuts with an ordinary saw and shapes with sandpaper, and he **sanded and polished the cut edges smooth** on the theory that a rough edge gives a crack somewhere to start — a precaution he stated plainly he could not vouch for.[9] See [boats/enuf.md](/boats/enuf.md#what-the-published-build-article-adds).

A third approach, argued for offshore work, replaces both with **traditional framed windows set in locking rubber channel**, epoxy-filleted to the cabin and tabbed in with cloth rather than through-bolted — the reasoning being that thicker glazing held by many screws creates a stress point at every hole, while the rubber channel doubles as a gasket for a storm cover and makes replacement far easier.[10] On the underlying question of whether the stock glazing is strong enough at all, see [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md#a-catalogue-of-conventional-offshore-features-weighed-and-mostly-declined).

## Drainage, and fitting order

A **small drain hole at the rear lower corner of each side window** is a documented refinement, added from experience — though largely unnecessary in practice, since closing the hatch before water starts coming in deals with the same problem.[11] Condensation running off the inside of the glazing is a separate matter, treated with a drip rail, a moulded gutter, or drain holes; see [window glazing materials](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md#cabin-condensation-a-drip-rail-a-moulded-gutter-and-window-drain-holes).

In build order the **back and side windows generally go in before the front one**, with deck painting and the remaining deck fittings following.[12]

## Departures builders have made

The plans' continuous side window is one of the more commonly altered details. One build replaced it with **7 × 14 in oval ports** finished with trim rings — a personal touch to make the boat "just a little different from all the rest," with no loss of visibility the owner could detect, carried over to a one-piece lift-out hatch fitted with its own oval port so the boom stays in view from inside.[13] Others have changed the glazing material, tint, or thickness rather than the opening; those choices are treated at [window glazing materials](/construction/window-glazing-materials.md).

## A thickness failure, and a caution against skimping

One completed Paradox's original **1/8 in plexiglass** side windows **failed after five years** in service, prompting their replacement — a caution against skimping on window thickness at build time.[14] Clearing the old silicone bedding compound during that same replacement had no real shortcut — a razor-blade scraper, then rough sandpaper, proved the best combination found.[3]
