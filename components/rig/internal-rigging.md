# Internal rigging

Internal rigging is what distinguishes the Paradox from almost every other sailing boat her size: **every control line comes inside the hull**, so the boat is steered, hoisted, reefed and sheeted by a sailor sitting in the cabin with the hatch shut. "All control lines lead inside the cabin, including control of the rudder."[1]

The category overlaps with [running rigging](/components/rig/running-rigging.md) by design, because the sail-handling lines are led inside too. The division used here is where a line does its work: the **rudder line** and the inboard runs, cleats, and hull penetrations belong to this page; the halyard, mainsheet, boom haul and topping lift as sail controls — and the table of their dimensions — belong to [running rigging](/components/rig/running-rigging.md).

## The rudder line

The Paradox is steered by rope rather than by a hand on the tiller. A line attached to the tiller "runs in a continuous loop at the sides of the cabin," carried on "four small pulley blocks at each corner of that loop," so a sailor "can sit anywhere in the cabin and steer the boat," including all the way forward.[2] Pulling the side of the loop nearest the hand swings the tiller that way — the boat is reined exactly as a horse is.[3]

Because the line is small in diameter and is held rather than cleated, grip is the real ergonomic concern, answered either by **knots worked into the line** (drawn on the plans, though which knot is left to the builder) or by **moulded handles** seized one either side, positioned to fall to hand from the seat.[2][3] The full treatment — the blocks' positions against the plans' drawn locations, locking the helm off, tiller extensions, and diagnosing stiff steering — is at [internal steering](/components/steering.md), and is not repeated here.

## Where the lines come to hand

Inside the cabin, **three cleats on the forward bulkhead** lock off the **boom haul (furling line)**, the **topping lift**, and the **halyard** — "so all controls are inside."[3] The rudder line and the mainsheet both fall to hand from the same seated position, so that setting a course means tweaking the steering line every few minutes or so and otherwise leaving the boat alone.[3]

The mainsheet arrives at the helmsman **from behind** rather than from in front, and its slack falls into a bin behind bulkhead 4 where it cannot tangle round his feet — an arrangement that works precisely because a Paradox's sheet is set and locked rather than continuously played; see [rig — why the mainsheet presented at the helmsman's back is not awkward in practice](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-mainsheet-presented-at-the-helmsmans-back-is-not-awkward-in-practice).

## Where the lines pierce the hull

Every line led inside has to cross the hull somewhere, and each crossing is a potential leak.

- The **halyard** enters through a **deck tube** at the mast.[4]
- The **rudder line** leaves through the transom's tiller opening, closed by the [vent baffle](/components/vent-baffle.md); why that opening exists and whether it is necessary is treated at [transom deck plate — the vent baffle](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#the-vent-baffle-why-the-tiller-rope-opening-exists-and-whether-its-necessary).
- Where steering lines pierce a **bulkhead** instead, individual **neoprene boots** have been made up per line for watertightness in a flooding event — done on [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) with matching boots for the halyard and reef lines, directly analogous to [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s single tiller boot.[5]

## Keeping the tails out from underfoot

With every line brought into a cabin roughly 3 ft by 6 ft, the **loose tail** is a genuine nuisance rather than a tidiness question. Two solutions are on record. One makes the halyard and boom haul from **opposite ends of a single 12-metre rope**, leaving only about 4 ft of loose rope in the cabin altogether.[6] The other fits small **hanging line bags** — rock-climbers' chalk bags, with a rigid lip and hanging loops, one per sheet — and gives each line a **different colour**, adopted after 3/16 in line proved to be "everywhere" underfoot while sailing.[7]
