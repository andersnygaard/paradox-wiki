# Furling system

The [Paradox rig](/components/rig/index.md) reefs and stows its sail by **rolling it up on the boom** (the "rolling boom") rather than by conventional slab reefing. The stock arrangement, the fixes builders developed for it, and the technique the designer himself uses are documented below.

## The stock gear's inherent difficulty: a three-hands problem

Operating the furling gear as designed effectively needs **three hands** — one to ease the halyard, one to pull in the furling line, and one to hold up the boom so it doesn't go overboard ("man lost a lot when he lost his tail"). The friction is compounded by the **round thimble** the plans call for to run the reefing line through. The reefing/furling system has been flagged as the one part of the design giving pause, precisely because it is unusual.[1][2][3]

Even so, the stock, unmodified gear is workable: raising sail means releasing the furling/reefing line and pulling the halyard while holding up the boom; reefing means easing the halyard while pulling in the furling line at the same time, a two-handed operation with some friction but "not too bad." A typical reef on [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) takes about **30 seconds**. **Matt Layden**, by contrast, **furls/reefs Paradox faster** than that, and can **reduce sail downwind in strong winds (20-25 kt)** where other sailors must head up into the wind to reef. This is the clearest evidence that the furling system's difficulty is not inherent to the design but at least partly a matter of **operator technique** — the designer works the gear noticeably faster and more capably than other experienced sailors of very similar boats, including in conditions where they need to change point of sail to cope. The [original Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md), "Paradox #1," was available in the water for other builders to inspect firsthand.[4]

## Glen Maxwell's topping-lift solution

The problem was met in earnest on the December 2001 [Cedar Key to Honeymoon Island sea trial](/boats/zoe.md#sea-trials-route-cedar-key-to-clearwater), where it appeared in two distinct forms. The **tang on the end of the boom was not stiff enough**: it bent inward and periodically bound while the main was being rolled up, which furled the **mainsheet around the sail** and locked the whole assembly. Because this happened while attention was directed forward, it was typically discovered only once the mess was made, forcing the main to be hoisted again and untangled. Separately, furling was harder than hoped because **the sail fell as the halyard was slacked**, making the furling line difficult to haul. Everything worked if the boom was supported during furling, and the only way to do that with both hands free for halyard and furling line was to let the boom rest on the coach roof. A **temporary topping lift** rigged during the trip's mid-point layover confirmed the diagnosis, and a permanent one controlled from below went onto the punch list at the cruise's end.[5]

The fix for the three-hands problem was a **topping lift** to take the boom's weight during furling or raising:

- A **temporary standing topping lift** ran from the **end of the boom** to the **masthead**, adjustable via a **trucker's hitch** reachable from the cabin hatch, so it could be slacked to let the boom settle into its stowed resting place.
- A second topping lift supported the **front of the boom**, running from about **6 ft above deck** to the front of the furling axle.
- In use: take up the **forward** topping lift, then the **aft** one — freeing the boom to turn so the sail can be raised, taking care to keep the yard on the correct side of the topping lift.
- The system **worked very well**; raising and lowering sail was "no longer a chore." The one remaining problem was that it was easy to over-tension the topping lift so it rode against the sail.[3]

**Furling on the side of the boom away from the mast** matters too: it makes the furling line exit the drum on the outboard side, giving a fair lead to the reefing-line thimble. Lacking the proper-size thimble called for in the plans, a nylon bulls-eye fair lead can substitute, drilled and tapped into the furling axle — though this is "not much more friction free" than the stock brass thimble would have been. The real source of furling friction, it turned out, was not the thimble at all but the topping lift itself, before one is fitted.[6]

On glassing the hull bottom: a **single layer of fiberglass** is fine unless hard groundings are frequent, but a **heavy lay-up** is worth it on a from-scratch "keeper" boat — building a replacement while still sailing the older boat lets lessons learned be incorporated directly into the new build (see [topside and hull painting choices](/construction/topside-painting.md) for the related repainting decision on that same secondhand boat).[1][6]

## The running topping lift and internal reroute

To fix the over-tensioning problem with the standing topping lift, a **running topping lift** solves it: the line enters through a deck fitting just inboard of the furling line (alongside the halyard, furling, and tack lines), runs to a **double block** — one sheave for the furling line, the other for the topping lift — sharing the same **U-strap** that already holds the furling-line block, then continues through the **vent box** beside the furling line to a **cam cleat**. Run this way, the topping lift needed no further tending: it gave **no further problems** once installed, and the control could be led into the cabin so the hatch need not be opened even for foul-weather reefing — a position that also makes sense because the sailor must be at the mast base anyway to guide the furled sail into its stowed position.[7][8]

## Full hardware and rigging recipe

The fullest documented hardware description for a working topping-lift/furling setup:

- **Boom-end strap**: a strap with an **eye at each end and a hole in the middle**, made from a **stainless steel tang, 6 in x 1 in** (West Marine part# 129544). The **main sheet** attaches to the strap's bottom hole; the strap mounts to the **boom end** using the middle hole; the **1/8 in topping lift** ties to the top of the strap. The strap **must be very strong** to resist bending inward and fouling the boom during furling — it took **three straps** before the design and material were right.
- **Blocks**: **Harken #021 dinghy blocks** throughout.
- **Topping-lift routing**: from the boom-end strap, the topping lift runs up to the **masthead** through a Harken bullet block, falls back down to about **3 ft above the deck** where a loop is tied in the line, then the bitter end is taken back up to about **5-6 ft above deck** and through a **second bullet block** mounted to the **forward face of the mast**, falling from there to the **furling axle**, where it ties off to a **brazed-on brass sail grommet**.
- **Downhaul/control line**: a separate **1/4 in line** ties to the loop described above; after adjusting both the forward and aft lift lines, a pull on this single line raises the **boom at both ends simultaneously**. A temporary cleat secures the downhaul until the system is tested and tuned.
- **In use**: after the sail is furled, slack the topping lift and guide the sail into its stowed position; to make sail, pull the topping lift to raise the furled sail to just above the coach roof, hoist, then slack the topping lift again so it doesn't interfere with the set sail. Care is needed that the **aft end of the yard doesn't slip behind the topping lift** until about 1/4 of the sail has been unrolled.[9]

A second, independent builder reached the same conclusion about the strap's stiffness by a different route — see [Scout — rig deviations](/boats/scout.md#rig-deviations-a-21-halyard-purchase-and-a-stainless-mainsheet-chainplate).

Photos and a drawing of the arrangement are held in the group's files-section folder, "Topping Lift" (see [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) for related storm-tactics context).[10][11][12][13] The same hardware exchange also carried a parallel offer of [bronze rudder-gudgeon hardware](/construction/rudder-bearing-materials.md) made available to the list around the same time.[14]

## A further boom-end refinement: tang spacing, and a high topping-lift join (January 2014)

Answering a fellow owner's account of a topping-lift/sheet tangle that produced a knockdown on "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)" (see [boats/ironsides.md — a first sail at Lake Amistad, Texas](/boats/ironsides.md#a-first-sail-at-lake-amistad-texas-and-a-topping-liftfurling-snarl-january-2014)), Alastair Law reported an identical mishap of his own on an early outing in too-strong wind: the topping lift and sheet wrapped up with the sail, knocking the boat down with water coming in where the hatch was not quite shut — the boat "shrugged it off... and carried on as if nothing had happened." Two changes fixed it for good: spacing the boom-end tang further out from the boom's end, and setting the topping lift's own join as high as possible, so the pull on the boom's end runs as close to vertical as it can. The knock-down itself, in hindsight, built confidence rather than shook it — a conventional open dinghy handled the same way would likely have meant a serious swim, where the Paradox cost only a wet sleeve.[129]

## Build-tolerance lesson: the tack-strop pipe

The plans-length pipe cut for the tack strop should be cut **a little long (with about 2 in of trim allowance)**, then trimmed down once clearance is confirmed — cutting it too short leaves the rolled-up sail sitting very tight against the cabin side when swinging up, and a too-short pipe cannot be lengthened again short of remaking the fitting from a fresh length of pipe (an **8 ft length of schedule-40 brass pipe** is enough stock to redo it).[15]

A possible plan-sheet discrepancy has been flagged in the same fitting: **Sheet 9** gives the tack-strop spacer pipe as 1/4 in (6 mm) inside diameter with an outside diameter of 14 mm, while the strop itself is specified at 8 mm — leaving the strop seemingly loose inside the spacer rather than a snug fit.[16] Read plainly, the 6 mm figure is the strop **rope's** own diameter running inside a 14 mm OD pipe, not a snug-fit spec — 12 mm water pipe (standard stock in both the UK and Australia) works as the spacer in practice.[17][18]

A taller cabin top raises the same clearance question from the other direction: fitting a longer strop to clear the extra height is one option, but reshaping the cabin instead — increasing the curvature of the cabin top, or raising it only toward the rear — can restore the needed clearance without lengthening anything, since the only true foul point for the furled sail against the cabin is the corner where the side and the top meet, near the front.[19]

## Hardware materials: stainless steel and tufnol as bronze/plastic substitutes

Several of the plans' fabricated bronze and cast-plastic fittings can be built in more locally available materials without a functional penalty. Stainless steel is a viable substitute for "the Bronze bits," but the plans actually call for two different fitting types under that label: the **ringnails** (fine in galvanized steel if fully encapsulated in epoxy, though a galvanized nail won't last as long as bronze) versus the fabricated hoops that hold the stowed yuloh and mast, the rudder hardware, and the boom's forward reefing fitting. For that second group, **tufnol and stainless steel** work for the rudder fittings and boom axle, and more of the same water pipe used for the tack-strop spacer serves to make the holding hoops — with no problems reported.[16][20][18]

**Tufnol** is an engineering composite, resembling bakelite in appearance, made of linen fabric and resin. It machines easily, resists reaction with most chemicals, and is fairly expensive; it comes in blocks and sheets, and offcuts can be sourced through a workplace rather than buying full stock. Chosen for the rudder fittings over building the plans' own composite fitting simply when a piece large enough is already on hand, it should perform similarly to the plans-specified part.[21][22]

Separately, the plans' masthead lightning-dissipation rod (see [lightning protection](/design/lightning-protection.md)) need not be bronze either: **marine-grade aluminium is an acceptable substitute** for the rod itself.

Bending bronze rod to shape — as when forming the furling rod itself — is safe to do hot: aside from work-hardening some alloys may already carry from mill forming, heating bronze to bend it does not meaningfully weaken or soften it once cooled, or does so only to a very small degree.[112]

## Bending the furling rod: two working methods

A 2011 question about who actually bends the roller-furling rod, and how, drew two different working answers.[115] The quick version: a strong vice, a long lever, and a big hammer.[116] The fuller cold-and-hot method: lathe a shallow groove into the rod first, at the point where a small brass screw — passing through the boom into the bore, right next to the reefing spool — will locate and secure it; clamp the rod in a bench vice fitted with soft copper jaws so the bronze isn't marred; heat only the single spot to be bent red hot with an oxy-acetylene torch; then bend it using a length of pipe slipped over the end as a lever. Hot bronze moves with little force and "no drama at all."[117]

## Interest from outside the Paradox community

The rolling-boom system has also drawn attention from builders of other small cruisers. A Bolger Micro Navigator fitted with a 200 sq ft lug sail (in place of the plans' chinese gaff cat yawl rig) — whose own three-reef-point sail requires opening the main hatch and leaning out to reef by hand — raised the question of whether Paradox's furling gear was specifically designed to avoid that problem, whether the approach could be adapted to other lug rigs, and how the furling drum and tack-line swivel are built.[23] Adapting the rig to a different boat requires first placing the sail's center of effort where **Matt Layden** placed it on Paradox, or balance will suffer. The roller furling, despite looking complicated, is "about as simple a solution as I have come across" once built — a bronze shaft riding in a bronze/copper pipe inside the boom end turns a furling drum via a line led below deck, and because the rolled sail and yard must be set aside to clear the hatch when not in use, the tack fitting passes through a pipe that swivels up or down into position. The plan sheets themselves are not shared outside the group, the furling system being Matt Layden's own unique development; the group's photo section and a specific Files-section photo (`TL07.jpg`, under "topping lift") show the arrangement.[24][25] That Micro Navigator project went on to a set of Paradox plans and a home-built yard and boom along traditional lines to get sailing that season, with a beefier tapered boom and the drum-and-thimble reefing system planned for the following winter — the converted sail's own shape expected to reef and stow neatly, and Matt Layden's "well thought out" furling design offered as a case in point of what "a few thousand miles of boating will bring to mind."[26][27]

## A tested alternative that failed: cleating the tack strop

Running the **downhaul ("tack strop") to a cleat**, the same way the reef line is cleated, was proposed as a way to reef without a topping lift at all: slacken the tack strop and begin rolling in the reef line, then after a foot or two has rolled in, slacken the halyard and lower the sail, repeating as needed — letting the sail's own luff and leech act as its topping lift. Tested against the rig, **it does not work**: releasing the tack strop leaves the furling-drum line with nothing to work against — the whole point of the tube fitted over the furling axle — so no matter how hard the line is pulled, nothing happens. The topping lift remains the field-proven fix.[28][15]

## Matt Layden's own technique: foot pressure, no topping lift

Matt Layden manages without a topping lift at all: he puts **pressure on the furling line with his foot** while hoisting sail, and **pressure on the halyard with his foot** while furling — freeing both hands for the line actually being worked, rather than needing a hand to hold up the boom (observed firsthand at the 2003 Cedar Key Messabout; see [matt-layden/biography.md](/matt-layden/biography.md)). It makes furling "look easy" and is worth other builders trying first, since it avoids rigging an extra topping-lift line altogether.[29] This is a simple **body-weight technique** rather than any different hardware or line arrangement, and it is consistent with the observation (above) that Matt handles the stock gear noticeably faster than other experienced sailors.

A topping lift and the foot-pressure technique are not mutually exclusive. The same thimble-in-a-bridle arrangement, already familiar from rigging a boom tent over a gooseneck-less boom, invites trying the foot-pressure method first while doubting one would "go too long without" some form of topping lift, being hard to "wrap [the] mind around the transition from stowed on deck, to unfurling" without one. A related fit question about the [tack-pipe fairlead](/components/rig/index.md): whether a strop hardened while the rig sits stowed on deck would go slack enough, once hoisted and standing more vertically, to let the pipe contact the deck in a lull or wind shift.[30]

## Independent builders converge on the same fix

Three independent builders each found the stock furling gear impractical without a topping lift and fitted one as a fix, making it the most consistently reported build-and-refit lesson for the rig — including the build of "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)."

A first hands-on rigging trial confirmed the pattern independently: a temporary furling arrangement made by winding extra rope onto the reefing spool technically worked but was not practical — lowering the sail required **3 in steps** at a time and gave blisters. The verdict, echoing the other builders' experience: **"a topping lift it is."** The topping lift then rigged had **similar geometry** to the system above but simpler: a single line over a masthead block, splitting to each end of the boom. Once the lengths were right, "it seemed to work ok." The plans' suggested **6 mm** furling-line diameter was judged "a tad thin for easy handling."[31a] See [Little Jim — the sail collected](/boats/little-jim.md) for the boat-level record.

Before that trial, a theory had been floated (untested) that if the radius where the furling line comes off the drum is significantly larger than the radius of the sail rolled on the boom, the boom would naturally want to roll upward and support itself when the halyard and reefing line are pulled tight, without needing a topping lift at all — a theory the first trial above did not bear out in practice. See [Little Jim — a topping-lift exchange](/boats/little-jim.md) for the full exchange, including the recommendation to fit a topping lift before decking over the hull.

## Alastair's fuller walkthrough, and a continuous halyard/furling-line innovation

The fullest first-person walkthrough of exactly how a topping lift interacts with the halyard and furling line in practice:

- **Furling (reefing) sequence**: lock off the furling line, then haul on the main halyard — this lifts the boom above the cabin into a severely reefed (no-sail) position. Tighten the topping lift at that point; this stops the boom from falling when the furling line is next eased. Hauling on the halyard alone, without locking the furling line first, "just gives you sore hands" and leaves the boom needing to be helped into position by hand — a second, independent confirmation of the same three-hands problem described above.[32]
- **Unfurling (making sail) sequence**: once as much sail as wanted is unfurled, make the furling line fast and release the topping lift.[32]
- One documented arrangement (with photos on a builder's website, "Afloat" page): one line runs from the boom's end up to the topping-lift halyard and back down to the furling axle; the topping-lift halyard runs through an eye and back down the mast into the cabin. Getting the halyard's attachment point on the boom-line right, so the boom hangs level, takes some experimentation.[32]
- **A genuine innovation**: to reduce the amount of loose rope in the cabin, the **halyard and furling line can be made continuous** — a single line rather than two separate ones — so that as the halyard is hauled in, the furling line pays out in step, with only about a **3 ft loop** actually loose in the cabin at any time. The trade-off is that this makes it impossible to simply release the halyard and drop the sail over the side in an emergency — though dropping all that sail over the side is not obviously a good idea in an emergency anyway. "So far that seems to work too." This is the first record of any Paradox builder combining the halyard and furling line into one continuous loop — a refinement on top of the two-line topping-lift systems described above, distinct from but complementary to Matt Layden's own foot-pressure, no-topping-lift technique.[32]

Asked again in 2009 whether the halyard could be made continuous with the reefing line this way, the technique was confirmed still working well, with one added caution: any twists must be worked out of the rope when rigging it, or the line will not run cleanly.[33][34] A fuller explanation of the same idea, aimed at an outside sailor weighing the system for another small boat, set out the underlying trade-off: a furling-line spool can be made much smaller in diameter than an ordinary halyard, since the roller boom gives it a large mechanical advantage, at the cost of needing more line paid out; the halyard itself still needs to be low-stretch, since the roller boom is what actually tensions the sail's luff, not the halyard. On keeping the reefing line from tangling once it is eased off, the simplest fix is also the most effective: leave the slack line alone, either loose in a small bucket or simply on the boat's own bottom boards, rather than coiling or piling it — once a loose pile of rope is disturbed, it tangles.[35]

## A 2:1 halyard purchase: an untried idea, independently built on Scout

Working only from the plans, a prospective builder floated a further purchase for the halyard itself: a block fixed on top of the yard, with a second attachment point just below the masthead sheave, turning the stock **1:1** halyard into a **2:1** purchase — releasing twice the length of line for the same amount of fall, in exchange for an easier pull, and possibly making it easier to lower the yard and furl at the same time. The idea was floated as untried, with no reply on record.[128]

The same idea had, independently, already been built and proven: see [Scout — rig deviations](/boats/scout.md#rig-deviations-a-21-halyard-purchase-and-a-stainless-mainsheet-chainplate) for a working version — a second pulley fitted to the yard, with the halyard led back up to dead-end at the masthead block — giving the same 2:1 mechanical advantage, halving the effort to hoist the yard and allowing much tighter luff tension for windward work, at the cost of the same doubled line-handling.

## A tacking-direction asymmetry with the furl direction

Sailing alongside **Matt Layden** at Ft. Desoto just before the 2003 Everglades Challenge (see [boats/original-paradox.md](/boats/original-paradox.md)), photographs showed Matt's sail rolling **counter-clockwise as viewed from the cockpit** — the opposite furling direction from the "furl away from the mast" convention described above.[36]

Sailing reefed for the first time in a long while during the same outing, "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" proved a **full knot slower on starboard tack** (sail hanging away from the mast) than on port tack while short-tacking — the first record of a builder considering reversing the stock furling direction based on a measured speed difference between tacks, rather than for ease-of-handling reasons alone.[36] See [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md) for the same exchange's sailmaking side.

The stock plans give a concrete answer for which side that is meant to be: **Sheet 9 shows the sail, and the furling line, coming to the port (outside) side of the boom** — the same convention already noted above as leaving the furled sail stowed along the port side deck — confirmed as working without trouble on one boat, with an open question left unanswered on record over whether reversing it would introduce mast chafe from the sail's friction on the other side.[37]

## A further reversed-furl account: a vertical luff, and a possible starboard-tack advantage (January 2014)

A further, independent account of furling against the stock convention came from "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)," observed rolling her sail up in the opposite direction to the plans. The advantage: furled this way the luff stays vertical, which helps when tensioning it, with a possible further advantage sailing to windward on the starboard tack, since the tack then tends to stay closer to the mast. Scout's own reversed direction turned out to be unintentional rather than a deliberate choice — "I never gave it any thought or realized it was backwards" — arrived at by feel rather than by design, and left unchanged once it was noticed to be working.[131][132]

## A furl-direction/thimble alignment rule, and an alternate mainsheet-tang fitting (31 January – 1 February 2014)

The [Amistad tangle](/boats/ironsides.md#a-first-sail-at-lake-amistad-texas-and-a-topping-liftfurling-snarl-january-2014) above was traced to its actual cause: the furling swivel-rod's thimble must line up with the furling line as it comes off the *outside* of the drum, and the sail has to be hand-wrapped onto the boom, the first time, to match. On the stock plan the thimble sits to port, so the correct hand-wrap sends the furling line off the top of the drum down the port side; wrapped the other way round, the line kinks enough to make furling next to impossible.[133] A second, independent binding cause on the same boat was an overtightened boom-end bolt: free to rotate by hand on the bench, the stainless tang cants and binds once the topping lift loads it under sail — cured by leaving about 1/4-3/8 in of slack in the bolt, backed by a sloped stainless washer.[133]

"[Scout](/boats/scout.md)"'s own reversed furl (above) has a matching physical explanation: her thimble sits to starboard, opposite the plan's port side, so unfurling naturally runs the opposite way.[134] Her mainsheet-attachment tang is fastened with a stainless hanger bolt — wood-screw threaded on one end, machine-threaded on the other — set back behind the boom's trailing edge on a couple of washers and held with an adjustable nylock nut backed by a locknut, tightened just enough to take up the slop without binding; the tang itself is bent below the boom to more closely match the mainsheet's own running angle.[134]

## A halyard-knot failure and furling-line jam, recovered underway

The furling system's first documented **in-use mishap** came from a halyard-knot failure, not the topping-lift/three-hands problem otherwise documented throughout this concept (see ["Building encouragement"](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md) for the fuller trip report). Hoisting sail one morning, a **halyard knot let go** just as the sail reached the masthead, dropping the whole rig into the water — recovery required re-anchoring, dropping the mast, re-reeving the halyard, re-stepping the mast, and getting under way again. Once sailing, the sail proved not fully hoisted despite a tight halyard: the **furling line had jumped off the reel and jammed around the axle**, locking the furling gear so the sail could be neither raised nor lowered further. Rather than stop to fix it immediately, the fix waited: sailing on with the sail as set, beating up a tidal creek on the current until there was room to deal with the problem properly, then clearing it by dropping sail, anchoring, rewinding the furling line by hand, and getting back under way.

Two lessons follow: **check the halyard knot before hoisting**, and **keep some tension on the furling line while hoisting sail** — a slack, free-running furling line risks jumping off the drum and fouling if the sail catches wind and unfurls too quickly as it goes up.[38]

## What the topping lift does, rope choice, and use

Stated plainly, the topping lift supports the boom so it can rotate freely as sail is shortened or increased; without one, the sail tends to foul the side deck or the coachroof as the boom swings. It can also take the weight of the furled sail secured over to one side, keeping the hatchway clear — useful when mooring to port, since the furled sail is normally stowed along the port side deck where it would otherwise be in the way.[39] A **splayed topping lift** alone, without the fuller running system described elsewhere in this concept, can support the furled sail this way when stowed — done occasionally on one boat, with a reference photograph on file showing the arrangement.[40]

A Polypropylene line is a poor choice for the topping lift's hauling part: it kept slipping out of its jam cleat. Replacing just the hauling part with Terylene rope, while keeping the fixed part (tied off at the apex) in the original Polypropylene, solved the problem.[41]

In use, before hoisting sail the furled sail should be "topped up" on the topping lift first; while hoisting, care is needed that the yard does not catch under the topping-lift line.[42]

A full walkthrough of a mature two-line topping lift in daily use treats reefing and fully lowering the rig as two distinct operations rather than one: to reef, take up the topping lift's slack, furl in as much sail as wanted, then slacken the topping lift off again; to lower the rig completely, take up the slack, furl the sail all the way in, make the furling line fast, and only then release the topping lift so the boom can come down to the side deck on the halyard. Because the topping lift only ever carries a static load — nothing runs freely through it under way — no pulley blocks are needed anywhere in the system; a simple eye at the masthead is sufficient.[43]

## A UK builder's topping-lift question

A *Messing About In Boats* sea-trials article (see [boats/zoe.md — press coverage](/boats/zoe.md#press-coverage-two-2003-magazine-articles)) showing Matt Layden getting along without a topping lift prompted the question of what other builders' experience was with or without one.[44] From a hands-on trial: sailing without a topping lift is possible (tried on land), but a topping lift makes the operation "much simpler, with a lot less heaving" — a third independent builder restating the same three-hands difficulty and topping-lift fix already established above, with a topping lift simpler than the two-line system described earlier.[45]

## A second, later topping-lift question, and two more confirmations

Nearly two years after the first topping-lift question, the same question came round again — undecided whether to fit one at all, and inclined toward Matt Layden's own without-a-topping-lift practice in the interest of keeping the design's inherent simplicity (no standing rigging, no foresail, a yuloh rather than an engine for backup propulsion).[46]

Two more builders answered from direct experience, both confirming the topping lift is worth fitting rather than skipped:

- One **could not get the stock gear to work at all** — "I just didn't have the touch" — and has had zero problems since adding a topping lift.[47]
- The other restated the mechanism plainly: without a topping lift, slackening the halyard drops the boom straight onto the cabin roof, where it bangs about while the furling line is hauled in; thicker line might hold enough tension to keep the boom up unaided, but even with a topping lift fitted the furling line still needs a hard pull.[48] A sail's **heavy cloth (about 6 oz)** likely makes the problem worse than it would be with lighter cloth.[49]

This makes at least **five independent builders** who each tried the stock gear and independently reached the same conclusion: fit a topping lift.

## The question recurs once more in 2009

The same "is it really necessary" question came round again in mid-2009, from a builder who had noticed Matt Layden's own design goes without one.[50] The answer given was the same as ever: not necessary — Matt himself does without it — but not something its answerer would want to be without.[51]

A follow-up asked for an actual design to build from.[52] The answer: a simple two-line arrangement, one line from each end of the boom joining together and passing through an eye at the masthead, holding the boom in the position it sits at when the sail is hoisted — care is needed only to keep the yard on the correct side of it when starting to hoist. Photographs of the arrangement are held in the group's "Little Jim" Photos-section folder, alongside the "Topping Lift" files-section folder already referenced above.[53] The design was duly adopted, marking the rope at the position that lifts the boom correctly so the setting could be reused reliably.[54] The same builder's next question left the topping lift behind for an unrelated, persistent problem — his own outboard motor tangling with the mainsheet — see [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling — a 2009 recurrence across four boats, and two new fix ideas](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-2009-recurrence-across-four-boats-and-two-new-fix-ideas) for the full exchange this drew.[54]

## A third topping-lift question: reading the deck-tube layout at the mast

Having settled on fitting a topping lift, a detailed question returned about the exact rigging geometry, working from a reading of the plans' deck-tube layout: three tubes through the deck close to and forward of the mast on the port side (read, in order outward, as the tack strop, the topping lift, and the furling line), and one tube aft of the mast to starboard for the main halyard. The question also asked whether one builder's own topping lift still runs a three-block purchase to starboard, and whether its downhaul has since moved from a mast-mounted fairlead and jam cleat above deck to a below-deck block reached through a deck tube and cleated at the front of the vent box, matching the main halyard's own arrangement; and how much independent control such a rig gives over the boom's two ends — whether the aft end can be hoisted without disturbing the forward end's position, and whether the boom can be raised or lowered horizontally. A further comparison concerned which side of the boat carries the topping lift's single masthead block: one runs to **port**, split from the furling rod on one part to the mainsheet tang on the other; a **starboard**-side block, by contrast, keeps the topping lift clear of the yard (avoiding sail wear) but forces the two-part span to cross the boat when the sail is stowed on the port deck.[55]

The masthead-block question was answered directly for the port-side arrangement: an eye (not a block) is screwed to the aft side of the mast, carrying the two-part span to port of the mast but starboard of the sail. The ropes have never been felt to get in the way when stowed; the only real drawback is care needed not to let the yard end up on the wrong side of the ropes when starting to haul — not disastrous, but it forces the sail to be reefed slightly, since the yard then cannot be hauled all the way to the masthead. The aft span also lets the halyard and topping lift be tied off away from the mast, to stop them slapping against it at night.[56]

The fuller answer on the three-block arrangement: yes, a topping lift still runs to starboard with a three-block purchase, and yes, the downhaul now leads to a block in the vent box via a deck tube, cleated at the vent box's front exactly like the main halyard. Of the three port-side deck tubes near the mast, the **innermost** is in fact the topping lift, not the furling line as guessed — the outermost is the tack strop, and the furling line runs between the two. The idea behind the multi-block purchase is that hauling in on the topping lift brings the boom up and centers it into the correct position on its own, letting one line do the work of two; in practice it works less well than hoped, but well enough that it has not been changed. The lines have never fouled, since the aft one sits well back — as in the port-side arrangement, the halyard is snugged up once the sail is stowed to port and the lines tied off to it so they don't drum against the mast. Sailing off the hook and hoisting on a starboard tack is the norm; if the yard does catch on the lift it simply lowers slightly and flips clear — the one point where it becomes a real problem is, as with anything, when everything most needs to go right, and the fix is the same as ever: take the time to think it through, and with some luck the result will look deliberate.[57] Both explanations were duly thanked, closing out the thread.[58][59]

## A Norway owner's reefing-friction and yard-taper questions

A new Norway-based owner of a previously-built Paradox — [Robert Biegler](/people/robert-biegler.md), sailing "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)" out of Trondheimsfjord after buying her from her original UK builder — met the stock furling gear's friction firsthand: raising and reefing the sail for the first time did not work at all, requiring the sail to be rolled up by hand. Adding **two blocks** — one redirecting the reefing line aft where it leaves the drum, one above the deck hole where the line passes below — reduced friction enough to make reefing possible, though it remained more of a bother than reefing a junk rig. Left open: whether a **larger-diameter reefing drum** on the boom would help further, whether the fixed (rather than adjustable, under-way) topping lift he was using was itself a factor, and whether the boom needs raising to reef better.[60]

A related yard-taper question concerned the **higher-peaked Mk2-style sail**: Matt Layden is on record that this sail plan performs better, at the cost of a small triangle of sail left exposed when fully rolled — and a thickened aft end to the boom (so the boom's own end rises as the sail rolls up) was floated as a way to eliminate that leftover triangle, alongside a boom fattened toward its middle to accommodate the extra cloth a broadseamed sail's camber adds when rolled.[60]

Two independent replies answered the friction and technique questions. Hauling the sail up is not usually the hard part, since the reefing line runs freely; the working routine is to hoist the boom on the topping lift first, then slacken the topping lift once the sail is set so it does not interfere with the sail's shape. **Matt Layden's own technique** — not yet mastered by the reply's author — holds tension on the halyard and the reefing line simultaneously while easing the halyard out as the reefing line is pulled in, keeping the boom hovering in mid-air throughout the roll; gloves help grip the line for a better pull. Reefing is easiest on the **starboard tack**, when all the lines run free, and hoisting is easier with the wind from the starboard side. If a reefing drum was built to the spar plan's own dimensions, enlarging it should not be necessary.[61]

The remaining friction is a **known, general issue** with the stock gear rather than specific to this boat — see [the stock gear's inherent difficulty: a three-hands problem](#the-stock-gears-inherent-difficulty-a-three-hands-problem) above, and the independent topping-lift fix every builder who tries the stock gear eventually converges on. A separate reply from an owner still finishing his own boat asked in return whether more **yuloh blade area** gives more thrust, without a recorded answer.[62]

## Resolving the drum-and-spacer friction, years later

The reefing-friction question raised on the Norway-owned boat above (see [a Norway owner's reefing-friction and yard-taper questions](#a-norway-owners-reefing-friction-and-yard-taper-questions)) was left only partly answered at the time, with the underlying cause still unclear. A fuller mechanical description of the drum-and-axle assembly: the furling drum sits at the boom's forward end; the boom itself rotates around a curved bronze axle; a brass spacer pipe sits between that axle and the deck, with a strop exiting the spacer's lower end through a small deck orifice to a cleat inside the vent box, so that hauling the halyard tight lets the strop and spacer hold the boom's forward end in place while it still swings side to side for sheeting.[63]

On the same boat, years later, the friction traced to two separate faults rather than one: the boom could not lift clear enough of the coachroof to rotate freely, and the reefing line ran out of alignment with the drum, bunching and coming off it. Lengthening the brass spacer, by fitting it inside a longer piece of yellow MDPE piping, solved the first; cutting small pieces of copper pipe to centre the lower axle-rod end on the bolt between the spacer's fork supports — so the bolt no longer took an uneven load — plus greasing the axle, solved the second.[63] Neither of the fixes floated at the time (a larger-diameter drum, or raising the boom) turned out to be what the boat actually needed. A warped boom found on the same refit was judged serviceable rather than a fault in itself, since a straighter boom simply rotates more easily when reefing.[64] Once every control line — including the topping lift — was rerouted through ball-bearing blocks into the cabin, the whole system could be adjusted "in a jiffy" with very little effort: the clearest confirmation on record that the stock gear's persistent difficulty lies in blocks, alignment, and friction rather than in the basic mechanism.[65]

## A proposed larger-diameter furling spool

A further, independently floated idea for reducing furling-line tension: build up the furling spool's own diameter beyond that of the boom itself, reasoning a fatter spool needs less line tension to roll up the sail — a wooden doughnut, split in half and glued or screwed on, reinforced with fiberglass for strength, was proposed as the way to do it, modeled on however Matt Layden achieves the same effect on his own boat. Consistent with the general principle already established above that a full reefing drum, wound with the sail fully raised, gives maximum leverage (see [a Norway owner's reefing-friction and yard-taper questions](#a-norway-owners-reefing-friction-and-yard-taper-questions)).[66]

In practice, simply winding many turns of the reefing line directly onto the drum to build up its working diameter works well enough on its own. A wooden doughnut fixed to the boom does the same job while adding a further benefit: the tack-lashing holes can be drilled straight through the doughnut instead of through the boom itself.[67]

## A proposed circular furling-drum profile, and a further secondhand account of Matt's technique

A further recurrence of the "how does Matt do it without struggling" question, over two years after the [second, later topping-lift question](#a-second-later-topping-lift-question-and-two-more-confirmations) above, drew a detailed answer from an owner sailing with an external halyard and topping lift.[125][126] Tensioning the topping lift before easing the halyard keeps the strop vertical and the furling line pulling fair to its drum; letting the halyard go first instead lets the boom move forward, leans the strop out of line, and makes the furling line noticeably harder to haul.[126] The same owner proposed gluing crescent-shaped wood pieces to each side of the furling drum's square center shaft, rounding its profile to a slightly larger circle — reasoning a round profile would rotate more smoothly, in effect acting as a lower gear — untried and unconfirmed as of this account, distinct from the larger-diameter doughnut idea above in aiming at smoother rotation rather than reduced line tension.[126] Separately, Matt's own foot-pressure technique (see [above](#matt-laydens-own-technique-foot-pressure-no-topping-lift)) was independently, if more vaguely, recalled by a further correspondent as using both hands and a foot to control the reefing.[127]

## Stowing a taller sail once furled: a storm-trysail-like partial reef

Once the taller Mk2-style sail is fully furled, the yard simply falls on top of the boom with some loose cloth underneath, snugged down with a couple of nylon or Velcro straps, or a loop of bungee cord; a second support tube mounted forward of the existing one is a further option for holding it down. The retaining hoops that do this job double as handholds for anyone who ends up in the water alongside the boat. The last few feet of unfurled sail, left up with the yard raised rather than rolled all the way in, can serve much like a storm trysail in genuinely heavy weather.[68]

## A bearing fix for the furling axle's forward thrust

Because the boom constantly pushes forward against its own furling axle in normal use, the bearing there needs to resist that steady thrust rather than simple rotation alone. One durable fix: **dome the forward end of the axle bar**, then braze a cap onto the end of a length of water pipe and bond that pipe into the boom, cap end first. The axle then rides as a loose, rattling fit inside the pipe, bearing only against the domed cap; with a smear of grease, the joint has run trouble-free for years, showing "no signs of doing anything except operate perfectly." A sealed bearing at this point was considered and rejected — with a plain, greaseable bearing already giving no trouble, a sealed unit was judged a step backward, harder to fix if it ever did fail, for no real gain over one that already works.[69]

The same constant forward-thrust load explains why this fitting's own retaining screw rarely needs attention: gravity, not the screw, is what actually holds the axle in place against the boom's own forward push, so the screw can safely be left loose on a day sail or weekend outing with no ill effect.[69]

## Boom-furling across the Layden family (microcruising.com)

The stock system across Matt Layden's boats is a **simple lug sail, manually rolled/furled around the boom** — used since [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) (1985), whose "interesting details" list includes a "roller furling main."[70] On Paradox, Matt kept this same approach but **added a drum so the sail could be reefed from inside** the cabin, rather than needing to go on deck.[31] Later boats in the lineage carry the same feature: Enigma and Sand Flea are both described as having a "handy roller furling boom."[71] The whole reefing operation — releasing the halyard and pulling in the reef line(s) — takes about **20 seconds**.[72] See [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md) for the broader rig context.

## How the boom-furling drum works (Dave's Wiki)

A [third-source wiki](/references/daves-wiki.md) gives the clearest mechanical description of the system, corroborating the above: "These boats furl the sail around the boom. **The boom actually rotates on its axis. There is a drum at the forward end of the boom, around which the sail furling line winds. When the sail is raised with the halyard, the furling line is payed out, allowing the boom to rotate and release the sail. The process is reversed to reef the sail.** All control lines lead inside the cabin." The rig is a ~100 sq ft low-aspect-ratio lug on an **unstayed mast that can be removed to pass under bridges**.[73]

## The Mk1/Mk2 tack-angle difference, and why the sail can't be fenced onto the drum

Builders have asked whether a **stationary housing or fence around the furling drum** — as seen on some commercial roller-furling gear — would stop the sail from wandering as it rolls up, or keep the furling line from slipping off the drum.[74] Neither problem is solved that way in practice.[75]

Where the sail sits on the boom as it furls is governed by the **taper of the boom** and the **tack angle** of the sail as cut, not by any fence around the drum. The original ("Mk1") sail has a tack angle of nearly 90 degrees, so it furls pretty much on top of itself; Matt Layden's taller, later ("Mk2") sail plan (see [Mk2 sail](/construction/sailmaking.md)) has a tack angle greater than 90 degrees, so it wants to **move forward along the boom, toward the drum,** as it rolls up — on the Mk2 sail specifically, the sail needs to sit roughly **7.5 in away from the furler drum** (an earlier, widely-repeated figure of 2.5 in was a typo) or it will try to climb over it while reefing. Trying to fence or otherwise constrain the sail against this tendency does not work; it simply jams.[75][76] The fix is the same one already established for the reefing-triangle problem — **tapering the boom itself** (or building up its outboard end) so the sail has room to sit where it wants to go; see [the reefing triangle, and why reshaping the boom's own taper is the wrong fix](/components/rig/index.md#the-reefing-triangle-and-why-reshaping-the-boom-taper-is-the-wrong-fix) for the boom-taper technique in full, and [why the mainsheet presented at the helmsman's back is not awkward in practice](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-mainsheet-presented-at-the-helmsmans-back-is-not-awkward-in-practice) for related rig detail.

Separately, the furling **line itself has never been reported coming off the drum** across three seasons' use of the stock gear, since the furling-line system (unlike a simple headsail roller-furler) is never run slack — it is under tension at both ends of its travel. The one part of a typical rig that **does** foul up regularly is the topping lift, not the furling drum;[75] see [the running topping lift and internal reroute](#the-running-topping-lift-and-internal-reroute) above. A carbon-tape-wrapped rigid-foam core has also been proposed as an alternative to building the boom in solid wood.[77]

### Worked boom dimensions for the Mk1 taper

A worked Mk1 boom gives a concrete example of the taper described above: **50 x 38 mm** (with 8 mm radius corners) at the clew end, tapering to **35 x 38 mm** at the tack end — a difference in circumference of about **30 mm**.[78] An independently-guessed rule of thumb for a first attempt at this taper — a **3/8 in difference in diameter** between the clew and tack ends (about 30 mm of circumference on a round boom section, matching the worked Mk1 figure closely) — is a reasonable starting point without the exact plans dimension to hand; since the rig is small, the fit can be trial-and-error, refined afterward with a spokeshave and plane.[79]

Furling itself, once the boom is correctly tapered, is reported to be **as easy as it looks** in the WaterTribe video of Matt Layden's own roller-blind style furling demonstration — the main practical hazard being rope blisters until a sailor's hands harden to the furling line, not any difficulty with the mechanism itself. The Mk2 sail's forward creep as it furls (see above) is repeatable once the boom is set up correctly, so a boom tapered to keep the furled sail clear of the drum on the first reef stays clear on every subsequent one.[78]

The 2.5 in figure above was later corrected to **7.5 in** — a typo in the original report, not a measurement error — meaning the Mk2 sail's forward creep while furling is three times greater than first recorded, and a boom tapered for the Mk2 sail should allow for the larger figure.[80][81] Replacing a Mk1 boom confirmed the same worked dimensions given above (50 x 38 mm at the clew, 35 x 38 mm at the tack) while rebuilding in mahogany.[80]

Asked directly, once the corrected 7.5 in figure was on record, whether the boom's taper itself should now be made more pronounced to match: a bigger taper was not convincingly a help, since the increased tack angle on the Mk2 sail already pushed the tack-securing point further back along the boom regardless of taper — reasoning that could not be fully worked out mathematically, but the rebuilt boom's real-world furling performance would be the judge.[82]

The stationary-housing/fence question above was answered to satisfaction — a lighter drum arrangement means fewer parts to build in the first place, since neither problem it was meant to solve (sail position on the boom, or the line coming off the drum) is actually caused by the drum itself.[83]

### A second builder's own forward-gather fix, and Matt Layden's technique reconfirmed

Experience of both sails — the Mk1 (since passed on to another UK builder) and, currently, the Mk2 on "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" — yielded a cure for the sail gathering forward while furling: attach the **tack close to the drum**, and keep the **clew end of the boom raised slightly**. A topping lift also helps considerably.[84] On how Matt Layden makes furling and unfurling look so easy without a topping lift at all, his own explanation runs directly: Matt holds tension on the **halyard with his foot while lowering**, and on the **furling line with his foot while raising** — freeing both hands for the line actually being worked (see [Matt Layden's own technique: foot pressure, no topping lift](#matt-laydens-own-technique-foot-pressure-no-topping-lift) for the fuller record of this same method). Varying the topping lift's height at the clew had already been tried without success, but was worth trying again once a newly rebuilt boom was in service — rig details generally take longer to get right than expected.[85] From a non-Paradox roller-furling boom ("Seaward Fox"), the same foot-pressure technique works too, comparing the stock Paradox gear to the roller-furling boom on the F-27 trimaran.[86]

## A topping-lift fix for the sail's forward creep onto the drum

Beyond the boom-taper fix and the two untried alternatives already given (see [the Mk1/Mk2 tack-angle difference, and why the sail can't be fenced onto the drum](#the-mk1mk2-tack-angle-difference-and-why-the-sail-cant-be-fenced-onto-the-drum)), the topping lift itself offers a simpler practical cure for the taller sail's tendency to climb forward onto the furling drum: raise the boom's aft end on the topping lift until the furled sail rolls evenly, then mark the topping-lift line at that position as the correct setting going forward. Photographs and a drawing of a working topping-lift arrangement are held in the group's files-section "Topping Lift" folder referenced throughout this page.[87]

## A second use for the topping lift: readiness, yuloh clearance, and a rain awning

A furled sail kept topped up serves purposes beyond readiness for furling or hoisting. Topped up while under power or lying at anchor, the boom sits clear of head height, leaving the boat ready to make sail again instantly and giving enough clearance to work the yuloh without the furled sail in the way; a topped-up boom can also support a tent or awning, worth having at anchor in a rain storm.[88] See [Faith — a topped-up boom's other uses](/boats/faith.md#a-topped-up-booms-other-uses-readiness-yuloh-clearance-and-a-rain-awning) for a full firsthand account of these secondary uses.[89] A simpler alternative for sun or rain protection alone needs no topping lift at all: an umbrella clipped directly onto the hatch does the same job.[90] See [Little Jim — an umbrella instead of a topping-lift awning](/boats/little-jim.md#an-umbrella-instead-of-a-topping-lift-awning) for the fuller account.

## Anticipating an upcoming reef: leaving the topping lift set

The topping lift is normally slackened again once the sail is set to the size wanted, but it can be left tight instead when a reef is expected soon. One documented case: sailing up a winding channel with the wind broadly astern, knowing a stop would eventually be needed, sail was reduced progressively corner by corner while the topping lift stayed set throughout — the sail still pulling right down to a small enough size to handle comfortably.[91]

## Why the forward reefing fitting needs so many parts: tension when hoisted, compression when reefed

A newcomer working from freshly received plans, meanwhile adapting Paradox's roller-reefing scheme to a different boat as a practice project before starting a Paradox build proper, questioned why the boom's forward reefing attachment seemed more complex than its apparent job required — reading its purpose as letting the boom spin, bringing the attachment point back near the mast clear of the roller, and letting the whole assembly pivot around the mast — and proposed a simpler J-shaped bar or tube instead, also asking whether Enigma's own roller-reefing mechanism differs from Paradox's.[92][93]

Answered first in plain functional terms: the fitting has to reef and unreef the sail via the roller drum and reefing line, give the reefing line a fair, unobstructed lead from the deck down into the cabin, and be strong enough to do both at once — judged, once built, "pretty nifty" at doing all three.[94] A blunter caution followed separately: leave the stock roller boom exactly as designed, since its full "nuance" only becomes clear through use.[95]

The mechanical reasoning behind that caution came from a builder who had once tried the very same simplification: a test hoist showed the modified fitting would not work, so it was rebuilt to the stock design and has worked perfectly ever since. With full sail hoisted, the tack fixing is in tension and the fitting's lower strut carries no load at all; once the sail is reefed, the tack fixing goes into compression instead, and as the boom swings around the mast during reefing it is subject to real bending loads the strut exists specifically to resist. On Paradox the same fitting also doubles as part of the sail-stowage system. The standing advice: don't fiddle with something that already works well.[96]

A follow-up thanks left one observation unanswered on record — that a different builder's finished hull appears to substitute a plywood part for the curved rod/tube at the same forward fitting — while its other two open questions, whether a similar-looking system seen at a 2008 small-boat coastal race belonged to the stock Paradox design and whether Matt Layden's later Enigma design uses the same mechanism, were both taken up again in the days that followed.[97]

Watching video of Enigma's own furling system in use settled the second of those two questions by observation alone: Matt kept the mechanism essentially unchanged moving from Paradox to Enigma.[98] A second builder, working independently from a plainer fitting he had once built himself by his own means and methods, confirmed the same tried-and-reversed lesson already established above: the effort cost a couple of wasted weeks that building to the stock design would not have, and if redone would be built exactly to Layden's own plan — "gained even more respect for his work, if that is possible." A published how-to article on the fitting is available for reference, and the comparison drawn was with Matt Layden's own much smaller "[Sand Flea](/matt-layden/sand-flea.md)" dinghy, whose furling gear is far simpler but would only suit that boat's own small size and deck arrangement — underscoring that the forward fitting's complexity tracks the size of boat it serves, rather than any excess on the designer's part.[99] The video, once located, was shared onward to the list together with a further set of Paradox, Enigma, and Little Cruiser sailing videos.[100][101]

## The Y-fitting's purpose confirmed, and the plans' own May 1996 revision

Asked directly what the forward fitting's "Y" split itself accomplishes — beyond the tension/compression case already made above — one answer read it as letting the boom keep moving fore-and-aft under sail while restricting side-to-side ("wrist") movement at the joint, helping the boom stand up on its tube and reach fully to the boat's side once lowered, though judged "not absolutely necessary."[102] That reading was corrected directly from firsthand experience: the split **is** necessary, since with the plain, unsplit original the joint collapses sideways once the strut goes into compression while reefed, dropping the boom onto the cabin top or, if running, the deck, and making it awkward to tack or gybe (and "can give you a headache").[103]

The same "Y" fitting is recalled here as a **May 1996** revision to Matt Layden's own drawing, replacing an original design in which the bottom tube's rope tied directly to the next piece of structure — illustrated with the same `reefingdrum.jpg` photograph of the superseded original already posted to the list a month earlier, in October 2009, under the fitting's other, better-documented revision date: "obviously this simple method didn't cut the mustard."[104][105] That earlier posting places the revision in **May 1994**, from an actual dated Sheet-9 notation rather than from memory — see [tack strop — a May 1994 plans revision](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#a-may-1994-plans-revision-explains-the-forked-vs-plain-spacer-tube) for the fuller account. Reusing the identical photograph to illustrate a "1996" date here is recorded as a discrepancy rather than silently corrected, but the two are very likely the same revision, with 1994 the better-attested year. The same message gave a hedged, partial answer to the other open question left above: a photograph seen separately of a Texas 200 coastal-race boat's own reefing system "looked very similar" to Paradox's, though without a copy in hand to compare directly, so the two systems' exact relationship remains short of fully confirmed.[104]

Persuaded, the newcomer who had originally proposed simplifying the fitting concluded the revised, "Y"-fitted design was "pretty much the kind of simplification I was thinking of" in the first place, and confirmed he would build to the superior, later drawing rather than his own — closing out the thread.[106]

## A follow-up: what removing the tack strop entirely would demonstrate

A follow-up question asked whether sailing with the halyard slack, and the boom resting on the deck or cabin top, makes tacking or gybing awkward — confirmed as so.[107] The fuller mechanical answer, offered as a thought experiment rather than a real proposal: remove the tack strop entirely and rotate the boom, and the sail winds onto the boom while the reefing line pays off the drum, so the boom rises up the mast as high as desired; let go, and gravity reverses the whole process, the sail unrolling, the reefing line rolling back onto the drum, and the boom descending until it lands on the deck. The current design's strut prevents exactly this from happening — a further illustration of the general lesson already drawn above: understand why a design feature exists before trying to improve it, since with a designer as deliberate as Matt Layden, an apparently complicated detail usually has a good reason behind it.[108] Pressed on what the thought experiment was meant to show, given that removing the tack strop would in effect release the whole rig assembly from the deck,[109] the answer confirmed it was illustrative only, not a real proposal — and that **both** Matt Layden's original fitting and an independently-attempted simplification of it share the identical failure mode, collapsing sideways once the strut goes into compression while reefed.[110]

## A further reply: a built variant documented in photographs

A further reply to the same thread pointed to photographs of a working example, posted to the group's Photos section under "A Version of the Roller Reefing," to help visualize the fitting — built a little differently from the stock design, and working well so far.[111]

## Drum purchase depends on how much line is already wound on, and a downhaul-vs-cleated-halyard trap

Furling can jam in the last few feet even after several successful furls if the drum starts out without enough line already wound onto it: a near-empty drum's smaller working radius gives far less purchase, so the reefing line runs out before the sail is fully in, and pulling harder against an empty drum only tears at the hands rather than moving the sail — the same principle already established above for a proposed larger-diameter spool. Filling the drum with line restores the purchase a fatter apparent radius provides, though furling still grows harder the further the sail is lowered. A second, unrelated jam produces an identical symptom: hauling on the downhaul without noticing the halyard has already cleated itself stops the sail furling no matter how hard the downhaul is pulled, since the halyard's own tension is what has to give for the sail to come down.[113] Blisters from both mistakes are common enough in early-season use that carrying a pair of sailing gloves, worn until the hands harden to the line, is a standing fix.[114]

A further practical fix works ahead of the problem rather than after it: loading the furling drum completely with two full layers of line at the time of rigging, before any jam has a chance to occur — since an unfurled sail's first hard reef tightens considerably more than expected, pulling more line off the drum than a lightly-wound drum has to give. Keeping the drum's working diameter as large as possible this way also gives more purchase exactly when it is most needed.[130]

## A newcomer's boom-securing question, and video documentation of the gooseneck (December 2013)

Having looked at photographs of Paradoxes across the web without finding one that showed how the boom is actually secured at the mast end, a newcomer doubted a deck fitting alone could hold its forward end against the wind.[118] The boom is secured to a flexible gooseneck, held down to the deck by a lanyard that cleats off inside the vent trunk; the furling line itself is under considerable tension in normal use, and between the two the gooseneck is not going anywhere in a hurry short of that lanyard parting company — worth checking for chafe now and again. A close-up video shows the joint working as a true universal joint, and two further videos document the same gooseneck roller-furling system in action.[119][120]

Whether the line running up from the forward end of the roller fitting functions as a topping lift, and whether it is normally kept under some tension to relieve strain on the sail, remained an open question on record.[121]

## The topping-lift question answered: zero tension, and Matt Layden's own private advice against fitting one (December 2013)

Answered directly: there is no rigid deck fitting and no screws bearing the feared load — the gooseneck itself needing a photograph to show properly. The line in question is indeed a topping lift, meant to run under **zero tension** and play no part in setting the sail: the sail is hoisted, then the furling line itself serves as a downhaul to tighten the luff as taut as possible, a tight luff being important to a lug sail's efficiency. A topping lift is not part of the original design; many builders have added one to ease handling the boom during furling, but it comes at a cost, and Matt Layden fits none on his own Paradox (see [Matt Layden's own technique: foot pressure, no topping lift](#matt-laydens-own-technique-foot-pressure-no-topping-lift) above) — in private correspondence, he specifically discouraged fitting one at all.[122]

Asked whether that advice reflected preference or a technical reason, a further builder reported having tried sailing without a topping lift and judged himself simply not strong enough to manage the boom that way — another confirmation alongside the independent builders already on record reaching the same fit-one conclusion (see [independent builders converge on the same fix](#independent-builders-converge-on-the-same-fix) above).[123] Given the boom's long moment arm, the newcomer whose original question opened this thread concluded a topping lift was worth fitting after all, closing the loop on his own tension question.[124]
