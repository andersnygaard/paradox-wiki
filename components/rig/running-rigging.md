# Running rigging

The Paradox's running rigging is the sail-handling gear: the lines that hoist, sheet, reef, and support the lug sail, together with the blocks and fittings they run on. The boat carries strikingly little of it — the whole rig runs on **three lines**: "one to raise sail, one to reef and one to sheet."[1] A **topping lift** is the common fourth, not on the stock plans but fitted by most working boats, and a **downhaul** goes with it where the full system is built.

The lines that steer the boat and the inboard runs where all of this is cleated to hand are covered separately at [internal rigging](/components/rig/internal-rigging.md) — on a Paradox the two categories overlap, because every sail control is ultimately led inside the cabin.

## Rigging dimensions: the complete record

**No complete rigging schedule is published anywhere in the sources.** The plans as builders quote them give a rope size for exactly one item (the tack strop); Don Elliott's sixteen-part construction series gives none at all; and the one detailed builder's bill of materials in the archive buries all of it inside a single undifferentiated "deck hardware (blocks, cleats, plates, round rail, fittings…)" line at $550.[2] Every dimension that *is* on record is gathered below, with the gaps left visible rather than filled in.

| Boom haul (furling / reef line) | Not dimensioned on the spar drawing. The other end of that same **12 m** rope. The materials list gives **1/4 in** for the reef line specifically, matching the furling downhaul figure below | **Dacron** | [3][28] |
| Mainsheet | The materials list gives **5/16 in (8 mm)** for the "sheet"; the spar drawing's own sheet-diameter figure instead reads **6 mm (1/4 in)** — the two disagree. Attaches to the bottom hole of the boom-end strap | **Dacron** | [4][27][28] |

| *Anchor rode, for contrast* | *~30 m of **8 mm** three-strand* | ***Nylon**, explicitly not dacron* | *[9][10]* |

The anchor rode is included deliberately: it is the **only** place in the whole record where a rope *material* is argued from first principles, and the reasoning there — that a rode needs stretch and shock absorption, so three-strand nylon serves and low-stretch dacron does not — is the closest thing the sources offer to a stated material philosophy.[9]

The plans' own materials list fills in three of the gaps above at once: item 25 calls for **5/16 in (8 mm)** Dacron "for tiller, halyard and sheet," and **1/4 in** Dacron for the reef line — corroborating the furling downhaul figure already in the table. The spar drawing disagrees with the materials list on the sheet's own diameter, giving **6 mm (1/4 in)** on Sheet 1 instead of 5/16 in; raised as a discrepancy and tentatively put down to an older-revision CD copy of the plans differing from a printed set, but not resolved on record.[27][28] A separate reading of the plans quotes the tack line itself as **5/16 in (8 mm)** Dacron running from the spacer pipe, through the deck, to a cleat in the vent trunk — which does not square with the 6 mm figure already on record for it above, a further unresolved discrepancy.[29]

A 2023 question about upsizing the halyard, reef, and topping-lift lines by a couple of millimetres beyond the plans' own figure[30] drew a third, independent confirmation of the spar-drawing reading: the sheet and reef lines are **6 mm dacron**, "according to the plans" — matching Sheet 1's 6 mm sheet-diameter figure and the reef line's own materials-list figure (1/4 in, likewise 6 mm) already given above. The same reply's own hardware went the other way for durability: blocks sized for **8 mm line** fitted throughout the boat, with **Barton size 1 (#01150)** blocks specifically for the halyard and reef.[31]

## Rope materials: what is actually discussed

On the question of **Dyneema versus nylon** and the modern low-stretch cordage generally: **the archive does not contain that debate for the Paradox's running rigging.** Dyneema appears three times in the sources and not once as a halyard, sheet, or reef line —

- as the pivot line of a **rudder blade**, spliced to a "fool's purchase" tensioner, and that on **[Hogfish](/people/chris-morejohn.md)**, a 32 ft sharpie, not a Paradox;[11]
- as **webbing** (not rope) from Sailrite, used for the corner rings of a home-sewn sail on [Miramis](/boats/miramis.md);[12]
- and on a non-Paradox boat entirely, tensioning a wire forestay.

That absence is itself worth recording. The rig carries so few lines, under such modest loads, and is reefed by rolling rather than by hauling a leech down, that low-stretch high-modulus cordage has never been argued for on this boat the way it would be on a bermudan rig with a tensioned luff.

What the record *does* establish about material is narrower and practical:

- **Polypropylene fails as a hauling part.** Used for a topping lift's hauling line it kept slipping out of its jam cleat. The fix was to replace **only the hauling part** — with 9 metres of **Terylene** — and leave the fixed part, tied off at the apex where it never renders, in the original polypropylene.[5] The transferable principle is that the distinction that matters on this boat is whether a given part of a line actually runs through a cleat or block, not what the line is nominally called.
- **Nylon, not dacron, for anything that must absorb shock** — stated for the anchor rode, where stretch is the point.[9]
- **Grip beats specification on the steering line**, which is held in the hand rather than cleated: knots worked into the line, or moulded handles seized either side, are the answer the design itself gives.[7]

A first-time builder unable to source dacron locally, despite it being specified throughout the plans, asked in 2015 whether nylon or polypropylene could substitute, so long as the working load was acceptable — the only time a running-rigging line's *material*, as distinct from the anchor rode's, was questioned directly.[21] The one answer on record cuts against a like-for-like substitution: nylon stretches considerably more than dacron,[22] the opposite of what a halyard or sheet wants and exactly what a rode wants. The same builder, still unable to find dacron rope locally, reported that plenty of polyester rope is available but sellers rarely label it "dacron" in their specifications.[23] The reply on record disagreed that the two are interchangeable — polyester is not dacron — and recommended asking instead for Dyneema, Spectra, or another modern non-stretch synthetic by name.[24] That correction did not last a full day: it was retracted as a mistake, with an apology — dacron is simply a trade name for polyester, so the distinction drawn a day earlier was wrong.[25] A follow-up search turned up plenty of dacron rope for sale after all, on both US and UK eBay.[26] The exchange therefore closes against its own initial advice: ordinary polyester rope, of the kind already reported as available locally, does serve as a like-for-like dacron substitute, and dacron rope itself can also be bought directly online if wanted — no case for a low-stretch synthetic (Dyneema, Spectra) as the answer to this particular sourcing problem survives the correction.

## One rope doing two jobs

Because there is so little running rigging and all of it ends up inside, the **loose tail** becomes the real stowage problem rather than the line itself. One long-standing solution makes the **halyard and the boom haul out of opposite ends of a single 12-metre rope**, leaving only about **4 ft of loose rope in the cabin** altogether.[3] A different answer to the same problem fits small hanging line bags — rock-climbers' chalk bags, with a rigid lip and hanging loops, one per sheet — together with **different line colours**, adopted after 3/16 in line proved to be "everywhere" underfoot while sailing.[8]

## Where each line runs

- **Halyard** — up the mast to a masthead sheave and down through a **deck tube** into the cabin.[13] The hole through the deck sits **aft of the mast** rather than in line with it, twisting the halyard a quarter-turn between sheave and deck; see [rig — why the masthead halyard hole sits aft of the mast](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-masthead-halyard-hole-sits-aft-of-the-mast-twisting-the-halyard-a-quarter-turn). The yard carries an **eye strap on its underside** to take the rope the halyard attaches to.[13] A **2:1 purchase** has been built on at least one boat — see [furling — a 2:1 halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-21-halyard-purchase-an-untried-idea-independently-built-on-scout).
- **Boom haul (furling line)** — to the furling drum on the rolling boom, reefing the sail from inside in about 20 seconds. See [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md).
- **Mainsheet** — to the bottom hole of the boom-end strap and aft to the helmsman, arriving at his back rather than in front; see [rig — why the mainsheet presented at the helmsman's back is not awkward in practice](/components/rig/index.md#why-the-mainsheet-presented-at-the-helmsmans-back-is-not-awkward-in-practice) and [mainsheet, outboard, and rudder fouling](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md).
- **Topping lift** — from the top of the boom-end strap to a masthead block, down to a loop tied about 3 ft above deck, back up through a second bullet block about 5–6 ft above deck on the mast's forward face, then down to the furling axle where it ties to a brazed-on brass sail grommet.[4] Because it only ever carries a static load, **no blocks are strictly needed** in it — a simple eye at the masthead suffices.[14]
- **Downhaul** — tied to the loop in the topping lift, so one pull raises the boom at both ends simultaneously.[4]
- **Tack strop** — see [tack strop](/components/rig/tack-strop.md).

## A crowded masthead, and rigging-friction lessons (2015)

A newcomer working out where to mount a topping-lift eye or pulley on an already-crowded masthead — having bought a double pulley for the yard and a further pulley for the mast, and planning to mount the halyard pulley just above the sheave on the port side — drew practical masthead-layout advice from an established owner: avoid cutting a slot in the mast's upper part for the halyard sheave, since even an unbroken one "looks like a weakness" that could have been avoided; instead glue a small wood chock about 1½ in below the mast top on the starboard side and suspend a block from a short rope strop simply resting around the mast top, the chock stopping it sliding down, with a similar, lighter-duty arrangement — sometimes no more than a small fairlead — for the topping lift on the opposite side; whatever the arrangement, the halyard sheave needs to sit as near the top of the mast as it possibly can, or there will not be enough hoist to get the sail fully up.[15][16]

A newly launched boat's own masthead, rigged with a temporary doubled purchase while its builder's shoulder healed, gave two further lessons applicable beyond that one rig: **bigger pulleys and sheaves cut the effort needed to raise the sail** — small ones drag on the rope and add real effort to hoisting — and **every run needs to be kept free and straight**, since a rope rubbing or twisting even slightly along a spar or bulkhead can make a rig miserable to use, corrected simply by re-rigging it to run straight. Full detail on that particular rig lives at [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#post-launch-rigging-refinements-august-2015).[17]

A separate rig on a different, non-Paradox boat supplied a further transferable lesson in the same exchange: fitting **shackles and wire hoops to separate the pulleys** keeps them from binding, since binding there only ever occurs with the sail fully hoisted — staggering the masthead blocks so they angle apart, rather than stacking in line, does the same job, and lacing a sail too tightly can bind the blocks solid enough to jam it fast aloft regardless — the fuller story, including how that was discovered the hard way, is at [people/nickolas-bell.md](/people/nickolas-bell.md#a-jammed-halyard-and-a-close-call-under-a-different-rig).[18][19][20]
