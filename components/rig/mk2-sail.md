# Mk2 sail

The revised Mk2 sail plan for the Paradox, including changes to its tack geometry.

## Measuring a sail against the plan

A sail bought or inherited second-hand does not necessarily match the plan it is assumed to be cut to. One example, checked directly against the original sail plan, came out with its luff 250 mm short, its foot 190 mm short, and its head 140 mm short — "a much smaller sail than the Mark 1" — yet its dimensions did not match the Mk2 plan either.[1] The only reliable way to identify which generation of sail is actually in hand is to measure it against the plan, not to go by its reputation or its seller's description.

## Tack geometry and the furling drum

The Mk2 sail's tack angle is greater than 90 degrees — wider than the [Mk1](/components/rig/sail.md)'s near-90-degree tack, which furls almost on top of itself — so the sail creeps forward along the boom, toward the furling drum, as it rolls up. See [furling system — the Mk1/Mk2 tack-angle difference, and why the sail can't be fenced onto the drum](/components/rig/furling.md#the-mk1mk2-tack-angle-difference-and-why-the-sail-cant-be-fenced-onto-the-drum) for the boom-taper fix this requires.

Run through Sailrite's own online sail calculator, the two plans' tack angles came out as **91.3 degrees** for the original sail against **95.65 degrees** for the newer one, with the calculator crediting the newer sail with only about **95.8 sq ft** of area — the calculator's own tack-angle output carried a caveat flagging it as approximate, and no reference to the climbing risk turned up in the archive's own posting history despite the wider angle.[2] Two further fixes for the same climbing tendency, beyond the boom taper above, were floated without being tried in practice: nudging the boom slightly further forward so the furled sail can overlap the drum a little as it rolls up, or lengthening the swivel rod's own tail so it reaches a spool of any diameter.[3] A simpler practical fix followed almost immediately: raise the boom's aft end on the topping lift until the furled sail rolls evenly, then mark the topping-lift line at that position — see [Furling system — a topping-lift fix for the sail's forward creep onto the drum](/components/rig/furling.md#a-topping-lift-fix-for-the-sails-forward-creep-onto-the-drum) for the full technique.[4]

A second, independent check of the same geometry in 2010 — measured directly against the plans rather than run through Sailrite's calculator — arrived at matching figures: **95.6 degrees** for the newer sail against **91.3 degrees** for the original, worked from the full sail-dimensions recipe Matt Layden posted in 2003 (tack-to-peak **4,150 mm**, throat-to-clew **4,322 mm**; see [construction/sailmaking.md](/construction/sailmaking.md#a-grommetyard-lead-angle-mismatch-and-two-independent-fixes) for the fuller recipe). Swapping those two diagonals — tack-to-peak at 4,322 mm, throat-to-clew at 4,150 mm — would instead cut a sail with a **90.66-degree** tack angle, close to the original's own; the swap was weighed on the reasoning that the resulting further-aft centre of effort would reduce the risk of lee helm, given the Mk2 foot's own reduction from the original — put at **200 mm** in this account, an approximate rather than exact match for the **170 mm** already recorded below between sheet 1's 3,350 mm figure and the Mk2's 3,180 mm foot (see [boom length](#boom-length-a-plans-inconsistency-and-why-not-to-shorten-it-before-launch)) — but the swap was never built.[12]

Asked directly before the order was placed, Matt Layden confirmed the wider angle is not an unintended by-product of the newer sail but its deliberate purpose: it keeps the sail snugged up against the furling drum, since without it the sail works its way aft along the boom over successive rolls, spoiling balance and set. Matt reached this answer by unrolling and re-measuring his own sail, not having anticipated that the tack-to-peak/throat-to-clew diagonals could be read either way. This settles what the 2008 discussion above could only observe without explaining: the sail's tendency to sit forward, toward the drum, is the wider angle doing its intended job, not a side effect the boom taper merely accommodates. The same exchange reconfirmed a topping lift as neither necessary nor desirable on Matt's own boat, and established that the sail's four corner grommets belong in the sleeve itself rather than above or below it, to match cleanly against the spars.[13]

## Locating the sail's forward top corner, and tuning balance through the sleeve gap

The sail's forward top corner sits about **800 mm** from the mast centerline, measured along the top edge — or about **900 mm** to the halyard attachment point along that same edge — a dimension not given directly on the plan and best confirmed by measuring a print of the drawing rather than assumed.[5]

A long gap left in the sleeve at the sail's head, where the sail meets the yard, makes this dimension easy to adjust after the fact: moving the halyard's attachment point along the yard, through the gap, retunes the boat's balance without altering the sail itself.[6] In practice a gap of roughly **12 in**, once the sail is tensioned along the yard, has no significant effect on performance — its only job is to keep sail material from being trapped between the yard and the mast — and a wear patch on the lower sleeve, where the boom presses the sail against the mast, is worth adding at the same time. The gap needs no particular shape (no half-round cut); the sleeve can simply stop and resume further along the yard.[7][8]

The boom's own taper, needed to keep a fuller sail's forward creep clear of the furling drum as it rolls up, is a related but separate fitting question — see [Furling system — the Mk1/Mk2 tack-angle difference](/components/rig/furling.md#the-mk1mk2-tack-angle-difference-and-why-the-sail-cant-be-fenced-onto-the-drum). The boom-to-mast reach at the tack, by contrast, is fixed by the tack strop's own pipe length and the curve bent into its swivel rod — see [Tack strop](/components/rig/tack-strop.md) for that fitting's geometry in full.[7]

## A further recurrence: the throat-to-halyard distance re-estimated, and a Mk2 sleeve finished without a cut-out

A further inquiry, in November 2012, sought the same throat-to-halyard dimension recorded above by an independent route, reading Matt Layden's own file-section sail diagram directly rather than a scaled print: the diagram carries no stated measurement, but a rough reading puts the halyard fixing point about a third of the way up the yard from the throat — further aft than on the Mk1 sail — or, by a separate rough estimate from the same diagram, about a metre from the throat; either figure is explicitly flagged as approximate, with several inches of further sail-to-sail tuning expected depending on the yard's own weight and flex.[16][17]

A 2015 exchange finally supplied the exact measurement asked for in 2012. Asked where along the yard the halyard should attach, with members reportedly citing a range of 700-900 mm, two Mk2-sail owners independently measured their own boats and got the same figure: **900 mm (3 ft), exactly.**[26][27][28]

Rather than build in the sleeve gap recorded above, one Mk2-sail owner chose to leave it out altogether, being unsure exactly where the halyard hole should fall, and had the sailmaker add a 600 mm sacrificial patch to the sleeve instead to absorb chafe there, reasoning a small hole drilled through the patch — once its position could be confirmed by measuring an already-built Mk2 sail — would be neater and simpler than a cut-out, and that the patch's extra thickness already put chafe at that spot beyond concern; on the Mk1 sail he had sailed previously, chop on a port tack had rubbed the sail against the mast, fore and aft, over more than the length of the sleeve slot, without ever doing real harm.[18][19][21]

The same exchange restated the general standard for how a Paradox lug sail should set, independent of generation: no **girt** — no diagonal crease — should show between the throat and the clew, while a slight crease running the other way, between the foot and the tack, is normal and preferred, and can be eased out by adjusting tension on the tack.[20]

## A peaked head, and what follows from it

The sail plan current in 2014 — the latest revision of the design's sail at that date — was **peaked**, its head not parallel to its foot, a shape shared with some Polynesian sails rather than with a Bermudan rig. Where a tall Bermudan mast exists to lengthen the luff, this rig keeps a deliberately short one.[9] Sails of this shape do not need the luff hauled down tight to perform on the wind, behaving more like a junk or lateen rig; see [Clew — trim: sheeting the clew](/components/rig/clew.md) for the sheeting rule of thumb that follows. (The later [Mk3](/components/rig/mk3-sail.md) sail was not drawn until 2023, so a 2014 reference to "the latest sail" cannot be to it.)

## Boom length: a plans inconsistency, and why not to shorten it before launch

The plans are not internally consistent about the boom's own length. Sheet 1, showing the sail together with the original Mk1 sail plan, draws a boom (including the furling spool) of roughly **3,450 mm** against a sail foot of **3,350 mm** — about 100 mm longer than the foot. Sheet 12, the spar-details sheet, instead gives the boom as **3,650 mm** — a further 200 mm longer than the figure implied by sheet 1.[10] A builder fitting the Mk2 sail, whose foot measures **3,180 mm**, only needs a boom of about **3,300 mm** to suit it — some 350 mm shorter than a boom built to sheet 12's dimension.[10]

The recommended course even so is not to cut the excess off pre-emptively. Once the boat is launched, the sail's position is slid fore-and-aft along the boom to set the rig's balance (see [Rig — wrapping a Mark II sail on the boom: tack position, balance, and taper](/components/rig/index.md#wrapping-a-mark-ii-sail-on-the-boom-tack-position-balance-and-taper)), and only once that trim is settled does a builder know how much boom can actually be spared. One Mk1-sail boat ended up with about **160 mm** of boom unused aft of the sail once balance was set; removing it was considered, but the possible effect on the boom's support points when the rig is stowed inside the hull was reason enough to leave it as built.[11]

## Which UK boats carry which generation

By September 2011, [JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md) carried the newer Mk2 sail, while [Faith](/boats/faith.md) and [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) both still carried the original [Mk1](/components/rig/sail.md).[14]

## Windward performance and reefing point: firsthand comparisons

Whether the Mk2's wider tack angle also confers any windward-performance advantage, beyond solving the drum-climbing problem it was designed to fix, remains without a direct answer: a January 2012 inquiry asking directly whether owners who have sailed both generations found the newer sail faster to windward drew no comparative answer on record.[15] Two 2015 firsthand comparisons speak to how the two sails otherwise differ in use. One owner's original sail had never been very good — the sailmaker had not accounted for the yard's bend under load — and was sailed regardless for twelve years; switching to the Mk2 was "a huge improvement."[24] A second, professionally cut Mk1 was judged by its own owner to have carried full sail comfortably well into a Force 5, where a Mk2 fitted the same year showed a noticeably greater heeling moment and wanted reefing by the bottom of a Force 4 — a difference significant enough that, before a fair Force 3 comparison against sister boats could be made, that owner's early verdict was that a well-cut Mk1 might be the better buy if choosing again.[22] Sail-area choice between the two generations also tracks local sailing conditions rather than any single right answer: a US Midwest builder, expecting mostly light August winds, chose the larger-area Mk2 deliberately to nurse headway out of light breezes, contrasting his own reasoning with an English owner's Mk1 preference in the stronger, steadier winds of the UK coast.[23] A third owner went straight to the Mk2 without trying the original, reasoning that Matt Layden's own revision had already absorbed the lessons of the first design.[25] A firsthand Mk1-only account offers a hypothesis for why the two generations might differ to windward without settling it: a hull only begins acting as a foil once heeled significantly and moving at speed, so a taller sail's own reported pointing advantage may come from its higher centre of effort simply heeling the boat more at a given windspeed, rather than from reduced turbulence off a higher peak — full theory and citation at [boats/mini.md — the rig: from standing lug to sprit](/boats/mini.md#the-rig-from-standing-lug-to-sprit).[43]

A further practical rule followed some weeks later, from the same owner whose early Force-3/Force-4 comparison is recorded above: the Mk2 sail needs noticeably more tension on the rig's lines generally than the Mk1 does, and the halyard's own fixing point on the yard needs to sit **further aft** than it would on a Mk1, or the hoisted boom sits too low and can strike the sailor's head — consistent with the [700-900 mm halyard attachment point already measured above](#a-further-recurrence-the-throat-to-halyard-distance-re-estimated-and-a-mk2-sleeve-finished-without-a-cut-out). Sorting a new Mk2 sail out this way took **7 outings and 25 nights afloat**.[29] The same July 2015 exchange, prompted by a first-time sailor's rigging trouble, is covered in full at [furling system — a newcomer's rigging troubles, and a further compound halyard purchase](/components/rig/furling.md#a-newcomers-rigging-troubles-and-a-further-compound-halyard-purchase-july-2015).

## A yard-taper/sail-cut mismatch, and a longer replacement yard

A sailmaker's inspection traced a persistent crease running from the peak down to the clew to a mismatch between spar and sail: the sail itself had been cut to fly on a straight, non-tapered spar, but the yard in use had been built to the plan's own taper, which begins roughly where the halyard rope attaches. Rather than unpick and re-sew the sail to fit the tapered yard, the fix chosen was to remake the yard as a flat, square, straight spar — slightly longer than the original, to suit the Mk2 sail — with the tie-off points along it free to be adjusted afterward to help furling and get tension right.[30] This recurs a length problem already on record for the Mk1-to-Mk2 conversion: switching to the newer, taller sail has more than once left a builder's existing yard too short, solved in one case by converting to a length of aluminum tube already on hand (see [rig and sail — yard material](/components/rig/index.md#yard-material-douglas-fir-remains-standard-aluminum-a-proven-alternative)).[31]

The same rebuild prompted a general point on **matching a spar's stiffness to how the sail was actually cut**: it is the yard's bend under load, not its static shape, that a sail must be cut to fit, since a stiffer or more flexible yard than the sailmaker assumed throws off the same peak-to-clew crease regardless of taper. Measured directly — a 10 kg weight hung from the yard's centre, supported at both ends, with deflection read at the centre and at points 500 mm and 1000 mm to either side — one yard came out at 6 mm, 16 mm, 21 mm (centre), 20 mm, and 11 mm across its five measured points, the kind of concrete figure a sailmaker needs rather than a guess. The standing advice to keep weight aloft to a minimum applies here too, regardless of which fix (yard rebuild or a stiffer material) is chosen.[32] Getting a sailmaker to cut a well-fitted sail in the first place is more reliably done by handing over the actual yard and boom to be measured against, rather than paper dimensions alone — "if your sailmaker doesn't know why you are waving a yard and boom about, find another sailmaker."[33]

Once the new, flat yard was ordered, the same short-yard problem recurred for the Mk1-to-Mk2 conversion once more: a first instinct was to consider an aluminium tube of 5 mm wall thickness as a replacement, but built experience says that guess runs too heavy — see [rig and sail — yard material](/components/rig/index.md#yard-material-douglas-fir-remains-standard-aluminum-a-proven-alternative) for the actual dimensions, alloy, and in-service behaviour of a proven aluminium yard.[34][35]

## A 2016 recurrence: furling still uneven after 25 outings, and a sailmaker's flatness check

A further owner reported the same underlying symptom some months after the sail-trim tuning recorded above: the sail still does not roll up evenly on the reefing line, spoiling its own shape and stressing the cloth unevenly where it creases, with a marked drop in performance to show for it — an open problem, unresolved on the record.[36] The same owner later added a further, distinct symptom of the same underlying problem: sailing a long port tack with the sail reefed, it chafes against the mast enough to wear a few tiny holes over time.[39] A fellow builder's untried suggestion for the creasing was to cut more hollow into the sail's luff and leech — since the crease likely comes from more draft sitting in the sail's centre than at its edges — and, for the chafe, to reinforce the tack, since a hollow luff would then leave only the tack itself rubbing however deep the reef, an area that could be reinforced without affecting the rest of the luff.[40] A further reply granted the camber was likely overdone — cut to the owner's own specification about three years earlier — and that a future replacement sail would carry less of it, at the cost of some other compromise; sailmaking was described as enough of a black art that chasing perfection risks never actually sailing, in contrast with junk-rig sailmakers said to spend years altering and remaking the same sail without settling on a final shape.[41] Prompted by a separate correspondent's own account of curing twist by converting to junk rig, the same owner reported adding four new tell-tales to the sail's leech that week for clearer real-time feedback on sail set and where the twist was actually occurring — see [tell-tale placement](/components/rig/index.md#tell-tale-placement) for the general technique this builds on.[42]

A related but distinct symptom had already been fixed once, elsewhere: creasing caused by a sail cut for a straight, non-tapered spar was cured by swapping a wooden, plan-tapered yard for a straight, non-tapered aluminium one (see [boats/thor.md — an aluminium yard proven at sea](/boats/thor.md#an-aluminium-yard-proven-at-sea-a-planned-aluminium-boom-and-a-jibing-lesson-december-2015)), with the same swap planned for the boom too — though matching the spars to the sail's actual cut fixes shape, not necessarily uneven furling on its own. The sailmaker's own flatness check came with the story: take the sail down off its spars, have three people pull gently until the pressure is even at each corner, then hold it at each corner and confirm it lies crease-free.[37]

A separate exchange the same week restated the underlying lesson — that a sailmaker must be told how much a yard actually bends under load rather than left to assume it stays straight — with a fresh data point: a first sail, cut flat without that information, developed bad creases under load, while a later sail, from a sailmaker who understood lug rigs, sets far more powerfully, though it now needs reefing by Force 5.[38]
