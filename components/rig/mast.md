# Mast

Where the [Paradox](/paradox.md) mast passes through the deck, the structure is locally reinforced. This beefed-up area is called the **partners**: it distributes the loads that the sail induces around the mast into the deck and hull.

On the Paradox this reinforcement is built together with the **vent trunk** (the boxed structure carrying the mast down and the ventilation through the deck). The topping timber a builder sees on the vent trunk in construction photos is the partners; the **deck ply lays over that timber**, and the mast (and vent) holes are then cut to match.[1]

## Build sequence: decking before or after the vent trunk

- **Plans method:** [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s plans call for waiting until **after the deck is on** before cutting the mast/vent holes.
- **Before-decking approach:** the vent trunk can instead be completed **before decking**, reasoning that there is little room to work with the deck off and even less with it on. To do this before decking, a **mock-up of the lower part of the mast** verifies that the control lines will clear the mast and **exit into the cabin at the correct angle** — confirming that the Paradox is sailed with its running rigging led into the enclosed cabin.[1]
- **A second before-decking confirmation:** the same before-decking sequence can be followed by building the mast step and vent trunk (with one side left removable) onto **bulkhead 2** before assembling the hull, so the bulkhead can be set up horizontally on trestles to fit the mast to its step and partners — useful for working indoors without a 20 ft-high roof. The deck's mast hole is then simply cut to match once the deck goes on. See [reading the mast drawing](#reading-the-mast-drawing-a-recurring-point-of-confusion) above for more.

A sketch of the vent detail in the [building articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) shows the detail: `http://www.tomah.com/delliott/paradox3a.jpg`.[2]

## Vent trunk top dimensions, and why exact measurements don't matter much

The plans leave several vent-trunk dimensions vague enough that a builder working strictly to spec can be left unsure of the design intent. The vent trunk's top measures **420 mm by 250 mm wide**, with a tolerance of about **±10 mm** since the dimension "isn't critical." A build may pencil in **72 mm** for the width of the tapered block at the trunk's narrow end (from the transverse section on plan sheet 9), but in practice the block can simply be built up from offcuts and ground to final shape freehand with an angle grinder and sanding disc — consistent with the broader view (see [hull assembly sequence](/construction/hull-assembly-sequence.md#transom-and-bulkhead-4-crown-and-fitting-bevels-at-trial-assembly)) that many Paradox joinery dimensions are better fitted by eye at assembly than pre-cut to a theoretically perfect figure. The vent trunk's side and rear panels can come entirely out of scrap plywood offcuts, needing no additional material.[3]

The vent trunk and mast are best fitted **before assembling the bulkheads to the hull sides**, since there is no longer enough clearance to maneuver the mast once the hull is assembled and the builder must work with everything horizontal instead — consistent with the before-decking build sequence described above, and easing the mast-step/partners fitting work described there as well.[3]

## Attaching the vent trunk top to bulkhead 2

The vent trunk's top simply **butts and glues** to bulkhead 2 at deck level, rather than needing a notch or dado cut into the bulkhead. The top of the box itself is fairly thick — about **1 in** — and can be built up from **several pieces of wood laminated together** to form the curve needed to line up with the top of bulkhead 2, which avoids any further shaping once the piece is made.[4] A separate small cleat, curved to the deck crown and glued on to carry the trunk top, is an alternative worth avoiding: it makes drilling the fairleads for the roller-reefing line and the tack downhaul noticeably harder to get at afterward.[4] The **port cleat** for the vent trunk can end up shifted outboard from the line originally drawn on the bulkhead without being a mistake — it reflects an intentional, if undocumented, adjustment rather than a drafting error; cleats built with roughly **250 mm between cleat sides at deck level**, bonded solid in epoxy, and less splayed than another builder's, are still within the design's tolerance.[5]

On the **starboard side** of the vent box, leaving that side unfixed and instead screwed in place with a sealant allows it to be removed later for access to the box's interior; a permanent strip of wood added to the top of the box on that side both provides a fixing point for the screws and strengthens the box wall where the mast sits only a fraction of an inch away — an alternative to relying on bulkhead 2's own deck for that strength.[6] Building the vent trunk this way, entirely from scrap plywood offcuts and other salvaged material — including offcuts left over from previous projects for the laminated frames, and a piece pinched from elsewhere to complete the rudder — can still finish within about 1–2 mm of the plans' stated dimensions.[6]

The **drain pipe** through the vent trunk uses **1 in PVC pipe** per the plans; epoxy does not bond well to PVC mixing sticks and squeegees, but it does bond to the pipe itself once the surface is prepared: rough the pipe up with **40-grit sandpaper**, then cover both the pipe and the drilled hole with epoxy and a little filler powder. Drilling the hole through the hull needs a very powerful electric drill with a bit extension, taken slowly, letting the drill cool down periodically rather than run continuously. The pipe is installed **after the bottom is fitted**, so it can pass through the bottom sheet and be faired off underneath.[6]

Building a **dummy short bottom section** to test-fit the mast-foot fitting before a mast exists is a workable way to make sense of the mast drawing at the same time as the vent trunk, for a builder working through both together.

## Vent trunk and mast step construction, confirmed again

A newcomer working through the plans for the first time raised the same vent-trunk/mast-step question this file already answers above: the base of the vent trunk, and how the mast sits into it, is confirmed once more to be built up from scrap offcuts and epoxy, leaving a square recess for the mast foot, then trimmed to final shape freehand with an **angle grinder and coarse sanding disc** — the same technique already on record. The recommended sequence is likewise confirmed: build the mast and fit the vent trunk to bulkhead 2 **before** assembling the hull, so the bulkhead can be set up horizontally on trestles for repeated trial fits of the mast step and partners — much easier than lifting the mast in and out of a part-finished hull.[7][8][9]

## Mast step height above the waterline, and the stock partners dimension

A newcomer wondering how the mast is even supported, never having seen photos of it, prompted the fullest restatement on record.[175] The mast step sits an inch or two above the waterline, with the partners (see above) at deck level. The stock distance from the base of the mast step to the top of the partners is **500 mm (20 in)** as built to plan — not the roughly three feet occasionally recalled from a modified boat, where a different mast bury and cabin height throw the figure off; going from memory of a modification rather than checking the plans directly is exactly how that wrong figure arose.[176][177][178] The mast carries no stays or shrouds (see [why the mast carries no stays or shrouds](#why-the-mast-carries-no-stays-or-shrouds), below), and the vent trunk around it carries rainwater from the deck down to a drain below the step, as already described [above](#attaching-the-vent-trunk-top-to-bulkhead-2).[176]

## No mast-lowering pin on Paradox, unlike Little Cruiser

Some of the [building articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) describe a pin that can be pulled to lower the mast, which construction photos of most built boats do not appear to show.[10] **The Paradox mast in fact carries no such pin.** Its foot simply sits on a block fastened to the bottom of the hull, held upright by the partners described above; to lower it, the mast is lifted roughly 600 mm straight up and off, then laid down on top of the rolled-up sail and boom.[11]

[Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), by contrast, does use a mast pin: her mast is supported on the foredeck by two hinged struts, and pulling a pin at the mast's foot lets it fold backward until it rests on the cabin top — a genuine quick-lowering rig suited to ducking under bridges, distinct from Paradox's lift-off arrangement.[12]

A river-based builder nearing launch, whose local waters offered good sailing obstructed by a series of low bridges, asked whether the mast could be made to hinge for repeated lowering and raising, rather than lifted fully off and stowed as described above.[94] The suggested answer looked outside Paradox's own stock rig, to a hinged tabernacle arrangement documented in a separate junk-rig group's files (a diagram named "Henpig3.jpg") that would need some custom metalwork of its own, distinct from — if comparable in spirit to — Little Cruiser's own hinged-strut tabernacle noted above; a copy of the same file was then added directly to the Paradox group's own files for easier reference.[95][96]

## A further 2015 idea: a keel-mounted, built-in tabernacle

A prospective builder weighing mast weight against a history of shoulder surgery proposed going beyond a deck-mounted tabernacle to a permanent, structural one keel-mounted rather than merely deck-mounted — citing B&B Yachts' "Princess Sharpie" series as a possible model, and fifteen years' own experience with a deck-mounted tabernacle on an Alacrity 19, raising and lowering which "was a pleasure to use." Judged the Paradox mast not especially heavy either way, in wood or aluminum; the idea was left as a thought rather than a worked design.[148] A separate correspondent, having previously built a free-standing mast of his own and checked Paradox's mast design against the same engineering calculations, judged it strong as designed; he considered a tabernacle himself but decided to stick with the stock arrangement, mostly for its neatness.[156]

## Reading the mast drawing: a recurring point of confusion

The plans' spar drawing, sheet 12, recurs as the single hardest sheet in the whole plan set to interpret at first reading — its mixed-scale convention (full size fore-and-aft and sideways, but scaled along the spar's length) and stray dimension-arrow marks have tripped up more than one first-time builder working the plans page by page.[13] See [building plans — reading the plans: a first-time builder's backlog of questions](/references/building-plans.md#reading-the-plans-a-first-time-builders-backlog-of-questions) for the fuller technical resolution.

The sheet actually carries a drafting error: a set of right-pointing arrow heads sits to the left of, and touching, the labelled centreline, and should simply be painted out with correcting fluid before the rest of the sheet is read.[14] Once removed, the drawing splits cleanly into three views. The centreline and everything to its right is a **half section viewed from the bow** — the dimensions there apply to both the port and starboard sides of the mast. Everything to the left of the centreline (excluding the stray arrows) is a **section viewed from the port side**, showing the mast's taper along its length; the mast's **rear face is straight**, with all the taper cut into the front and sides. At the bottom of this side view sits a **half-section plan view at deck level**.[14] Read this way, the rear (straight) section of the mast is built first, then one side is joined to it to establish the taper, then the front, with the masthead plugs and copper grounding strap fitted before the final side closes the box.[15][16] The drawing is genuinely to scale, but the **vertical and horizontal scales differ** — so the rear stave's thickness can still be measured straight off the drawing even though it looks disproportionate at first glance; the reading "clicks" once a builder recognizes the mixed-scale convention, though it remains confusing on a first pass even for a builder already familiar with similar mixed-scale drawings from other designs.[17]

The same three-view, mixed-scale confusion recurred independently on three further occasions, each resolved the same way. In 2002, a builder starting a scale balsa model could not see how the mast's four-sided profile fit together; the answer restated the plans' mixed-scale convention (large scale for cross-sections, compressed scale for length) and the mast's flat-back, tapered-front shape, recommending a model be drawn at one consistent scale rather than following the plans' compressed-length convention literally.[18][19][20][21] In 2003 the same "Section at Deck" half-section was again mistaken for a full section — complicated by the mast's sharply curved bottom and by working out how the 86 mm and 24 mm figures related to the centreline — and was answered with the same three-view reading: the half-section's left side is the front (rotated about the "86" line), the centre upright is the rear, and a third upright is the side cheek, with an assembly sequence (rear timber first, then a side, then the front once cured, then end plugs and wiring, then the final side, then rounding the corners) matching the account above.[22][23][24] That 2003 explanation was later credited by another builder as a "Rosetta Stone" for reading the drawing — the clearest walkthrough of it on record.[25]

A third builder hit the same wall in 2009, having owned the plans and Building Manual for over two years before the sheet suddenly "clicked" one weekend while working out what timber to order for the mast itself — a delay that had nothing to do with attentiveness, simply that the mast stage had not yet been reached. His own walkthrough works from the same paper-masking method described above: laid over the sheet, a mask covering everything at and to the right of the repeated "900" dimension line leaves the **port-side profile** exposed on the left — the mast's straight aft face and tapered, curved forward face — giving widths, top to foot at six stations, of **34, 54, 70, 80, 84, 84 mm**. Sliding the mask right to instead cover everything to the left of the same line exposes the **fore-and-aft half-section**, drawn at half thickness since the mast is symmetrical in that plane (per the sheet's own centreline marking); read this way, the two side pieces come out at width × thickness of **34×10, 54×14, 70×17, 80×19, 84×19, 84×19 mm** (with a slight taper at the mast foot), and the front and back pieces at **10×12, 22×16, 32×21, 36×23, 38×24, 38×24 mm** — remembering that, because the sheet again shows only half the front/back width, the plans themselves actually print **5, 11, 16, 18, 19, 19 mm** at those same stations.[26]

The masthead detail on the same sheet shows a **halyard sheave**, but leaves the question of headroom for a masthead light open: the plans' dimensions leave very little room past the sheave and its axle for wiring or a fixture routed fully inside the mast at the top, a constraint that shapes how builders route masthead-light wiring in practice — see [running lights — routing masthead-light wiring outside the mast, below the sheave](/construction/electrical-wiring.md#routing-masthead-light-wiring-outside-the-mast-below-the-sheave).[27]

The same confusion recurred once more in February 2015, again from a newcomer already at the mast-building stage, expecting four sections of two types glued up into a box and unable to square that expectation with the sheet.[149] The fix was restated in full: the same blog write-up pointed to in 2012,[150] the same drafting-error correction and three-view reading described above,[151] and, as a further pointer not previously on record, the pages of Don Elliott's building manual (pages 73–74) covering the same material.[152]

A more basic version of the same underlying question recurred again in July 2015: whether the mast's pieces should be glued together first and then shaped (tapered), or each piece shaped individually before gluing.[158] The confirmed order is to **shape first**: taper each piece to its finished profile before any gluing, leaving the side pieces' forward edge oversized to make the next step easier. The tapered front and rear pieces are then fitted to one side, checking that the mast is not bent — made easy since the mast's rear face is straight and gives a reliable reference. The top and bottom plugs are fitted next, with the mast's interior painted or sealed and any internal wiring run, before the second side is fastened on to close the box; the corners are then rounded off to finish — the same rear-first, side, front, plugs-and-wiring, final-side sequence already established above.[159]

A further recurrence surfaced in May 2016, when [William Johnston](/people/william-johnston.md) — already on record building a first mast that came out heavier than most (see [mast weights compared](#finished-mast-weights-compared)) — set out to rebuild it lighter and hit the same sheet-12/manual-page-74 confusion again: measuring his existing mast at 76×127 mm at the deck against an expected 86×70 mm, and unable to settle which board the drawing's 86 mm figure belongs to. Full detail at [people/william-johnston.md](/people/william-johnston.md#rebuilding-the-mast-for-weight-may-2016).[173]

The answer, once more, was the sheet's standard three-view reading, restated in the fullest terms yet for this recurrence. To the right of the centreline is a half-section viewed from the stern, showing the finished mast's starboard half: 19 mm for the side cheek plus a 20 mm hole, doubling to a total width of 78 mm — the hole being half the combined width of the front and rear infill pieces. To the left of the centreline is a section viewed from the port side: at deck level the total width is 86 mm, with 24 mm taken by each of the front and rear infill pieces and 38 mm left for the hole between them. "Section at Deck" is the further half-section already described above — a plan view of the starboard half at deck level, the port half its mirror image. Reading along each horizontal dimension line from the left: the first figure gives the finished mast's own fore-and-aft width, which also matches the port and starboard side cheeks' width; the second gives the front (and rear) infill piece's own thickness; the third gives half the combined width of the front and rear infill pieces; the fourth gives the side cheeks' own thickness. The stray, superfluous arrow heads pointing at the centreline from its left side are the same drafting error already noted above, and should simply be disregarded.[174]

A further recurrence surfaced in January 2018, from a new correspondent working the plans out of the usual order — building the mast and the boat's other removable parts (yuloh, rudder, boom, yard) before starting the hull itself, for lack of space yet to build the hull — who could not identify what each of the drawing's three sections represented, though guessing, in line with the reading already established above, that the mast's own plain rear face was simply the one left out of the drawing.[180] The reply pointed again to the same blog write-up pointed to in 2012 and 2015,[181] and, for the first time, back to William Johnston's own mast-dimensions exchange above as a further worked example.[181]

What actually resolved the confusion was a photograph rather than any text: the correspondent later confirmed it was the blog's own first photograph that made everything click into place, and that a simple caption identifying it as a side view of the plan would have avoided the confusion outright.[182] The same three-view reading given above for the 2016 recurrence was also repeated, essentially unchanged, in a separate reply sent directly to the original question — delayed in reaching the group by the external mail route it was sent through, so that it arrived, and was numbered, after the exchange above had already closed.[183] The same correspondent went on to redraw the sheet as a full-size vector drawing, intending eventually to vectorize the whole plan set; see [building plans — a vector/3D-file request](/references/building-plans.md#an-october-2017-recurrence-a-vector3d-file-request-unanswered).

The same thread continued into February 2018 with a prospective builder combining the drafting-error correction and the three-view reading directly onto an annotated copy of the drawing itself, hosted externally for reference since the group's own files section would not accept the upload.[184] Prompted for further comment, [Alastair Law](/people/alastair-law.md) offered a structural diagnosis rather than another reading of the existing sheet: the sheet's real difficulty is that Matt Layden tried to fit three separate views into too small a space on one drawing, and the fix is simply to redraw it as three separate drawings.[185] A second round of edited drawings followed within the week, checked in turn against a detail — a part the blog write-up pointed to since 2012 labels "BACK" — that reads differently from the plans.[186] A further, independent builder hit the same wall the same week while making his own mast, and, as with several of the recurrences above, resolved it by redrawing the sheet himself to work out the measurements needed.[187]

### An exploded drawing that made the plans' mast intelligible

The same wall was hit — and published — in 2006, by a builder who found the mast the **only part of the whole boat he could not interpret from the plan**, everything else in the build having gone easily.[163] What unstuck him was an **exploded drawing supplied privately by [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md)**, which, combined with advice from [Al Law](/people/alastair-law.md), made the sheet clear; once he understood it, he rated the mast build itself easy, and thought he could redraw the sheet more clearly himself. He also pointed readers to **two photographs of a mast under construction by [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/index.md)**, published on [Andrew Layden's website](/references/andrew-layden-website.md) and dated 2005-05-22, as giving a clearer idea of what the drawing is trying to show than the drawing itself.[163] Elliott's manual is the same source pointed to for this sheet in 2015 above; the exploded drawing is a separate item, and is not known to be published anywhere.

## Cutting the taper: a circular saw is the wrong tool

A further recurrence of the same confusion, eighteen years after a first pass at it in 2003 (above), turned in 2021 from reading the drawing to actually cutting it: working from a 2003-issue set of plans and Don Elliott's own building manual — the copy in hand ending before Don's own boat had even launched, leaving it unclear whether later sections existed — the same 2005-05-22 construction photographs pointed to above (of the mast Matt Layden is shown cutting for the boat later named Enigma) were recalled, and whether that circular saw alone was enough to cut the mast's taper was put to the list directly.[189] It is not: a circular saw is the wrong tool for the job, and the taper is instead worked down with a hand-held electric planer, reckoned no more than a night or two's work in the shed.[190] The same exchange reaffirmed Don Elliott's manual as the fullest published walkthrough of the whole build, from an owner whose own Don-Elliott-built mast has needed nothing beyond repainting in all the years since — see [boats/edge.md — sold to Deric, and restoration begins](/boats/edge.md#sold-to-deric-and-restoration-begins-2017).[191]

## Mast rake — where it comes from, and how it's built

The mast is drawn with a clear **rearward rake**, though the plans do not dimension this rake explicitly as a separate angle anywhere. One reading of the plans: the mast is **parallel to bulkhead #2**, with the offsets (top and bottom) from bulkhead #2 to the vertical **scarf line** implicitly defining the rake — a hedged best guess rather than a precise answer.

The fuller and more direct account of **how the rake is actually built in**, from firsthand experience building the mast:

- The mast's **back (aft) side is straight** — it does not rake.
- The **forward face slopes aft**, which is what produces the visible rearward rake.
- This shape comes not from cutting an angle into flat stock but from **curving the mast's side plates**: when the **face plate** is glued on last, it simply **follows the curve already built into the side plates**, producing the raked forward face as a natural consequence of the side-plate shape rather than a separately-dimensioned angle.

This complements the [mast interior sealing and anchor-light fit](/construction/mast-sealing-and-fit.md) concept (finishing/fit issues with the same hollow mast).[28][29][30][31]

## Why the mast, and the sail on it, are offset to port rather than centered

The mast is set off the boat's centerline to port, with the sail rigged to the mast's own port side — putting the sail's own position slightly further from the centerline rather than closer to it, the reverse of what carrying it to starboard would give.[135][136] The offset clears an unimpeded opening in the forward cabin bulkhead for access to the forward section of the boat, an opening a centered mast would not leave nearly as convenient, if it left one at all.[136] Rigging the sail specifically to the mast's port side, rather than swapping it to starboard, has been suggested — as an unconfirmed guess, not a settled answer — to let the whole rig pivot sideways and lie flat along the port side deck once dropped.[136] Asked directly why the boom is not instead carried to starboard, the sail's exact lateral position was judged to make very little difference to the underlying force vectors; the real value of the arrangement as drawn is being able to stow the sail quickly.[137][138]

The geometry behind that offset was suggested to be carefully calculated as a whole — mast position, boom, and the way the boom support swings down to port — so that moving any of it would call for redesigning the boom support along with the route the control lines take through the drain into the cabin.[139] The mechanism was then confirmed directly: the **vent box** itself is set off-center to port, and the mast is seated within it as far to starboard as the box allows; that positioning is what lets water entering through the mast partner area drain overboard without a drop reaching the cabin. Centering the mast on the boat's own centerline would mean redesigning the whole system, for a performance gain judged not worth the trouble — the arrangement as drawn works well and is "extremely well thought out." No meaningful difference in sailing characteristics between tacks has been noticed even with the sail carried against the mast on the burdened side; if either tack is favored at all it is port, where the sail sits against the mast, though the effect is negligible.[140] Matt Layden's own test sails, made while experimenting with longer chine runners, included the sail carried to the mast's starboard side, but he showed no tendency to make that change permanent.[141] A prospective builder in British Columbia weighing the same offset against the late Phil Bolger's own boats — several of which carry offset centerboards and equally unconventional mast and stern arrangements, and "function just fine" regardless of looks — judged it a non-issue; on his own build he eliminated the [vent box](/components/vent-box.md) altogether for the extra room it freed up.[142]

## Mast material choices

Most builders simply follow the plans' wooden box-mast construction, drawn on page 12 of the plans, but two alternatives are on record. **Irrigation tubing** makes a workable mast: 3 in tubing has given excellent results on an earlier, smaller 17 ft boat and was planned for reuse — cheap, light, and "ready to use" straight from an irrigation supplier, with no building required at all.[32][33][34][35][36][37][38] The more usual route is a scratch-built wooden box mast, for which builders weighing wood type, taper shape (hexagonal, octagonal, or round), and dimensions against a possible future stretched hull are pointed to [reading the mast drawing](#reading-the-mast-drawing-a-recurring-point-of-confusion) above.

## Reusing a secondhand dinghy mast

Beyond building a mast from scratch, one recorded case of **reusing a ready-made aluminum dinghy mast** establishes the fit question a builder must answer: a **Skipper 12 dinghy mast** — a constant 2-3/8 in diameter, 1/16 in wall thickness, about 6 in short of the length needed (fixable with a wooden plug extension) — was checked against Paradox's own mast requirements.[39][40][41]

The rule of thumb: an aluminum mast of roughly **equivalent diameter to the plans' wooden mast will do the same job**, provided the aluminum extrusion's minor-axis width matches the mast width called for in the plans. Any candidate secondhand mast should be checked along its full length for signs of prior weakness — a series of circumferential holes, or a "straightened" former bend — before reuse. The case for aluminum generally: it is **lighter, more reliable, needs no maintenance**, accepts hardware easily via pop-rivet, and simplifies mast wiring; if a wire is run for lights, tying it off into "Y" shapes with cable ties roughly every foot before inserting it stops the wire's annoying nighttime "pinging" against the inside of the mast.[42]

### A repurposed flagpole as a mast, on both of one builder's Paradoxes

A further real-world example of a ready-made metal mast rather than a scratch-built or salvaged-dinghy one: one builder's first Paradox stepped an ordinary aluminum flagpole as her mast, and his second boat, also built with a metal mast, may well have reused the same approach. Full account: [people/michael-beebe.md](/people/michael-beebe.md#a-2015-dockside-look-at-the-second-paradoxs-modifications).[164]

## Aluminum as a mast material: engineering fit, not just substitution

Beyond the secondhand-aluminum-mast question addressed above, the engineering discipline behind a like-for-like substitution matters: an aluminum mast salvaged from a lighter dinghy cannot simply be reused on a Paradox on the strength of matching diameters, since aluminum and wood of the same diameter do **not** carry comparable strength — a mismatch "patently absurd" to assume, doubly so on an unstayed rig with no yard parrel to share the load, where a mast built too light will go out of column and buckle under a deep reef. The correct method is either to run the numbers — using a yacht-design text's spar formulas to work out the aluminum diameter and wall thickness that actually equal the plans' wooden mast in strength — or to buy new material sized to match, rather than trust luck with a secondhand spar.[43]

A practical caution pulls the other way: building the plans' wooden mast has **proved easier than expected** for at least one builder, and a hull already finished around an unsatisfactory aluminum mast would be far harder to convert to a different rig than simply building the wooden mast correctly the first time — an argument for getting the substitution right before committing the hull, not after.[44]

A 2008 case worked the same substitution question through for a specific tube: a **63.5 mm (2½ in)** diameter aluminum tube with a **3 mm** wall thickness, proposed as a straight substitute for the plans' wooden mast. The verdict was conditional — such a tube could work, provided the wall is not too thin — with a specific reinforcement offered for the mast's weakest point, the lower end: split a length of the same tube lengthwise and force it inside the mast as an internal sleeve, then cut the sleeve's upper edge at a shallow angle, roughly a **6:1 slope**, so the reinforced section ends in a gradual rather than a sudden change in wall thickness and stiffness. No further reply settling whether the 3 mm wall itself proved thick enough is on record.[45][46][47]

The same substitution question resurfaced independently a few days later, the tube now restated as **63 mm** rather than 63.5 mm, and drew two further, more cautious opinions. One judged the tube better suited to a **boom** than a mast, recommending roughly **73 mm or 88 mm** diameter with a **5 mm** wall for the mast itself if built in aluminum at all — but would personally use wood regardless, reasoning that wood floats in the event of a real structural failure, and recommended weighing several independent opinions rather than any one answer alone. The other questioned the substitution's whole premise, seeing no advantage over wood beyond ease of construction and judging the tube undersized on its face; the suggested check was a simple deflection test — lay the tube across two sawhorses with a weight at its center and compare the sag against a wood box-section built up to the mast's actual dimensions — predicting wood would win the comparison clearly.[48][49][50]

The question was ultimately settled by cost rather than engineering: pricing the aluminum tube proved prohibitively expensive, so the substitution was abandoned in favor of a wooden mast after all.[51]

That was not the last word on the specific tube, though. A fresh 2013 aluminum-mast question — a builder with a piece of old mast tubing of similar dimensions to the plans' wooden mast, back rebuilding after a long break — drew a reply with no direct experience to offer beyond confirming the plans' own wooden mast is very stiff.[123][124] The same thread drew a real-world answer at last: the 63.5 mm, 3 mm-wall tube from the 2008 exchange above had, after all, eventually been fitted — much lighter than wood, and roomy enough to step all cables, lights, and the radio antenna inside it, with a shaped wood insert at the base under a hard rubber strip covering the tube's stamped end, and the main-halyard sheave carried on a riveted omega clamp. See [Cachivache — a 2013 return to the aluminum mast](/boats/cachivache.md#a-2013-return-to-the-aluminum-mast) for the fuller account.[125] The original questioner, weighing the same scavenged tube against his own build, decided to give an aluminum mast a try.[126]

A related open question: whether Matt Layden's own wooden mast was actually engineered to a calculated section, or was more a matter of what materials Matt had on hand at the time — unanswered on record. A separate ambiguity — a "how many spares will you have?" aside — was later clarified as being about carrying spare masts at all, not spare parts for one.[52][53]

A later, general sizing rule of thumb for replacing any of the plans' wooden spars — mast, boom, or yard — with aluminum tube: pick an outside diameter close to the original wooden spar's own, then choose a wall thickness giving the tube a similar mass to the wood it replaces, using a tube supplier's own catalog (which lists weight per foot) to find the match; working the underlying strength-of-tubes math out directly is possible, but has proved a "good headache" in practice for at least one builder who tried it.[103][104]

A reply to that sizing question, for a small reduced-scale sail of about 7 sqm, guessed a tube about the same as used on a **Sunfish** mast would probably work, at least for a while, and flagged the **partners** — where the mast passes through the deck — as the point of greatest stress and so the most vulnerable, needing no sharp edges there. The same reply made a case for sticking with wood rather than substituting aluminum at all: wood floats, and a **square-section** spar with only slightly radiussed edges, enough to let paint stay on, is actually the **strongest shape for a given weight** — worth weighing before committing to a round tube.[105] A same-day follow-up pointed to a Laser-class sailing forum thread on mast-tube sizing as further outside reading.[106]

## A non-Paradox aluminum boom, and the case for keeping the boom's own taper

A further reply in the same thread added a general caution: the shape of a spar and its yard's own flex matter a great deal to how a boat sails, a lesson learned firsthand rather than from theory.[107]

One aluminum-spar build, mounted to a reduced-area Paradox-style sail on a different, homebuilt boat rather than a Paradox itself, worked the substitution out in practice: a 60 mm outside-diameter, 3 mm wall-thickness aluminum tube for the boom, with a cast aluminum tang-end fitting (a 10 mm stainless threaded rod set into a lathe-turned aluminum round, machined for a snug fit inside the tube) and a matching cast fitting at the roller-drum end (a 12 mm stainless rod, made the same way), riding in two lathe-turned brass bushings; the boom already turned smoothly on its bushings before the sail itself was rigged. The yard was built from 40 mm plastic tubing with a 30 x 12 mm wood strip fitted the full length inside it, to stiffen it in one direction.[108]

Photographs of the build drew a caution familiar from the [furling system](/components/rig/furling.md#the-mk1mk2-tack-angle-difference-and-why-the-sail-cant-be-fenced-onto-the-drum)'s own established taper requirement: an untapered boom is a problem for furling, since the boom's taper is what keeps the sail's shape correct as it reduces and takes up the boom's own slack ("droop") once the sail is fully rolled up.[109] The untapered aluminum tube itself was judged plenty stiff — probably heavier than a wooden Paradox boom — and turned freely on its bushings, though the boat remained in the workshop with the furling system itself still untested; the plan, if the taper problem proved real in practice, was simply to tack away the last foot of sail cloth with bungee cord in a real blow rather than furl it fully.[110]

A further reply, examining the same photographs, flagged one further detail worth changing: the reefing-line spool's own diameter was very small, meaning a hard pull on the line — a spool built to the plans' own dimensions needs gloves to operate comfortably, and a rebuild would size the spool area larger in diameter, both at the rim and at the boom's own core, for a lighter, less-tensioned action.[111] The small diameter, in this build, was intentional rather than an oversight: it lets several yards of spare reefing line wrap around the shaft rather than needing to be stowed loose in an already-cramped cabin, and the drum's own side plates were built to a larger diameter than shown on the Paradox plans.[112]

The taper question was answered in full: a tapered boom keeps the sail's shape correct as it reduces, and takes up the boom's own slack once the sail is fully rolled; simulating that shape on an untapered tube, rather than rebuilding the boom from scratch, can be done by attaching tapered strips — wood or dense foam both suggested — along the boom's own length.[113]

## A first Paradox aluminium boom, proposed (December 2015)

Following an aluminium yard's proven success (see [rig — yard material](/components/rig/index.md#yard-material-douglas-fir-remains-standard-aluminum-a-proven-alternative)), a boom in the same 1.5 mm-thick, 50 mm high-tensile tube was proposed as the first aluminium boom fitted to a Paradox herself, rather than the reduced-area homebuilt rig above.[165] A standing caution against the idea: plenty of weight in the boom is what stops it lifting in a blow, a property the light aluminium tube used successfully for a yard might not supply.[166] See [boats/thor.md](/boats/thor.md#an-aluminium-yard-proven-at-sea-a-planned-aluminium-boom-and-a-jibing-lesson-december-2015) for the fuller account.

## A spliced, two-piece aluminum spar for stowage, proposed (2018)

An outside correspondent, replying to a maiden-cruise report, proposed cutting a spar — yard, mast, or boom — into two roughly 5-6 ft lengths and rejoining them with a sleeved splice, so each half stows more easily aboard a small boat: an inner sleeve of double the outer tube's own wall thickness, riveted fast to one half and left free to slide onto the other, with sail tension alone holding the two halves together under way, backed up by a length of paracord run inside the tube; the sleeve's ends should be "fishmouthed" — relieved into a shallow curve — to avoid a hard stress point where it meets the unsupported tube. Six-foot aluminum tube is the easiest length to source, buy, and ship. The idea drew on firsthand experience with US military aluminum radio masts using an identical staked, rather than riveted, sleeve joint with a 6 in protrusion; recommended 6061 alloy over 6063 for the tube, stainless steel rivets, and cautioned that an aluminum weld zone runs at only about 15% of the parent 6061 alloy's strength, favoring a staked or riveted joint over welding. Filling the finished tube with closed-cell foam, or pipe-insulation foam, was suggested as a way to make the whole rig float if lost overboard.[179]

This idea was not built or tested on a Paradox; it is recorded as a transferable spar-stowage technique, distinct from the continuous aluminum spars already built and sailed above.

## A castor-wheel deck joint, and a critique measured against Paradox's own tack strop

A further question about the same non-Paradox aluminum-rig build asked how the boom and its roller-reefing drum actually swing up into the sailing position at the deck end, once the sail is tensioned. The missing "axis" turned out to be a castor-wheel frame at the assembly's very base: the frame itself turns, giving the whole roller-reefing system full three-dimensional movement; hoisting the assembly needs one small further adjustment, so the bolt at the castor plate's side plates ends up parallel with the hull.[114][115]

Examined against photographs of the finished fitting, the design drew a critical firsthand assessment: no complete universal joint appeared to exist at the deck, leaving no freedom for the boom to move fore-and-aft — a motion it will try to make regardless; and the castor-wheel frame, built to carry compressive loads, was doubted strong enough for the tensile load this application puts on it instead — raising the question of why, having copied so much else from Paradox, the build had not simply kept [Paradox's own tack strop](/components/rig/tack-strop.md#a-fastening-caution-for-new-hardware-and-a-design-compliment), "the utter simplicity, reliability and repairability of a piece of string at this joint."[116]

The reply confirmed the missing-axis point and judged reworking the lower part back toward that original piece-of-string joint would not be difficult, while judging the castor joint itself, being steel, plenty strong for the tensile load regardless.[117] Pressed further on why an elegant, simple solution had been swapped for what read as a clunkier alternative, and on what load the fitting could expect in an inadvertent gybe — a manoeuvre that breaks the boom outright on some craft — the answer held that the boom itself, a 3 mm-wall, 60 mm outside-diameter aluminum tube, would fail first: if an inadvertent gybe broke it, the fitting would likely be torn off with it, and the original line-based joint would be lost the same way.[118][119]

## Box-girder mast construction technique, and the birdsmouth alternative

For a from-scratch wooden mast beyond the plans' own drawings, the general box-girder mast-building technique described in **Lin and Larry Pardey's "Seraffyn" books** applies: build up the inside of the mast at points where hardware like winches or fittings will attach, use **12:1 or longer scarphs** when scarphing mast timbers to length, and taper and round the mast's corners — rounding the leading edge is essential, while the aft edges may be left square or rounded to taste. A crumpled roll of aluminum foil run the length of the mast's hollow interior serves as a simple, effective **radar reflector** — "the biggest radar reflector around." **Bud McIntosh**'s book is another source of wooden-mast-building information beyond the Pardeys'.[54]

For a more advanced builder, the **birdsmouth** method — building a round, hollow mast from many long, narrow staves (like barrel staves), each with a matching notch ("rabbet") cut into its edge so the staves interlock into a circular section when glued up — is "the pinnacle of the mast maker's art," though it can be "maddening" for a first attempt.

## A proposed birdsmouth-building tutorial, and existing published guides

A birdsmouth spar is genuinely easy to build once tried: a short, five-foot practice sample is enough to see that "the mast literally builds itself when cut," with the real skill lying in **making the tapering jig** rather than in the cutting itself.[55]

Two published guides cover birdsmouth construction: an online walkthrough at `users2.ev1.net/~fshagan/bm.htm`, and a fuller article at `duckworksmagazine.com/r/articles/birdsmouth/mast.htm` — both judged **too thin for a first-time taperer**, lacking enough worked examples and step-by-step detail for a builder new to the technique.[56][57][58][59] A birdsmouth article also ran in **WoodenBoat magazine issue 149** (July/August 1999 issue, p. 31), "Bird's Mouth Hollow Spars" by Aime Ontario Fraser, covering the technique of Craig Wright and Richard Duke of Mountain Marine; a 6 ft test section built from the article's instructions proved very easy to follow "even for me, who has really poor woodworking skills." A further birdsmouth article is believed to have run in *Boatbuilder* magazine as well.[60]

## A birdmouth carbon-fiber composite spar, as an alternative to the plans' rig

Beyond reinforcing a spar locally with laminated carbon fiber tow, a builder can go further and build the whole spar as a **wood/carbon composite**, matching designer [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own original composite-rig intent for the design. One proposed method builds the spars using the **birdmouth method** — staved, hollow construction from strips of **Sitka spruce or a less-brittle cedar than Western Red** — with the resulting wood spar serving as a **permanent, semi-structural mandrel** for an outer layer of **braided carbon-fiber sleeve**. The braided sleeve is slid onto the spar dry, worked along its full length the way a **Chinese finger cuff** tightens, then wetted out with epoxy — ideally **without vacuum bagging**, where that equipment isn't available. An orthopedic-supply source for braided carbon sleeve exists (formulated for easy wet-out, of uncertain epoxy compatibility), though a sleeve sourced from the **aerospace/aircraft industry** would be preferable for better-characterized strength properties; **A&P Technology** (`braider.com`) is one candidate supplier specializing in braided composites generally. No report of the spar being completed is recorded.[61][62]

## A birdmouth-and-carbon composite mast, revisited

A further variant on the birdmouth idea: a composite spar combining a **birdmouth-built spruce mandrel** with an outer **carbon-fiber sleeve**, worked tight over the wood like a "Chinese finger cuff" then wetted out and squeegeed. The weight difference against the plans' spec mast would be negligible — if anything the composite mast's extra weight aloft would give a more comfortable motion — and the real value in building one would be the experience and "bragging rights" rather than a measurable performance gain. Such a spar is estimated to come out at roughly **half the weight and triple the strength** of the plans' mast.[63][64]

A similar composite spar has been built in person: a **gaff** laid up over a tapered PVC-pipe mandrel (the taper cut by removing gores from the pipe and cementing it back together), first sheathed in a layer of fiberglass and sanded fair, then two layers of tubular carbon fiber applied and wet out together in one step — using plastic tape wrapped over the layup in place of squeegees to consolidate it. In that build the PVC core contributed negligible strength of its own, but a **spruce core** would be expected to contribute plenty.[65]

## A 2023 straight carbon-fibre mast inquiry: sourcing, a stiffness caution, and the lightning-conductivity risk

Beyond the birdmouth-and-composite approach above, a straight carbon-fibre tube mast — rather than a wood/carbon composite spar — was raised in 2023 by a builder just starting construction, inspired by a YouTube builder's own carbon-fibre mast on a 14 ft "Ocean Capable Small Sailboat."[198] Carbon windsurfer masts are one possible cheap source; a used fiberglass mast fitted to a small trimaran as a test survived, though it was never tried in genuinely heavy winds, and cost nothing since it was acquired free. Further named sources for carbon spars or blanks: Forte Carbon, Hall Spars, and Duckworks' Scout dinghy kit, available with a two-part carbon mast — with the standing caution that a genuine carbon spar, once actually sourced, tends to be expensive.[200]

The idea also drew an engineering caution distinct from the pure weight-saving case made elsewhere on this page: a wooden mast's weight is really only a problem while stepping and unstepping it, and a larger boat's own freestanding carbon-fibre/glass mast, built specifically to flex and spill air in puffs, behaves quite differently from the plans' stiff wooden spar; a carbon mast could in principle be built stiff rather than flexible, but the point was judged to need more research before treating carbon as a straightforward substitute.[199]

One owner's own mast — round, hollow timber, built "bird's-nest" fashion — is already light with little windage, and a lighter, cheaper spun-tapered aluminium spar was weighed as a further step; not a pressing change, but any weight saved handling the mast becomes more welcome to an aging sailor.[201]

**Carbon fibre conducts electricity.** A carbon mast should be wired and grounded as though it were a metal one rather than a wooden one, with any masthead wiring designed accordingly — carbon's conductivity would in principle make it "a good lightning rod," for better or worse.[202]

## Wood species for a scratch-built wooden mast

Where **aircraft-grade Sitka spruce** is unavailable or too costly, several alternative woods make a viable mast if built to the plans' design specifications: **Douglas fir** is the strongest substitute, with **Honduras mahogany** and **Alaskan yellow cedar** also usable at some cost in strength. Sitka spruce and Douglas fir should be chosen for long, tight grain with minimal runout; Honduras mahogany is best bought from planed stock, since it tends toward a wilder grain; Alaskan yellow cedar need only be straight and clear — cedar's main advantage over spruce is its smell rather than its workability, spruce being very hard to beat for ease of working. Building by the **birdsmouth method** with a carbon-fiber sleeve (above) loosens the wood choice further, since the sleeve itself supplies much of the mast's strength regardless of which of these species is used for the staves.[66][67][68]

A birdmouth spar takes its name from the shape cut along one edge of each stave: an open "V," resembling a bird's open mouth, with the opposite edge of the neighboring stave cut to the complementary shape so the two dovetail together once glued up — clearest seen in two published illustrations, at `duckworksmagazine.com/articles/birdsmouth` and `users2.ev1.net/~fshagan/bm.htm`, rather than described in words.[69]

Birdsmouth construction has proven a popular choice among builders — judged "fun challenging" to attempt and not too costly a mistake to recover from if a stave is cut wrong.[70]

## A birdsmouth mast weighed against buying better lumber

A quarter of the way into his own build, one builder reworked his mast plan mid-course: rather than sourcing straight, clear lumber from the outset, he had bought cheaper, knotty spruce and then spent weeks resawing it, scarfing it, and designing a round, tapered birdsmouth section — tapered in both diameter and wall thickness — reasoning the result would come out lighter and stronger than a solid stock mast. In hindsight he judged the whole detour "not worth it" against simply buying better wood from the start.[188]

**Reclaimed heart pine as a mast/spar wood.** Old, well-seasoned **heart pine** salvaged from a house remodel (in one recorded case, framing lumber from a 1920s house, hard enough that driving a nail into it was nearly impossible) is a workable choice for a mast and spars, so long as the builder is prepared to dull blades cutting and shaping it — the wood's density and stability make it well suited to the purpose regardless of the extra tool wear.[71][72][73] Old-growth heart pine's tight, close grain and relative lack of knots tend to make it stronger than pine cut today; the one caution is wood that has sat in a hot attic rather than a remodeled living space, since heat damage or overdrying can make it split and check more easily, to the point of splitting when a fastener is driven without a pre-drilled pilot hole — a failure mode also documented in old-growth **Douglas fir**. Working a test piece first settles the question for any individual board. Epoxy adheres to well-seasoned heart pine without special preparation, the same as any other suitable spar wood.[74]

**Testing an unconventional local wood before committing to it.** When a builder has ready access to an unconventional but promising species — one recorded case was several hundred larch (tamarack) trees on the builder's own property, straight-grained hardwood with branches confined to the uppermost part of the trunk — the recommended approach is not to guess but to test: cut measured pieces, compare them against a known mast wood such as clear Douglas fir or Sitka spruce, and use a fishing scale rigged as a makeshift tensometer to record the breaking load, the bend under a given load, behavior under a twist test, and how well the wood takes epoxy. The same test methodology was suggested for judging old, large-diameter oak against the same benchmark woods. This is the general answer to "would species X work for a mast" whenever a builder has a personal supply of an untested wood worth evaluating on its own merits rather than by reputation.[75]

## A glued-2x4 mast with an internal wiring and lightning-ground conduit

Departing from the plans' own drawing, one mast was built from two ordinary **construction-grade 2x4s glued together**, rough-tapered by eye with a handsaw to a top measuring about **1½ in square** — slightly fatter than called for. Before glue-up, a rounded groove (**¼ in deep** in each half) was **router-cut** the full length of both halves to hold a **0.5 in polyethylene tube**, giving a permanent conduit through which the **6-gauge solid-copper lightning-ground wire** and the masthead-light wiring can both be pulled and later replaced without reopening the mast. Because a small mast built solid this way leaves little confidence for an internal halyard sheave — judged too weak-looking a fitting, and awkward besides with wiring already occupying the interior — the halyard instead runs over an external **swivel pulley** mounted on a small shoulder built near the top, with the conduit tube exiting through the mast's side just above the foot. **Gorilla Glue** — a moisture-curing polyurethane that expands and foams as it cures — was used throughout, expected to grip the conduit tube securely as it cures around it; common construction lumber with a few small knots proved adequate for the whole spar.[76]

Whether the same internal-sheave-to-external-sheave switch and wiring/lightning-conductor conduit is worth following remains an open question for at least one further builder weighing the same choice against sticking with the plans as drawn.[143] An untried idea floated for radar visibility is lining the mast cavity with tinfoil, reasoned also to help stop the internal wiring from slapping around inside the mast — though possibly complicating drawing the wires back out if they ever need repair. Realistic future repair need is judged low, except for the masthead light's own wiring, which will likely eventually need reterminating; leaving some extra cable zig-zagged inside the mast, rather than pulled taut, would let that slack be drawn up when the day comes.[143] That builder went on to fit the mast into the vent box horizontally, before it could be stood upright, and secured the internal wiring with electrical cable ties every 25 cm, drawn through an intentionally oversized 16 mm bottom hole so a draw wire could later pull fresh wiring back up the mast; the tinfoil idea itself was abandoned once he learned tin foil is aluminum and gets eaten in salty conditions, leaving the wiring without a radar reflector.[157]

## Whether a heavier mast reduces rolling: no

Building a solid mast from ordinary lumber (above) raises a natural question: would a heavier, more massive mast slow a Paradox's roll from side to side? The firsthand answer is no — long, narrow chine-runner hulls **already roll very little regardless of mast weight**, a fact that surprised on first sailing one, and confirmed again at nearly 40 ft on a sister hull built to the same lifting-body/chine-runner principle. See [chine runners — a claimed secondary effect: roll damping when running free](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-claimed-secondary-effect-roll-damping-when-running-free) for the fuller firsthand rolling record this restates, and [radio and electronics — a wind generator and radar](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md#a-wind-generator-and-radar-a-scale-mockup-exercise-and-how-little-power-a-paradox-actually-needs) for the standing view that keeping weight aloft down, not up, is the better goal for an unstayed rig like this one's.[77]

## Mast dimensions: slenderness as a deliberate weight-and-flex choice

The mast's upper section is noticeably slender compared with a naive expectation of a small boat's spar — a choice made deliberately for weight economy aloft and to let the spar flex and dissipate sudden gust loading rather than resist it rigidly.[78]

## Finished mast weights compared

Several owners have weighed their finished masts against each other directly. [SaSa](/boats/sasa.md)'s came in at **8 kg**;[127] [Scout](/boats/scout.md)'s measured heavier at **10.45 kg**, a figure that includes the masthead band and an internal copper run for the lightning-ground conductor.[128] The band and lightning strap together were guessed, unconfirmed, at around **2.5 kg** of that total;[129] Scout's own mast, though, wasn't built from scratch by her owner and her copper conductor was already installed when he acquired the boat, so the hardware's own separate weight couldn't be pinned down — the mast band alone was put, similarly unconfirmed, at somewhere close to half a kilo.[130] [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)'s Douglas fir mast, complete with its wiring and masthead light but no lightning conductor, weighed **8.5 kg**, re-weighed in January 2015 at close to **9 kg**;[131][132][133][147] [JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md)'s, also Douglas fir and carrying an internal coax run and lighting cable, weighed **9 kg** exactly.[134] A birdsmouth-built hollow mast of eight hoop-pine staves, glued up with professional help to the plans' circular cross-section — for [Thor](/boats/thor.md) — came in lighter still at about **7 kg** — the lightest wooden mast on record — at a real cost in labor, roughly ten hours to the glued-up stage plus a further full day spent planing and sanding it round.[146] The same mast was recalled a year later, more roughly, at about **20 lb (9 kg)** — evidently a rounded recollection rather than a fresh weighing, since nothing about the mast itself had changed.[170] A mast built oversized from a substitute wood rather than Douglas fir, following the drawing's own allowance for that case, is the heaviest wooden mast on record at **32 lb (14.5 kg)**, against other owners' more typical 22 lb.[171]

A finished **aluminum** mast weighs distinctly less than any of the wooden masts above: the 63.5 mm, 3 mm-wall tube already fitted to [Cachivache](/boats/cachivache.md#a-2013-return-to-the-aluminum-mast) (above) came in at about **6 kg**, a figure given in January 2015 replying to a further newcomer's own "why has nobody used an aluminum spar?" question — a newcomer with a set of plans and Don Elliott's build book, considering a Paradox build of his own, who could find no mast-weight estimate anywhere in them.[144][145] The question recurred a year later from the owner of the lightest wooden mast on record (above), who asked whether an aluminum mast section would be lighter still, noting such sections are readily available and not prohibitively expensive, needing only a wooden foot fashioned to fit the mast box — unanswered directly on record.[170]

## Sourcing a sheave and rejecting a masthead Windex

Where a sheave of the exact size called for (12 mm x 30 mm) cannot be bought, a same-size sheave salvaged from spares has been substituted rather than special-ordering one.[79] A masthead Windex wind-direction indicator is not a natural fit for the mast: commercial models either lack a suitable mounting base or are designed to fit to a masthead VHF aerial, which the mast does not carry if the VHF is a handheld set rather than a fixed one.[79]

A further question asked how far down from the masthead the halyard sheave should sit, finding no measurement given on the drawings.[97] The answer: as high as it can be got — one owner fitted a masthead light a couple of inches above the sheave, needing the mast slightly shortened to still stow inside the hull for trailering, with the light's own sleeve fitted over the mast's top so a slot cut down from the top of the wood could take the sheave. A sheave fitted a little farther down the mast on "Faith" has never let her sail be fully hoisted, a few inches always remaining wrapped round the boom.[98] Cutting a slot down from the masthead this way was judged easier than drilling through the wood at the right angle for the sheave pin; once the sheave is seated, gluing a small block of wood back into the slot to refill it restores the masthead's strength there, expected to hold up just as well as the surrounding wood.[99]

An August 2010 recurrence of the same question, from a builder just starting her own mast, drew the same core answer restated: fit the sheave as high as it can go, since too low a sheave stops the sail ever fully hoisting, done by cutting a slot down from the mast's top to seat the sheave and capping the slot with a home-made light fitting.[100][101] A photograph of the resulting masthead, its sheave temporarily removed for a coat of paint, answered the same request for a picture to work from, alongside a pointer to an existing photograph of "Zoë"'s own masthead already in the group's files under "Topping Lift."[102]

## Why the masthead sheave sits at 60 degrees, and the halyard, sheet, and reef-line diameters

A further exchange in June 2021 asked why the masthead sheave is drawn at roughly 60 degrees to the mast's centreline rather than square across it, and what rope diameters the plans call for on the halyard and sheet.[192] The plans' own spar drawing and its materials list turned out to disagree on the sheet's own diameter: the drawing itself specifies 6 mm (1/4 in) Dacron, while the materials list gives 5/16 in (8 mm) Dacron for the tiller (steering) line, halyard, and sheet together, and 1/4 in Dacron for the reef line — a discrepancy tentatively put down to an older-revision CD copy of the plans differing from a printed set, and not resolved on record.[193][194] Full rope-size detail: [running rigging — rigging dimensions](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rigging-dimensions-the-complete-record).

Having found the rope sizes, the remaining question was which way round to fit the sheave.[195] The angle follows from where the halyard actually runs: it comes off the sheave from the mast's starboard, aft side, while the yard itself raises up the port side, so squaring the sheave to the centreline instead would add friction and make both raising and furling harder. As thick a gauge of halyard line as practical is recommended, or the line run in two parts with the heavier gauge given to the handling portion; furling by hand calls for leather gloves regardless, since a thin line under load will cut into bare hands.[196] Whether the sheave itself could be built to a slightly larger diameter and thickness, to take a heavier-gauge halyard, was raised but left unanswered on record.[197]

## Whether an off-center masthead pulley bends the mast, and two builders' fixes

A mast dating to 2010 — carrying its halyard pulley on a stainless strap round the masthead rather than the plans' own internal, centered sheave — was found cracked about two-thirds of the way up five years later, prompting a rebuild and a debate over the cause. One view holds that cutting the slot for an in-mast sheave is itself a weakness worth avoiding, running the halyard over an external pulley instead — the same external arrangement the cracked mast already carried. The rebuild took the opposite lesson: mounting the pulley outside the mast's own centerline, on a strap or similar external fitting, pulls from off the mast's axis and so bends it more than the plans' design does — judged the likelier cause of the crack — so the replacement mast was built to the plans as drawn, slot and sheave included.[160]

A separate, practical answer to the same slot question keeps the plans' cut slot open rather than capping it over: a home-made LED masthead light is mounted on the side of the mast using a single extra-long bolt that does double duty as both the halyard block's own bearing and the light's mount. With the slot left open, the halyard can be lifted out entirely at anchor for a quiet night; under way, reefing tension is relied on to keep the halyard seated in the slot, with the one foreseen weak point being reefing time itself, when that tension briefly drops.[161]

The mechanism behind the off-center verdict above is a couple: a pulley block bolted rigidly to a strap at one side of the masthead cannot articulate, so any halyard load that is not running exactly parallel to the mast sets up a bending stress at the fixing — and the halyard load is seldom exactly parallel to the mast, since the yard's own angle shifts with how the sail is trimmed. Hanging the block from a rope strop instead lets it swing to whatever line the load actually pulls along as the yard moves through a tack, is eased off, or is brought in close-hauled. On port tack the yard tends to press against the mast even as the halyard pulls the masthead aft; on starboard tack the yard instead pulls the masthead to port, an effect that grows more pronounced — with more drift between yard and masthead — the more heavily the sail is reefed. A rope strop's own usable length is limited by needing the mast short enough to still fit inside the boat for stowage, capping how much slack the strop has to work with when the sail is fully hoisted.[162]

## The masthead-light circuit

A masthead light's wiring is commonly run outside the mast past the halyard sheave (see [running lights — routing masthead-light wiring outside the mast, below the sheave](/construction/electrical-wiring.md#routing-masthead-light-wiring-outside-the-mast-below-the-sheave)) and down inside the hollow mast, held off the mast's sides with insulation pipe lagging so it doesn't bang about underway or at anchor; near the mast's foot the wire joins a socket set into the mast wall, so a flex from the battery can simply be plugged in rather than wired through permanently. Running the light from a light sensor rather than a manual switch removes the need for a switch altogether.[80] A VHF antenna's coax feed is sometimes run up the mast alongside the light wiring; for the entry/exit sealing and conduit techniques a 2010 exchange worked out for the combination, see [electrical wiring — a 2010 exchange](/construction/electrical-wiring.md#a-2010-exchange-a-vhf-coax-run-alongside-the-lighting-wiring-and-two-further-conduit-techniques).

## Rainwater ingress at the mast step, and the swelling it causes

The deck's ventilation hole is designed to drain rainwater down past the mast and away through a pipe under the mast's foot. Doing exactly what it is meant to do can still cause a problem: rainwater draining this way keeps the wood at the mast's base damp, and damp wood swells. On one boat the mast jammed solid at its step after several days of rain during a cruise, requiring another hand ashore to help free it the next morning once the wood had dried enough to shrink back; the same fault recurred on the same boat.[81][82] Because of this, a mast step built to fit the mast too snugly is a liability rather than a virtue: it can become almost impossible to extract once the wood has swollen wet, so a step built with a small deliberate clearance — taken up with thin removable wedges between the deck and the mast rather than relied on to be a perfect dry-fit — is the more forgiving choice.[83]

This lift-off simplicity (see [no mast-lowering pin on Paradox, unlike Little Cruiser](#no-mast-lowering-pin-on-paradox-unlike-little-cruiser), above) also makes removing the mast entirely a viable precaution ahead of an oncoming storm, rather than something to attempt only at haul-out.[84]

## Painting versus varnishing

Rainwater working into varnish over time causes slight wood staining and degradation, most noticeably at points that already take chafe wear, such as under the leather sheathing where the boom and sail rub against the mast.[85] Paint is lower-maintenance than varnish and, if a bright colour is chosen, adds real visibility for safety — considerations that favour paint over varnish for spars generally, even though a darker or more saturated paint colour absorbs more solar heat than a lighter one would.[86][87]

## Why the mast carries no stays or shrouds

A recurring question — prompted most recently by the strong, unpredictable gusts common on the Patagonian coast — is why no stays or shrouds appear in any photograph or drawing of the Paradox rig.[88] The plans' answer is reefing rather than standing rigging: when a gust hits, the sail simply rolls down onto the reefing drum — one of the design's strong points is that this can be done without ever going out on deck.[89] A second, independent line of reasoning reaches the same conclusion from the hull rather than the rig: in practice nobody who has sailed the boat has broken a mast, since the hull's own narrowness means the energy in a gust rolls the boat over well before the mast could take enough load to fail — the mast is, in effect, protected by the hull beneath it.[90]

## Keeping the mast aboard through a capsize or knockdown

Nothing in the plans addresses keeping the mast inside the boat if she were to capsize with the sail already lowered: a cleated-off halyard offers some retention while the rig is raised, though its long, near-vertical run limits how much downward tension it can actually hold, and once the sail is fully lowered before a capsize nothing in the design stops the mast from falling out altogether — a possible fix floated for this gap is a pair of fairleads, one fitted to the mast just above deck level and one on the deck itself, pulled together with a short length of vertical line.[153] In practice the concern turns out to be smaller than it looks: with the halyard cleated off inside, the mast cannot be lost completely even in the scenario above, and the as-designed wooden mast has never shown any tendency to come out on its own — its own buoyancy holds it in through a knockdown of more than 90 degrees, and below that angle gravity keeps it seated the same way. A tiedown of the kind floated would do no harm for anyone wanting the extra reassurance, even though the design itself is not thought to need one.[154] A further mechanism was offered for the raised or partly-raised state: with the furling line and the halyard both cleated and free of excess slack, the two lines together lock the mast in position, and it would have to break one of them or a cleat to work loose from the vent box — the only way it could escape is if the furled sail itself unrolled, slackening the halyard. The same holds whether the sail is fully or only partly raised.[155]

## Stepping the mast: a first-timer's question, answered

A newcomer, still new to sailing and working through the plans for the first time, could find no clamp or fitting in the mast/vent-trunk drawings that actually holds the mast down against a gust, and asked for a step-by-step account of how the mast is actually stepped.[91] The answer, restated as an eight-step routine: lay the mast along the cabin roof, rear side down, foot forward; climb onto the foredeck; pick the mast up; swing it to the vertical; poke the foot through the hole in the deck; give it a wiggle to seat the foot; climb carefully back off the foredeck (the one real hazard in the whole operation); then get on with something less exciting, like making coffee. The sequence is judged almost impossible to get wrong, short of leaving a line slack enough to catch under the foot. Once the sail is hoisted, the halyard's own downward tension — reinforced indirectly by the furling line — is what actually holds the mast down in its step; on [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) the mast has never shown the least sign of wanting to move. The only trouble on record has run the opposite way, and on a different boat: one builder made his own mast step fit so well that he had trouble getting the mast back out again.[92][93]

## Easing mast raising and lowering for an aging or less able sailor (January 2016)

Raising and lowering the mast alone gets harder for a sailor without the balance and strength once had; one option considered is modifying the mast to hinge and rest on the cabin top, raised and lowered with a trailer winch.[167] The task is more manageable if the boat is level and steady — on its trailer, or afloat resting on a beach or mud bank — while working the foredeck; afloat in deep water the boat starts rocking as the mast is lifted in a kind of bear hug, and inertia can send sailor and mast in all directions, close to going over the side.[168] A tight-fitting mast makes the job harder still: one mast was initially so tight in its housing that freeing it took real effort, and swelled further after a few days' cruising until levers were needed to prise it out; shaving wood from the heel for a deliberately loose, rattling fit resolved the problem for good — a first-hand account confirming, in more detail, the tight-mast-step friction problem [recorded above only secondhand](#stepping-the-mast-a-first-timers-question-answered). A tapered guide channel built just forward of the mast hole, giving the mast's heel a bigger target to aim for going in, was proposed as a further aid, worth trying first in sheet aluminium before committing to epoxy.[168] Going the hinged-tabernacle route instead, one refinement is to offset the tabernacle slightly so the mast comes down to one side of the hatch rather than down the centre, leaving the hatch still usable to sit in without risking the mast on the way down.[169] Asked directly whether a mast giving this much trouble was built to plan or heavier, the answer confirmed the latter: sized up from the plans' own noted allowance for a substitute wood other than Douglas fir, it came in at 32 lb against other owners' more typical 22 lb — still weighed against the same hinge-and-tabernacle option, with family able to lift it if truly needed, though managing it unaided was still preferred.[171] Confirmed the following March that the hinge, fitted with a simple tabernacle, worked well and needed no modification to the boat itself.[172]
