# Tack strop

The tack strop and the tack strut / boom-axle pivot at the mast, which anchor the forward corner of the sail. The strop runs inside a brass pipe spacer and down through a flared copper tube where the line enters the deck.

## What the tack strop does, and doesn't do: luff tension comes from the furling line

The tack strop's own job is limited to fixing the boom's height at the mast; it does not set or maintain luff tension, a point of confusion for a builder working from the drawings and other builders' photographs alone rather than from sailing experience. Luff tension is actually held by the **furling line's own tension** once the halyard is belayed — the strop needs no separate downhaul or adjustment mechanism to do its job, matching Matt Layden's own instruction for it: simply "make fast to cleat and forget it."[1] A builder who had assumed, by analogy with an ordinary sailboat, that luff tension came from halyard tension alone confirmed the correction once it was given.[2] Closing out the same thread, a further builder asked to be put down for a copy of the design too, expecting to need it made slightly longer to clear his own taller cabin — consistent with the strut/pipe length depending on the cabin top's own height and shape, already noted above.[3]

## A fastening caution for new hardware, and a design compliment

Feedback on a newly-built tack-strop assembly added a practical fastening caution: mount hardware of this kind **bolted rather than screwed**, since it is expected to see some "interesting" loads in service, consistent with the load path already established above — while praising the tack strop and furling-line combination itself as a **genuinely universal joint**, doing its job regardless of the exact angle it ends up at.[4]

Answering that caution directly, one such assembly uses **four stainless steel #10-32 flat-head screws** with **Nylock stainless steel nuts and washers** underneath, chosen specifically to hold up under luff tension and wind loads.[5] A later rework of the same fitting replaced its original **cotter pins**, which had begun acting like fish-hooks and snagging the reefing rope, with **snap rings** instead, in a redesign using fewer pieces that also fixed an overlong-pin issue the earlier design carried.[6]

## The deck-entry tube wears the paint, but rarely more

The copper tube's edge does wear into the deck surface where the strop passes through it, so the fitting is normally built up to protect the deck rather than left bare: flaring out the bottom of the strut and building up a pad of epoxy around the deck entry is one fix, a tufnol pad another, and a plain metal washer would work as well. In practice the wear stays mostly cosmetic — it wears through the paint rather than the wood — because while sailing, the tie-down is normally in tension and holds the strut clear of the deck; the only time of prolonged contact is sailing with a deep reef, avoided where possible. So long as the strut's underside carries no sharp edge, the deck-entry wear is not a major problem, and simply anticipating it as a possible issue before building is usually enough to avoid it turning into one.[7][8][9]

## The tack strut also carries compression, not just tension

Two seasons of experience corrected a mistaken assumption about the **tack tie-down strut**: it is not purely a tension member. On the belief that the strut was only ever in compression while the sail was furled and being stowed, it was first built from ordinary 12mm copper water pipe, split at the top and left flimsy on the reasoning that it only had to be strong in tension. The forked end crumpled twice in successive seasons — first attributed to an unintentional jibe, then, after being straightened and reinforced with a second soldered-on pipe layer, to the strut bending round the mast when running downwind with the boom forward of the beam. Switching to thick-walled rubber hose to allow some flex without damage exposed the real cause: **when reefed, the strut must support not only the boom's weight (with the sail wrapped round it) but also the added tension of the reefing line itself** — a load a first season's largely unreefed sailing, and a looser rig setup, had never exposed. The eventual fix was a stronger strut built from **water pipe for the straight section, joined to a stainless-plate "vee" end** by a pipe fitting — a straightforward but more robust departure from the lightweight original. The episode is a caution against assuming a fitting's load case from rig theory alone; practical testing under a reefed sail is what actually reveals the load path.[10][11][12][13]

The failure mode is independently confirmed: the same lightweight-pipe approach did not hold up on another boat either, and the fix that finally worked was switching the strut material outright to **bronze (brass) Schedule 40 pipe**, after which the problem did not recur.[14] Trying to source the same fix in the UK, the plans' spec — **1/4 in Schedule 40 brass pipe, 14mm O.D., 220mm long** — does not map cleanly onto pipe sold locally; a question arose whether the bore should be 1/4 in with a 4mm wall, since that thickness would need to accept an 8mm tack strop passed through it. The **220mm length is not fixed** either: it depends on how far the 12mm brass bar is bent, where the tack tie-down hole falls in the deck, and the cabin top's height and shape, so the practical approach is to make the strut long and trim it to fit on final assembly — one came out at 250mm.[15]

A spare length of bronze (brass) Schedule 40 pipe stock was offered to any builder needing some.[16] By the time the offer arrived, the UK rebuilt strut had already stood up to a weekend's sailing without recurrence, so others were pointed to the spare-pipe offer instead.[17] That finished tack-strut installation, uploaded as reference photographs to the group's file area, also documents a practical chafe fix: the rope emerging at the deck entry is sleeved in plastic tube to reduce wear at that point.[18] A fuller trial followed a six-day cruise: the rebuilt strut held up to everything encountered, including force-5 jibes and sailing heavily reefed, confirming the original failure had indeed been a matter of an under-built strut rather than any flaw in the plans' own design.[19]

Answering another builder's question years later, the same underlying failure and fix were restated in brief: the original **12mm copper water pipe**, split at the end as the plans show, could not take the load once reefed and collapsed; the working replacement still uses ordinary water pipe, now capped with a **stainless steel U-piece**. The plans' own **Schedule 40 brass pipe** remains hard to source in the UK, but should work as drawn if it can be found.[20]

A second, independently developed fix for the same fitting uses a **split "Y" spacer**, strengthened with **wooden capping pieces glued either side and sealed under epoxy sheathing** — proven over two seasons of sailing with no further trouble. A reference photograph of this arrangement was uploaded to the group's Photos section.[21]

## The tack strut and boom axle's pivot joint: why a "V," and alternatives

The tack strut and boom axle both terminate in a **forked ("V") end** at their pivot bolt in the plans, which prompted a separate question about whether the V-shape is functionally necessary or simply how the plans happen to draw it. Two untested alternatives were floated: splitting only one of the two pieces (leaving the other whole) to make a narrower fork and a shorter pivot bolt, or dispensing with the fork altogether and drilling both pieces as plain round-section stock, bolted through side by side.[22] No engineering objection to either alternative was recorded; a builder who had already built the standard forked ("V") version in Schedule 40 pipe (thick-walled pipe, not a special "brass" grade as such) offered a photograph of the setup, while flagging one specific practical concern with substituting **galvanised steel pipe** — cutting it exposes bare metal at the cut ends, which will rust where the galvanising has been cut through.[23] The plans' brass pipe itself can be sourced from `www.onlinemetals.com`.[24] Proposed fixes for the galvanised-pipe cut-end rust problem included **epoxying the pipe ends** and fitting a soft-metal (copper) cap where the strut meets the deck;[25] a **cold-galvanising spray** product or a "Zinga"-brand coating, both of unproven durability in this application; and, as a more thorough option, having the small part **hot-dip galvanised** at a metal shop — though local galvanisers charge a fixed minimum fee regardless of the piece's small size, making scrap-yard-sourced galvanised pipe (cheap or free, replaced as it rusts) a more practical choice than a custom hot-dip run for a single fitting. Black-anodised steel was also floated as a durable, corrosion-resistant alternative finish.[26][27] Weighing the plans' brass-pipe source against local galvanised-pipe stock, shipping brass pipe from the US would carry a disproportionate CO2 cost for the size of the part, so galvanised steel was kept.[28]

Hot-dip galvanising was set aside for a further practical reason beyond cost: local galvanisers, at a fixed minimum charge regardless of part size, were judged simply unwilling to deal with the aggravation of one-off small pieces, whereas galvanised pipe is cheap or free at scrap yards and can be replaced outright as it rusts rather than protected indefinitely.[29][30] Sourcing suitable **brass tube** directly, as an alternative to galvanised pipe, ran into its own supply problem: non-ferrous scrap yards stock only thin-wall tube in short lengths, apparently because factories salvage their non-ferrous waste more carefully than their ferrous waste — leaving epoxy coating as the practical way to get a strong tube at minimal cost.[31] The epoxy-coating method itself was refined to **sanding (abrading) the pipe before coating**, with a final coat of paint over the epoxy to protect it from UV degradation.[32] Tested in practice, the epoxy method "worked fine" and was judged much simpler than sourcing or galvanising metal pipe at all.[33][34]

Reference photographs of a finished pivot joint were later uploaded to the group's Photos section.[35] The joint's fabrication was described in full: the furling rope runs through a sheave mounted on a plate that seizes onto the axle via a hose clamp; a sleeve, made from the same stock as the pipe, increases the axle's effective diameter and spins freely on it so the fitting is free to find its own angle rather than bind; two stops (initially just hose clamps) hold the assembly in place, with a planned upgrade to formal stops machined from the same brass pipe, drilled, tapped, and fitted with an adjustable set screw — brazing on a nut first if the pipe wall proves too thin to hold threads on its own. The pivot bolt itself is serviced periodically: once its threads wear thin from use, the plan is to fit a locking nut, cut the bolt off, and step up to the next bolt size. The end notches cut into the pipe were made with a fiber-disk cutoff wheel, though drilling and hacksawing the notches out, then sanding the edges smooth, was noted as a workable alternative.[36]

## A May 1994 plans revision explains the forked-vs-plain spacer tube

Comparing an unrevised plan set against one bearing a Sheet 9 revision dated May 1994 resolved a recurring point of confusion: the earlier drawing ties the tack strop's spacer tube off with a plain knot against the boom hook, while the revised sheet forks the tube's top into a "Y," letting the knot nestle in the fork instead.[37][38] A builder working from an older plan set, puzzled that his own drawing showed no fork while completed boats' photographs consistently did, found on comparison that his paper and disc copies disagreed with each other for exactly this reason.[39][40] The fitting the revision touches is the same spindle-and-tube assembly covered above: a 12mm brass rod bends down and splits, joined by a 6mm stainless bolt and peened nut to the split top of the strut tube, with sturdy construction called for since it carries real compressive and bending load whenever sailing reefed.[38] The revision reads as Matt Layden's own improvement to the fitting, not merely a drafting change.[40] A secondhand recollection elsewhere places the same revision a year later, in **May 1996**, illustrated with the very same `reefingdrum.jpg` photograph used here — recorded as a discrepancy rather than resolved, since the dated Sheet-9 notation used here is the stronger evidence; see [furling system — the Y-fitting's purpose confirmed, and the plans' own May 1996 revision](/components/rig/furling.md#the-y-fittings-purpose-confirmed-and-the-plans-own-may-1996-revision) for that account.

The revision's necessity was independently confirmed from years-old experience: the builder whose own tack strut famously crumpled twice in its first two seasons (above) had been working from a plan set predating the revision, and recognized the connection at once on seeing the photograph of the older fitting — the earlier, unforked joint genuinely does collapse sideways under a reefed sail's load, exactly as his own did before it was rebuilt.[41]

## A 2015 exchange: why the fork helps, pivot-bolt orientation, and a caution against over-modifying it

A further newcomer's question about the same fork — whether it serves any purpose beyond anchoring the tack line's stopper knot, since the plans otherwise show only a plain knot against the boom hook — drew a fuller mechanical explanation than the May 1994 revision note above states outright: the split is not strictly essential, but it lets the joint hinge in a single plane rather than wobble, keeping the fitting from becoming floppy in use.[78][79] The pivot bolt itself works best as a stainless bolt with a mushroom-shaped head, mounted so the smooth head, not the sharper threaded end, bears against the deck where the furled sail lies along the side deck — kinder to the deck paint; the tack line runs through the tube with its stopper knot sitting in the split just below the pivot bolt, never touching the bronze rod itself.[79]

A firsthand caution followed from a builder who had gone further than the plans' own single fork: a **double** Y-split bound the furler in some circumstances, the forced side-angle it put on the furling line fighting the pivot's own freedom to move in any direction; reverting to the plan's own geometry ended the trouble. The same builder judges any fork or Y adds an extra joint that can fail and weakens the pivot, where a plain rope strop, run exactly as the plans show, wears visibly and can be checked at a glance — "if it's good enough for Matt Layden, it's good enough for me," while welcoming builders competent enough to try improving on it. This restates, from the far side of an actual failure, the lesson already on record at [boats/ironsides.md — a roller-furler failure on the Saguenay Fjord](/boats/ironsides.md#a-roller-furler-failure-on-the-saguenay-fjord-2015): the plans' true bent-arm spec is **1/2 in** bronze rod, and the 3/8 in stainless substitute that failed there was simply too thin for the job. The plans' brass spacer tube is not always easy to find in useful lengths either — a 12 ft stock length goes a long way beyond what one boat needs, leaving plenty spare to give away.[80]

A second builder reported building the fitting broadly the same way, noting no rationale from Matt Layden himself is on record for the choice — early builders simply experimented with what the drawings left open.[81]

## A stainless-steel alternative for the swivel rod

Distinct from the tack strut covered above is the **swivel rod** bent into the tack-strop fitting itself, whose curve — together with the strop pipe's length — fixes the boom-to-mast reach at the tack. Answering a question about how best to bend this rod, one builder made his own from **0.500 in diameter stainless steel** round stock, heated and bent to shape with an **oxy-acetylene torch** — a straightforward alternative to the plans' brass, with no problems reported.[42]

A later question asked how to bend the plans' own **12mm bronze rod** version of the same spindle at all — called "seriously tough stuff," with advice received to anneal it by heating to cherry red and quenching in water, and asking whether anyone had managed to cold-bend it instead.[48] One answer: no need to quench it at all, since plain air cooling is good enough; slip a length of pipe over the rod at the point of the bend and use it for leverage, repositioning and bending a little at a time as needed, with even a forked tree or a hole drilled in a stump or log serving to hold the rod while bending it by force, no annealing required.[49] A second answer, using stainless rather than bronze, relied on brute force alone instead: two people and a big hammer, one heaving on a length of pipe slipped over the rod for leverage while the other struck it, with a genuinely solid base for the fixed end proving essential — an ordinary bench vice "just isn't man enough" for the job — taking the bend slowly and re-annealing if it gets harder to move.[50] The original question was closed out days later with a third, independently successful method: a length of steel conduit slipped over the rod for leverage, an old washing-line post to bear the bend against, and a little heat from a blowtorch were enough on their own to form the rod to shape, with no annealing or second pair of hands needed.[51]

A separate technique addresses how the boom itself is bored to receive this rod once bent: see [boom-end tack line holes — drilling the tack end for the swivel-rod bearing](/construction/boom-end-tack-line-holes.md#drilling-the-tack-end-for-the-swivel-rod-bearing-and-a-further-tang-bolt-caution).

## First live test: an untested, ad-hoc fitting, pending a fuller trial

Congratulated on his own boat's October 2008 launch, [Stephen Yahn](/people/stephen-yahn.md) was asked how his tack strop — built to a more rigid design than the plans, and doubted at the time as possibly "too clever" for its own good (see [above](#what-the-tack-strop-does-and-doesnt-do-luff-tension-comes-from-the-furling-line)) — had performed under real sailing, and whether a detailed drawing of it could be worked up for the plans if it proved out.[43] His answer was a candid non-endorsement: he would be glad to help any builder use the same design once he was sure it was actually correct, tested through real bad weather and some time; he had made no drawings, had built the fitting from whatever bits of material he happened to have on hand rather than to any considered plan, and did not think other builders should necessarily treat it as a first choice to copy yet — "it may be fine just as it is anyway. It needs testing."[44] This closes out the fitting's construction history on record without yet closing its performance question; see [boats/ironsides.md](/boats/ironsides.md#congratulations-and-the-tack-strops-first-still-unproven-test) for the fuller launch account. Seven months later, seven photographs of the finished fitting, titled "A Version of the Roll Reefing Tack Strop," were posted to the group's Photos section — the axle rod secured with a groove and brass screw exactly as called for in the plans.[45] One admiring reply guessed the parts looked like stainless steel work beyond his own means, though he could probably manage brass fittings himself[46] — confirmed as correct: [Stephen Yahn](/people/stephen-yahn.md) had made the whole fitting from stainless steel simply because that happened to be what was on hand in his own scrap box, judging brass or bronze would have worked just as well.[47]

## A further builder's own reinforcement, and a washboard alternative proposed for a commissioned rebuild

A further recorded reinforcement, this time to the standard deck fittings rather than the strut itself: hardwood, epoxied on over the stock fittings, to make them stronger — illustrated with a photograph posted to the group's own files.[52] The same exchange, prompted by a request for modification ideas for a hypothetical professionally built rebuild, also proposed doing away with the tack strop's own single-fitting job altogether in one particular: fitting a single fold-down washboard with a port in it, in place of the rear window, paired with a removable hinged tiller extension — full account at [hatch — a 2011 canvass for a possible commissioned rebuild](/components/hatch.md#a-2011-canvass-for-a-possible-commissioned-rebuild-hinged-versus-sliding-and-a-washboard-alternative).[52]

## A 2012 how-to thread: adhesive, rod material, and three further bending methods

A newcomer's detailed run of eight fabrication questions about the reefing-drum/swivel-rod fitting on Sheet 9 drew a full set of answers, several confirming techniques already established above from independent builders. The copper pipe that receives the swivel rod is glued in with epoxy — sealing the pipe's inner end first, before the glue-up, keeps the bore itself from being contaminated — with silica added to the epoxy for more of a gap-filling "glue" consistency.[53][54][56] One caution raised but not tested against the working copper-pipe installations already on this page: copper may not be stiff enough for the fitting's load-distribution job at all, since it deforms easily under load, and something stiffer might suit better.[55]

Rod-material choice divides along the lines already recorded elsewhere on this page: **464 naval brass** is hard to machine and, while it wears well, **silicon bronze** — the material actually called for in the plans — is judged the easier working choice; a separately reported build used **phosphor bronze** to the same plans spec, finding it "tough stuff" to cut and drill but otherwise straightforward.[54][56] The retaining-screw groove near the rod's inner end cuts easily with a hand grinder, is not especially critical to get exact, and on at least one boat was never actually fitted with its stop screw at all — the boom and strop could not plausibly part company without it regardless.[54][56] Two further, independent thimble/fairlead solutions for attaching the reefing line at the swivel rod: one builder developed his own modification to the stock fitting, illustrated in photographs uploaded to the group; a second simply drilled two 3 mm holes and screwed on a fairlead of his own.[54][56] Where no brass tack-strop spacer pipe was on hand, standard copper water pipe substituted, with the swivel end modified using ordinary plumbing fittings since the plans' own fitting as drawn was judged not strong enough — a second, independent confirmation of the water-pipe substitution already recorded above.[56]

[OkieBob](/people/okiebob.md)'s own account of finally bending the rod is one of the fuller do-it-yourself stories on this page: rather than farm the bend out, he tried a muffler shop (equipment too narrow for the rod), a metals supplier (no bending equipment, but similar rod in stock), a fabrication shop whose supervisor bent it slightly by hand across his own knee before the talk drifted into welding, and a machine shop and a retired machinist, neither available — before building his own jig at home from ordinary wood clamps and a 6 ft steel cheater bar. The bend itself, once attempted, "took just a few minutes." Two further bending methods were recorded from the same exchange: heating the bend area with an ordinary blow torch, as used for brazing copper tubing, lets brass of this thickness bend easily once red hot, with the caution to cut the retaining-bolt slot into the rod **before** bending it, since the groove machines far more easily while the rod is still straight; and splitting both the rod and the tack-strop spacer pipe cold, with a 32-tpi hacksaw blade, "went smooth," with the pipe bending easily by hand while the thicker rod needed a pipe-flaring tool to open its Y-bend first.[57][58][59]

The final assembly used a **clevis pin, not the plans' bolt**, to join the rod's forked ends — found by chance at a hardware store while shopping for a stainless bolt, and preferred once seen — with a keyring to keep the clevis pin from working loose.[59]

A keyring of this kind was endorsed as a good solution to a fastening problem already met once before on the same fitting: an earlier **cotter pin** had begun acting like a fish-hook, snagging the reefing rope, and was remade with a **snap ring set in a lathed groove** instead — a fix preferred at the time and recalled again here as the better of the two.[60]

## A 2012 recurrence: brass rejected, bronze and stainless favored, and a further bending method

A separate correspondent's question about the same swivel rod's material, unable to locate the group's own earlier discussion of it, drew a fuller debate a few weeks later.[61] Brass was judged the material to avoid, likely to prove too brittle and crack under stress — bronze probably the best choice, stainless steel probably pretty good too — with the observation that a metal easily bent also unbends just as easily.[62] Bronze or stainless was seconded independently, alongside a bending method distinct from those recorded above: a hole drilled in a tree to hold the bar, a scaffold pipe or similar slid over the projecting end for leverage, and a helper hammering it while it was levered round, with a log wedged in to give the bend a nice, controlled radius; the retaining groove machined into the rod's inner end was judged not strictly necessary — fitted once but no longer bothered with, its screw left untightened with no tendency for the joint to come apart.[63] Restating an established method with a further detail: 0.5 in stainless bar bent red-hot with an oxy-acetylene torch gives good control over where and how sharply the bend forms, depending on where the heat is applied, with a welding shop suggested for anyone needing help.[64] Naval brass sourced from Duckworks was reported in active use for the same fitting — "plenty strong," and pleasing so far, bending before it would crack, a direct rejoinder to the brass-cracks-under-stress caution above — though only time would tell whether it held up.[65]

## A 2012 comparison: "Miss Cindy" eliminates the tack strop with fore-and-aft topping lifts

While researching how other builders had bent the swivel rod, a further comparison surfaced: Tony Bigras' "Miss Cindy," a boat whose rig is described as similar to Paradox's own, does away with the curved swivel-rod fitting documented at length above, using fore-and-aft topping lifts to hold the boom and only a short strap where Paradox uses the metal tack strop.[66] Whether the tack strop is strictly needed once fore-and-aft topping lifts are already fitted — and whether Miss Cindy's own arrangement instead adds a complication, since it is not as easy to drop the boom flat on deck to unstep the mast — was raised as an open comparison.[66]

Two builders who also carry a fore-and-aft topping lift alongside the standard tack strop answered directly. One uses the lift only to assist furling and reefing — rigged as a "Y," with a single line running up the mast and down into the cabin — while the tack strop itself still does several jobs the lift doesn't: it stops the boom lifting too far, helps harden the luff, holds the furled boom in place on the port side deck when stowed, and anchors the fairlead for the reefing/furling line; on this view the Paradox tack strop, as designed, is "a piece of design 'genius' in all that it accomplishes so simply."[67] The other's own fore-and-aft lift has to be slackened when sailing close-hauled, since left taut it spoils the sail's shape, so when reefed and hauled in the strut still ends up doing real work supporting the boom; either way, the tack fitting has to hold the boom both vertically and horizontally along its length, a job Tony's own arrangement handles differently, at the cost of making it harder to get the boom down flat on deck — no problem for him on Miss Cindy, but likely to be a bit of a nuisance on a Paradox, where, for an unrelated reason, one owner already used to leave the boom raised because an outboard motor was in the way of dropping it. No single best answer emerged: many solutions exist, each with its own compromises, and choosing between them comes down to which features matter most to the builder.[68]

A further, apparently unrelated video surfaced in the same thread: a modified furling system using a wooden arm in place of the metal tack strop and swivel rod, filmed aboard an unidentified builder's own boat and reported to "work fine." The same boat's own website carried the Paradox plans and further photographs online, though watching the video alone left the mechanism's actual working unclear, including the purpose of a part labelled "filoire" (French for fairlead).[69] The filoire itself was confirmed to be simply a guide for the furling rope, keeping it clear of the arm and running along the cabin roof, secured close to the mast rather than left hanging loose; a separate elastic component of the mechanism was mentioned but not explained.[70][71] The plywood "fingers" forming part of the system had gone through an abandoned first attempt using round shapes, given up for lack of achievable precision.[70] The same boat's sail was put at a surprising 17 m² for a hull her size, cut completely flat and rolled around the boom — read by a further respondent, who judged the whole system worth adapting to a non-Paradox hull of his own as simpler than a junk rig, as the likely reason it furls so easily.[72][73]

## A 2013 recurrence: ten years' service confirms the deck-entry fix, and a chafe cure

A further newcomer's questions about the same fitting — whether the bottom tube's end floats on the line and bears against the deck, and whether the line itself works as the strop's "universal joint" at deck level — drew a restatement of what is already established above: the tube does bear on the deck, and the strop is in tension under full hoist but shifts into compression when reefed, taking some but not all of the boom's weight.[74][75] A decade after building it, the glass/epoxy deck patch and belled pipe end (see [above](#the-deck-entry-tube-wears-the-paint-but-rarely-more)) had scratched the paint but caused no significant wear beyond that — a long-term confirmation of the fix. A separate wear point emerged after a couple of years: the rope itself chafing where it bends at the deck entry, cured by switching to a thinner rope sleeved in plastic tube, with no further trouble since. The boom-axle retaining screw (see [above](#the-tack-strut-and-boom-axles-pivot-joint-why-a-v-and-alternatives)) was fitted originally but is no longer bothered with, the boom showing no inclination to come off the axle without it.[75]

A general wear-management principle followed from the same exchange: where two dissimilar materials rub together at a fitting like this, it pays to arrange things so that whichever one takes the most damage is also the one that is easiest to fix or replace — after ten years' service the pipe end itself was, by 2013, looking a bit bashed about and might need replacing within a couple more.[76][77]

## A 2021 build using a Harbor Freight tube bender, and a fuller account of how the fitting works

A further swivel-rod build in 2021 used **1/2 in silicon bronze rod** — matching the plans' own bent-arm spec already established above, rather than the thinner 3/8 in stainless substitute that failed elsewhere — bent on a manual tube/pipe bender's **1 in die**, working from a set of CAD-drawn templates and a written bending procedure of its own; the actual bend came out different from the templates on the first attempt, the bender itself being new to hand, though the bronze rod showed no sign of cracking on the die despite no prior experience bending that metal. A further set of surrounding pieces was still to be ordered to complete the fitting.[82] This is a further, more controlled bending method beyond those already on record above — [torch-heated bending, tree-and-pipe leverage, hammer-and-helper, and blowtorch-with-pipe-and-post methods](#a-stainless-steel-alternative-for-the-swivel-rod) — using a purpose-built commercial tool rather than an improvised jig.

Answering a newcomer's basic question about what the tack strop actually does — whether it limits the boom's forward movement, or the rudder's[83] — the fitting was worked through part by part, offered as one builder's own reading of the mechanism from photographs rather than from firsthand sailing experience.[84] The swivel rod's own axle end fits into a copper tube bored into the boom's tip, letting it rotate as the sail furls; the first curve is shaped to clear the furling drum's own forward-most disc, and the straight section beyond takes a thimble that guides the reefing line. The fitting cannot be made as one rigid piece running the whole way from boom to deck: as the sail shifts fore-and-aft a solid link would raise and lower the boom's rear end, which is exactly the collapsing failure mode the May 1994 plans revision (above) fixed by replacing a plain knotted joint with a hinged one. The yard is tied directly to the halyard at the masthead, acting in effect as a further universal joint there. Comparable mechanisms were noted on two further, non-Paradox boats besides "Miss Cindy" (a catamaran sailing identical twin sails, already covered above) — named as "Bigornick" and a "PDR(X)" — each using some variation of a topping lift in place of the tack strop.[84] The plans' own rope-diameter figures for the tack line itself, and an unresolved discrepancy in them, are gathered at [running rigging — rigging dimensions](/components/rig/running-rigging.md#rigging-dimensions-the-complete-record).

Practicing further with spare bronze rod left over from the same build, a follow-up question asked how the swivel rod's forked end is actually flattened to take the tack line's rope — by anvil and hammer, by press, or by simply drilling a hole without flattening the rod at all.[85]
