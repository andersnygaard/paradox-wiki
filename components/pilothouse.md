# Pilothouse

The Paradox's most distinctive feature: a small, fully enclosed walk-in cabin ("pilothouse") that a single sailor lives, sleeps, and steers from without going on deck. The helmsman sits inside, working the sheet and tiller through the closed hull, and the boat is sailed hatch-shut as ordinary practice rather than as a defensive posture. This page covers the cabin **as a structure** — how the box is built and why it is built in that order; its glazing, hatch, ventilation, beams, and interior each have their own pages, gathered at the end.

## Trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin

A raised-deck cabin was weighed against the stock trunk cabin directly on the mailing list: it would give more interior space and more top-side buoyancy in a knockdown, and might be easier and stronger to build, trading away the trunk cabin's lower side decks — though those decks see less use for walking forward, seldom needed on Paradox, than possibly for laying the furled boom and gaff along them when reefed, a use raised, and judged plausible by a second correspondent, but never confirmed as actual practice on record.[5][6][9] Two points were made for keeping the trunk cabin as designed: it gives much better visibility, particularly forward, when the boat is buttoned up for heavy weather;[7] and its profile is credited with helping the boat self-right from a full, 180-degree knockdown, alongside the deck arch discussed on [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md).[8] Neither point settled the safety question on its own: a raised deck's own greater buoyancy aloft was proposed as a competing advantage, cutting the odds that a knockdown escalates into a full inversion in the first place rather than only how well the boat recovers once already there;[10] and a separate view doubted that cabin shape decides the outcome at all, holding that a properly ballasted Paradox should not capsize outside a wave breaking directly over her, a case in which the cabin would matter little either way.[11] None of the three claims was tested against the others, and the safety comparison between the two cabin forms stayed a matter of competing theory rather than settled fact.

Cabin windows drew a separate strand of the same debate. A raised-deck cabin could in principle carry even more forward-facing glazing than a trunk cabin, and just as many side windows — it is a genuinely flush-decked design, lacking any raised structure to carry windows at all, that typically has none facing forward.[12] How close those windows should sit to the sailor split opinion: one view held that the farther a pane sits from the helmsman's head the less useful it becomes, since heeling and wave motion further degrade a distant view, whereas nearby ports let a small head movement compensate — reasoning drawn from experience designing unbuilt hulls with a more Birdwatcher-like cabin;[13] the opposing view worried that in so small and confined a cabin, sitting too close to the glazing risks a lurch smacking a sailor's head against the trunk side, especially with two people sharing the space.[14] A builder who had owned both kinds of window arrangement supplied the firsthand comparison neither side of that exchange could: his previous boat's low, raised-deck-style side windows were hopeless to see through and impossible to clean underway, so he ended up sailing by looking out the open slot instead, while Paradox's own windows work — short and wide, set away from the sides where spray would reach them, and close enough to the sailor to use without difficulty; sloped glazing, he thought, might improve on that further still.[15]

A further, independent account reached the same conclusion: most of the space under the side deck is shelving and storage, so moving the windows outward would not meaningfully add interior room, and sitting normally in the corner of the seat — near the cabin's own centre of rotation — leaves little relative motion to contend with; two aboard are unlikely to sit side by side in any case, so a window is no closer to a second occupant than to one alone.[16] Pushing the windows out to the sheer line was floated as an alternative, along with whether losing the side decks — climbing over the cabin top instead — would be missed.[17] A second owner reported never coming close to hitting his head either, on a similarly-windowed Gorfnik cabin as much as on Paradox itself, and found the side decks earn their keep stowing the yuloh and the furled sail together without blocking the way forward.[18][19] Pressed on the specific trade of moving the windows to the sheer line, the answer came back against it: the side window at the cabin's rear corner is where a shoulder rests in ordinary relaxed sailing, going forward would be harder, a taller structure raises weight aloft with a possible stability cost for a heavier sailor, and the neat stowage for the furled sail and yuloh would be lost — given the choice, the design as drawn wins out.[20]

A further account weighed in from the opposite direction: the side decks earn their place mainly for going forward onto the foredeck — a favoured spot to sit at anchor in fair weather — rather than for steering visibility, since helming from inside the cabin is only an occasional practice for some owners, who find window visibility a non-issue as a result; keeping the doghouse to the stock plan is the obvious choice if side bins are ever added.[21] A stretched, deck-cabin Enigma variant supplied the negative comparison: extending the doghouse aft to the gunnels, with a fully removable hatch cover added for forward access, looked and worked less well than Paradox's own arrangement — offered as one reason the designer himself has kept sailing a Paradox.[21]

A further real-world case bore the same lesson out. One owner had, on an earlier Paradox of his own, pushed the side decks out to the sheer line and fitted a longer tiller to sit sideways as on a conventional sailboat; the wider cabin cost him the side decks' use for stowing the yuloh and mast and made climbing on and off the cabin top harder, and rather than reverting the change he sold that boat and built a second one with the cabin to the stock plan — read as a sign the widening had not paid off as hoped, and reinforcing that a genuinely good reason is needed before departing from the designed cabin width; visibility itself was never in question, the same owner having sailed the boat for six days of cold rain without complaint.[22]

## A box that only becomes rigid once it is closed

The cabin is not stiff while it is being built. The window side frames go up while the structure can still move — the top rails "will flex a little after they're in place, but not much" — and it is only once every piece is in place that it becomes "a very rigid structure, designed to stand up to any sea condition."[1] The component that locks it is the **plywood roof skin**, which "adds great strength to the doghouse": it prevents any of the rails or beams from moving anywhere, and stops the roof twisting in any manner.[1] Everything below therefore follows an order in which each stage is checked and corrected *before* the next one removes the chance to adjust it.

## It goes on last, because afterwards there is no working room

Getting into a finished Paradox is a climb up and in — "more like climbing into the cockpit of a jet fighter" rather than stepping into an open day-sailer's accessible cockpit — so **all interior work must be completed before the cabin structure goes on**.[1] One deliberate exception is held open past that point: **one side of the vent trunk is left off** until the mast and all the lines are installed.[1] For the related technique of leaving the trunk's starboard side off late in the build to ease fitting the tiller baffle and other internal pieces, see [vent box](/components/vent-box.md#access-to-the-vent-box-a-removable-side-panel-versus-gluing-it-in-place).

## Fitting the cabin sides, and the rail the hatch depends on

The curved plywood deck pieces are first trimmed to accept the **window side frames**. Because there is a step in the piece, a **Dremel** is the tool for shaping it — a chisel will break chunks out of plywood, whereas cutting downward with the Dremel and then carefully chiselling out the small pieces comes out clean.[1] The side frames are then clamped in place and the **top rails checked for parallel**; if they are out, the correction is made by cutting further into the curved piece, not by forcing the frame.[1] This check is the reason the whole stage exists: the top rails are the groove the **hatch** slides in, so a cabin that closes up out of parallel gives a hatch that binds. See [hatch — keeping the top rails parallel](/components/hatch.md#keeping-the-top-rails-parallel).

With the fit right, the side panels are re-clamped, **pilot holes drilled for every nail** — with masking tape wrapped round the drill bit as a depth stop so the bit cannot go through the wood — and the panel glued in place with epoxy thickened with **Cab-O-Sil**. Excess is wiped up and the wipe-up itself used to fill any voids, and the joint left to cure for a **minimum of 24 hours**.[1]

Where the side frame's top and bottom rails meet the laminated front and rear deck-framing pieces, the pieces simply **butt together and are faired in with epoxy**; the fit becomes obvious once the frames are offered up rather than worked out from the drawing.[2] The two dimensioned corner-post angles that set how the end windows seat are covered at [deck and cabin beams — corner post angles on sheet 10](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#corner-post-angles-on-sheet-10-the-30-degree-and-14-degree-window-seating-detail).

## Setting the roof beams

Once the sides are glued the panels are rigid, and any remaining error in the top rails is taken out with a **wedge or a bar clamp** before the beams go on — the last moment at which it can be corrected.[1]

The three beams are stacked one on top of another on a level surface so their **top curves can be matched**; if they do not agree, the centre beam is turned over until they do. A common **centreline** is marked across all three at once, and each beam marked port or starboard.[1] The aft beam is then set in position on top of the rail and the bevel taken directly off the boat: a line is drawn **3/8 in on the roof's outer edge** onto the beam and on the inner edge onto the roof beams, and the two connected — that line is both the bevel to cut and the angle at which the beam matches into the top rail. A series of **slots cut with a back saw, chisel and file** brings the beam flush with the rail, and it is glued with a **small brass brad at each end** to hold it until the glue dries.[1]

The beams themselves — how many there are, their dimensions, their laminating jig, and the open question of whether the roof carries a standing load — are treated at [deck and cabin beams](/construction/deck-and-cabin-beams.md#cabin-top-beam-count-three-versus-four-and-whether-the-roof-takes-a-standing-load).

## The plywood roof, and why it overhangs the ends but not the sides

**Templates for the front and rear windows are made before the roof goes on**, since both are curved and the roof will overhang their top edges once fitted.[1]

The roof need not be cut from a single sheet with a large hole taken out of it; it can be **scarphed** from several pieces to save plywood, one documented roof being made in three pieces with three scarphs and six ground surfaces. The scarph is cut in place on the boat for the large forward panel and off the boat on a table for the added pieces — an approach that also serves for repairs, where the ply cannot be stacked to control the slope in the usual way. For 1/4 in plywood at an eight-to-one slope the scarph is two inches long, ground with a disc grinder and a 24–36 grit floor sanding disc worked across the width while moving up or down the slope, repeating until the plywood's own layers appear equal in width.[1] See [scarf joints](/construction/scarf-joints.md) for the tree's fuller treatment of scarphing.

The roof's edges are treated differently at the ends and the sides, and for a specific reason: to prevent rain or dew standing on the **Lexan edge**, the roof panel is run **slightly over the front and aft windows** but left **flush with the sides**. The **hatch slide rails** are what overhang the sides.[1]

## Sheathing and fairing the finished box

The window overhang is radiused front and rear, and the plywood radiused on both sides, before glassing. **Three pieces of cloth** can be used on the roof, overlapped and cut through both layers in the same way as the deck. The cloth is worked with particular care to **seal every plywood edge** from water, and on the sides is run down to the edge of the wood window frame. Once it has gelled, putty mixed with **white microspheres** fairs the entire cabin structure.[3] See [fibreglassing technique](/construction/fiberglassing-technique.md) and [puttying and fairing](/construction/puttying-and-fairing.md).

## Variants builders have tried, or declined

The stock cabin has attracted more proposed alternatives than most parts of the boat, and almost all of them run into the same objection — that the enclosure is what makes the boat what it is.

- **A beamless roof.** One build dispensed with the plans' roof beams entirely: a roof laminated from **two layers of 3.5 mm plywood** resting on the 1/2 in ply cabin sides and stitch-and-glued into place, with an external beam at the forward hatch coaming and a **bent aluminium-tube truss** carrying the roof's downward load and side thrust in place of internal beams. See [Noli](/boats/noli.md).[4]
- **Flush cabin sides.** Fairing the cabin sides out flush with the hull sides has been proposed repeatedly for interior room and outboard buoyancy, and answered each time with the stock stepped-in side deck's own purpose — it is where the yuloh and lowered spars stow — plus added windage, harder movement forward, and no real interior gain unless the side bins go too. See [flush cabin sides](/design/ideas/flush-cabin-sides-idea.md).
- **A slot top.** Considered for one UK build and weighed against the risk that a knocked-down Paradox with the slot open might not float as designed. See [hatch — a slot-top cabin: the self-righting trade-off](/components/hatch.md#a-slot-top-cabin-the-self-righting-trade-off).
- **A lift-off roof and hatch**, and a **canvas top** in its place — both proposed, neither built, and the canvas version rejected on the ground that it would considerably reduce survivability in a knockdown. See [hatch — a removable roof and hatch, proposed but not built](/components/hatch.md#a-removable-roof-and-hatch-proposed-but-not-built).
- **Separate ports for the continuous side window.** Built on at least one boat as a personal touch, with no loss of visibility reported. See [windows](/components/windows.md#departures-builders-have-made).
- **Raising the cabin for extra headroom.** The boom-strop, tack-strut, and mast-length consequences of raising any part of the cabin top, and a real 50 mm built example. See [crew size and fit — a 2014 exchange](/operations/crew-size-and-fit.md#a-2014-exchange-the-boom-mast-and-hatch-consequences-of-raising-the-cabin).

[22] [Paradox builders mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md) message 8628 (bourbeau / "drafbourbeau" / André-François Bourbeau, `paradoxbuilders`, 2015-09-01) — "Re: Trunk Cabin vs Raised Deck Cabin": full text and citation also at [people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md#citations) and [people/michael-beebe.md](/people/michael-beebe.md#citations); sailing his own Paradox in Texas alongside a fellow owner's, whose Paradox was built with side decks pushed out to the sheer line and a longer tiller for sitting sideways, notes that owner missed the side decks' use for stowing the yuloh and mast and found it harder to climb on and off the cabin top; rather than reverting the modification, that owner sold the boat and built a second one with the cabin to plan — "a good indication that the widening modification was not as successful as hoped for," and that "you need a real good reason to go to a wider cabin"; never heard the owner complain about visibility, even sailing six days together in cold rain. Signed "AF."
