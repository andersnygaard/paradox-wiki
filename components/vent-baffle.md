# Vent baffle

The baffle inside the Paradox's ventilation trunk keeps spray and green water out of the cabin while still admitting air.

## The transom baffle: sharing one opening between the tiller and the air

A single baffle, fitted to the inside of the transom around the tiller opening, does two jobs at once: it stops water entering the hull through that opening, and it acts as a ventilator, letting air pass the same way.[1] The tiller itself passes through this baffle on its way to the steering line loop inside the cabin.[2] The plans call for the baffle to be cut from the same 6 mm sheet of plywood used for the cabin roof and the hatch cover.[3]

## A rubber or vinyl baffle for genuinely rough water

The built-in baffle is judged adequate for moderate conditions, but not necessarily for the worst of them: if the boat were ever laid ahull in a gale, with the rudder raised clear of the water, "the built-in baffle may not be adequate for keeping water out," and the tiller port would need a waterproof gasket sealing it instead.[4] The recommended precaution, made before going to sea rather than after a problem shows up, is to fit an added rubber or vinyl baffle over the tiller port for rough-water sailing.[5]

## Two 2011 DIY tiller-port baffle designs

Years before the account above, two independent DIY fixes for water entering through the tiller/rudder-hole opening were already in service, both aimed at deflecting the flow rather than sealing it outright. One boat's own fix is a flexible boot made from black EPDM roofing rubber, tie-wrapped around the rudder and clamped to the hull; it does not need to be fully watertight, since it works by deflecting the water away rather than stopping it, in place of the "shot of water" that otherwise enters and explodes against the baffle when it hits — "kind of like a boot you would see on a sports car shifter."[6]

A simpler alternative fits a loose disc of rubber on the tiller itself, aft of the transom. Since the tiller opening doubles as part of the ventilation path together with the vent trunk, the disc is deliberately left loose: in normal conditions it does not close off the hole at all, letting air pass freely, and only presses up against the transom to act as a seal once a wave slaps the stern.[7][8] It does not need to be watertight either — "a bit like the mushroom valve in your inflatable air tubes, but a much looser fit" — it only needs to slow the water enough that it doesn't reach the top of the baffle.[9]

A further refinement, proposed but not built, would add a lip along the top edge of the baffle itself, to stop water shooting off the top of the baffle and soaking the whole stern compartment.[10]

## A 2011 warm-water ventilation question, and a six-year dry record

Asked how the baffle and vent trunk would cope sailing in warmer waters than most, the reply drew on the conditions the design was actually built for: the boat was designed to be sailed off the Florida coast, so warm-water sailing was already part of the original brief, and an air scoop could easily be fitted at the deck opening for extra airflow if wanted. Across six years of sailing, water has come in this way only once — about a mug full — and that was while beached, with waves breaking against the transom. The single most effective fix for keeping the interior dry has nothing to do with the baffle itself: a super-absorbent door-mat placed just inside the hatch catches the water, sand, and mud a sailor brings aboard on their feet at every beach landing, right at the source. Built and shut as designed, the hatch keeps out all water; left even slightly open, some may find its way in under the front edge.[11]
