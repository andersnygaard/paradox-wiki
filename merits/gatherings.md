# Apalachicola Bay, the Iowa messabout, a DCA week at Cobnor, and the Havasu Pocket Cruisers Convention

Four gathering points, of very different scale, round out the Paradox community's meetup record: a solo-scouted Apalachicola Bay, an unconnected Iowa messabout, a Dinghy Cruising Association week at Cobnor, and the much larger annual Havasu Pocket Cruisers Convention.

## Apalachicola Bay: a proposed meeting ground, scouted solo (2002)

**Apalachicola Bay**, with campgrounds on St. George Island, was an early proposed second gathering ground for Paradox owners — "a wonderful place to camp and sail," with each owner able to inspect and sail the other's boat and swap ideas, and open to other list members and to a more central location if one made better sense. Apalachicola is roughly an 8-hour drive from Memphis.[1]

Rather than wait for a scheduled group date, one owner made a solo scouting trip of the bay in February 2002, launching "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" from Carrabelle Beach on the eastern side of St George Sound (about 30 miles long) and anchoring near St George Island State Park, with an open invitation for anyone in the area to meet up over the weekend — the first record of a Paradox owner actually acting on a proposed group cruise, even though it played out as an individual scouting trip with an open invitation rather than the originally proposed joint outing. Retrieval at low tide there proved uncertain enough to shape later trailer designs (see [trailering — ground clearance for Gulf Coast ramps](/operations/trailering.md#ground-clearance-for-gulf-coast-ramps)).[2]

## The Iowa messabout: a third, unconnected gathering point (proposed 2002)

A general small-boat-builder gathering, not Paradox-specific, was proposed for **Lake Okoboji, northwest Iowa**, for the summer of 2003 — a third, geographically distinct small-boat gathering point alongside Cedar Key and Apalachicola Bay, with no recorded connection to either.[3]

## A DCA week at Cobnor, Chichester Harbour (August 2007)

Two Paradoxes, "Faith" and a second boat, sailed and anchored together for a week at a **Dinghy Cruising Association** meeting at Cobnor, Chichester Harbour, alongside a fleet of DCA dinghies — matching the dinghy fleet's performance on a reach and doing marginally better to windward, and riding out a near-gale at anchor on good holding ground. "Faith"'s own day-by-day log of the same week, including a man-overboard incident, is at [boats/faith.md](/boats/faith.md#the-cobnor-week-a-dinghy-cruising-association-meeting-and-falling-overboard).[4][5]

## A proposed all-Paradox gathering (2010)

A newer builder, working through his own hypothetical future cruising plans — trailering to the Gulf coast and on to the Bahamas, or a longer-shot solo passage to Hawaii once the boat was finished — floated the idea of a large, dedicated Paradox gathering somewhere, hoping for 2010; a reply wondered, half in jest, whether he might already be in Hawaii by then. See [people/rodney-cannon.md — a distant cruising dream, and a proposed Paradox gathering](/people/rodney-cannon.md) for the fuller exchange.[6][7][8]

A practical wrinkle in staging any such gathering in the Bahamas surfaced the following month: getting builders from outside the mainland US ("the other side of the Lake") and their own boats to the location at all, absent sponsorship from a well-funded outside backer. One proposed workaround was a **time-share/accommodation-sharing scheme** — whoever had a finished boat ready by the gathering's date offering to share it, with camping or overnight accommodation arranged jointly, so a builder unable to bring a boat of his own could still fly in, sail, and pick up hands-on tuning insight from owners who had already sorted their boats out.[9]

## The Havasu Pocket Cruisers Convention (Lake Havasu City, Arizona)

An annual trailer-sailer gathering held each February at **Lake Havasu City, Arizona**, founded by the owner of the Paradox "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)" (see [Sean Mulligan](/people/sean-mulligan.md)), drew roughly **70 boats** in 2010. By August that year, with the 2011 edition (**14–21 February**) still six months off, **40 boats and crews from seven Western US states and Canada** had already signed up, toward a stated goal of **100 or more**.[10] By late October, three months out, registration had grown to **102 boats and crews from 14 US states and 2 countries**, averaging 535 miles of driving distance and spanning 39 different boat models — a Montgomery 17 the most common, at 17 boats.[11]

## Three UK Paradoxes together at Poole Harbour (July 2013)

A Dinghy Cruising Association rally brought all three of the UK's then-existing Paradoxes to **Poole Harbour** on the same July 2013 weekend — [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md), [Faith](/boats/faith.md) (by then owned by Jim Hall), and [JoHanna](/boats/johanna.md) — reported at the time as probably the first time three Paradoxes had ever gathered together in one place, sailed in sunshine and a light wind.[12][13]

## Sail Oklahoma 2014: a joint builder/owner program on "Scout"

**Sail Oklahoma**, an annual messabout organised by **Jackie and Mike Monies**, became in **October 2014** the one event on record where a Paradox's builder and her sailor presented her together. Announcing it that August, the organisers said **[Sean Mulligan](/people/sean-mulligan.md) and [Lezlie Henson](/people/lezlie-henson.md) would give a joint program** on "[Scout](/boats/scout.md)" — her build, her finishing, and her sailing — across the **8–13 October** event: "We are excited to have the two of them together and this will be a great program."[14] Henson had built the hull as "Tardis" and sold her almost complete without ever sailing her; Mulligan finished her, renamed her, and by then had a full season and a [Texas 200](/merits/texas-200.md) behind him, so the two halves of the boat's story had never before been told from one platform.

The same event put a second Layden design and the designer himself in the same place: **[Chris Curtis](/people/chris-curtis.md)** — who would buy "Scout" from Mulligan the following March — showed his **Enigma 460** at Sail Oklahoma 2014, where **[Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)** inspected her in depth and "quietly gave me the OK, saying it was well built."[15]

## A proposed 12-hour distance race (February 2020)

A distance challenge was floated on the mailing list for **29 February 2020**: over a single 12-hour window in US Central time, whoever covered the most distance in a stock-length Paradox, sailed or yulohed with no motor and no towing, single-handed throughout, would win a **$600 prize**. Entry was free and open to anyone anywhere, including a sailor with access to nothing more than a lake, and no modifications were allowed — the declared purpose being to let a builder with only fresh water, or without deep pockets, compete on equal terms.[16] **[Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md)**, sailor of **[Thor](/boats/thor.md)**, called it a great idea and wished the entrants luck.[17]

Two questions from **[John Starkie](/people/john-starkie.md)** — who would put up the prize, and how the claims would actually be verified — drew a fuller answer: the proposer would personally fund it, planning to send the money to a trusted member of the group a week ahead of the race so that person could handle any payouts or disputes, and judged frequent updates from each boat during the twelve hours essential to keep it honest and entertaining; he also wanted the format usable again the following year, on a large local lake in a stock Paradox, and expected some electronic tracking device would answer the verification question for a future running.[18][19] **[Frederic Tallman](/people/frederic-tallman.md)** called the funding offer "very generous" and said he was looking forward to seeing the race happen,[20] and **[Chris Curtis](/people/chris-curtis.md)** — an owner who had put his own two Layden boats against each other more than once (see [matt-layden/enigma.md](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-2020-head-to-head-an-owners-own-enigma-460-against-his-own-paradox)) — said he would love to see Paradoxes racing against each other, calling it "great fun."[21] The proposer later reiterated the funding offer, proposing to place a portion of the prize with one of the group's better-known members to show good faith.[22] Verification drew two further, free alternatives to the electronic tracker already hoped for: **['Mattsplatt'](/matt-layden/biography.md#the-mattsplatt-identity)** — the group's founder, a different Matt from the designer — floated a satellite SPOT-type tracker while doubting most owners would actually own one, and judged the prize money itself could wait so long as the idea went ahead, hoping to see how big a fleet could be gathered "for next Feb."[23] Free options followed: sharing a live position through Google Maps,[24] and Apple's Find Friends — already used by **Peter Bennett**, owner of **[Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md)**, to track his own daughters while they travelled.[25] The proposer also asked whether any of the phone apps built for boat racing, or a tracking website he had found, `kwindoo.com`, could let entrants sailing from different places race each other in real time;[26] a free Android app built for exactly that purpose, SailFreeGPS, followed the next morning.[27] Thanking the reply, the proposer named the harder problem any such app would still have to clear: guarding against cheating at all, the same obstacle an earlier race attempt of his own had run into; short of building one from scratch, he floated hiding an anti-cheating check inside a phone app instead — comparing a boat's own recorded movement against the wind conditions at the time, watching for the vibration a motor would leave or the sudden movement a tow would cause — with the same app relaying results live so onlookers could follow who was winning.[28]

The proposed "any body of water" rule drew a rules critique of its own. **[Rick Langer](/people/rick-langer.md)** called the idea a great one, then argued that local conditions could hand an unfair edge regardless of a sailor's skill: a favourable-current lap of Manhattan Island's three tidal rivers — a route he had paddled himself, 28 miles, with currents exceeding 6 knots — or a wide bay with a steady beam-reaching breeze would each out-perform a landlocked lake sailor with neither current nor fetch to exploit.[29] **[Cory Sellars](/people/cory-sellars.md)**, sailing **"[Prescott Eagle](/boats/prescott-eagle.md)"** on Ontario's Great Lakes, confirmed a plainer version of the same problem: his own water stayed frozen into March or April, ruling out a 29 February start regardless of the rules.[30] **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)**, writing from Québec's Saguenay region, found the same problem in three feet of ice and suggested simply moving the race to a warmer month; more fundamentally, he doubted a single global race day could ever be fair, since good or bad weather on the day — not sailing skill — would decide the winner, and proposed instead letting each entrant choose any day within a one-month window; he also argued a fair course needed a return leg upwind as well as down, reckoning a downwind-only twelve hours would need more than 120 km of open water where a there-and-back course could be sailed on a lake as short as 50 km, a size not every entrant had to hand.[31] The proposer, open to the point, was willing to run the event over two or three separate days at different times of year rather than insist everyone start together, so long as he could still feel he had a fair chance himself, sailing a stock boat on his own local Texas lake.[32] **[Rob Kellock](/people/rob-kellock.md)** doubted that any software could ever police cheating at all, given how many variables would need locking down, and pressed the fairness question further still: nobody had yet defined what counted as a **stock Paradox**; a boat fitted with self-steering would hold a real edge over a full day at the tiller; a fixed US start time forced an entrant on the other side of the world to either start in the middle of their own night or wait twelve hours to judge their own chances before deciding whether to bother entering at all; and salt water, fresh water, and a triangular course for the upwind leg would all still need settling besides.[33]

By 15 February, with no confirmation a 2020 running was actually coming together, the proposer said he was prepared to "pull the plug on 2020 and set the date at 2021," while still hoping to see a race develop that he himself, with his limited time and lake-only access, could enter; **Curtis Clark**, owner of **[Raffles](/boats/raffles.md)** — a different owner from [Chris Curtis](/people/chris-curtis.md), above — replied that he had probably been going to enter anyway, since Arizona's mild February weather meant he would not have had to "brave any elements."[34] A further sentence from the same evening, recovered the same way when Curtis Clark quoted it back, showed the proposer going further still: if the details could be worked out, he would put up $3,000 in prize money, split between the first three finishers, in place of the single $600 winner first proposed, prompting Curtis Clark to ask whether they should now plan for 29 February 2021.[35] The proposer confirmed a preference for the last day of February 2021, but said he was loosening the fixed date to draw more interest, since some entrants' water stays frozen through the winter, without yet settling on one date that would suit everyone.[36]

Kellock's critique came with personal advice too: rather than see the prize money donated, he urged spending it instead on finishing the boat and testing her offshore in Florida rather than on a lake, since lake training would never prepare anyone for a Bahamas crossing.[33] Taking the point, the proposer floated a different kind of prize instead — rewarding **the most adventurous Paradox** of 2021, which by his own reckoning would have gone that year to Kellock himself — only to be told plainly that rewarding "adventurous" would just encourage somebody to do something dangerous for the money, and that anyone wanting Paradoxes racing together should simply finish a boat and go sailing with others.[37][38] Within the same exchange the whole funding offer came off the table: too many variables stood in the way of a fair contest, and the money had only ever been meant to see Paradoxes race for its own sake, not to be forced on anyone; the proposer said he would instead spend the time building his own sailing knowledge.[39]

Writing separately on the parent thread, **[André-François Bourbeau](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md)** judged the group honest enough that cheating was no real concern of his, restated his own fairness fix in fuller form — a self-chosen start day within a month-long window sailed on a triangular course, since a stronger wind speeds the downwind leg by exactly as much as it slows the upwind one — and offered his own answer to what counts as a stock Paradox: a standard fourteen-footer carrying identical weight, passenger included, a definition he could test directly against his own two Paradoxes, which sail at exactly the same speed against each other. He judged "most adventurous" no easier to define fairly, and no less likely to reward unnecessary risk, than the distance race it replaced, and offered a simpler alternative instead: the greatest distance covered while camping aboard, non-stop.[40] The idea of comparing boats outlived the prize: sailing a taller, higher-aspect sprit rig, Kellock judged his own boat noticeably less steady unattended than the stock lug rig he had started on, and proposed settling the question directly rather than by argument — an identical triangular course, sailed both ways, on a lake to avoid currents, with wind speed capped as in the America's Cup so every boat flew full sail, each entrant logging a GPS track and a wind-speed reading at every mark for the group to compare on elapsed time, boat speed against wind speed, and tacking angle (see [self-steering — rig height and unattended course-holding](/operations/self-steering.md#rig-height-and-unattended-course-holding) for the sail-stability comparison that prompted it).[41] The course itself followed within the day, posted as a GPX file exported from a chart plotter alongside the same route as a PDF chart and an Excel spreadsheet: a closed loop of three turning points near his own home waters, each leg close to a mile long, matching the proposed equilateral triangle almost exactly.[42] No sailed result from that course, or from the distance race itself, in 2020 or in 2021, is recorded anywhere in this archive as having actually taken place.
