# A Paradox and the Race to Alaska

The **Race to Alaska (R2AK)** is a roughly 750-mile, entirely **engine-free** small-craft race from **Port Townsend, Washington** to **Ketchikan, Alaska**, run up the exposed inside waters of the British Columbia and southeast Alaska coast — a hard test of any boat driven only by sail and human power. A [Paradox](/paradox.md) has two known connections to it, a decade apart: a 2015 mailing-list assessment of the design's chances, prompted by that year's inaugural race, and an actual entry eleven years later — registered, rigged and listed as the smallest boat in the fleet, but recorded by the race as never having started.

## A 2015 mid-race assessment: how would Paradox have fared?

During the actual **inaugural 2015 Race to Alaska**, itself run engine-free over the same course, list traffic weighed how a Paradox would have fared against the fleet still out on the water. One assessment held Paradox "way outclassed" by the leaders — she could finish the course, but much more slowly, since few boats do well going to windward in near-gale winds over the several hundred miles the course demanded.[2] A fuller reply set the question against the design's own résumé: Paradox is built to be sailed continuously — the trait that had already won her the [Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md#the-2003-everglades-challenge-matt-laydens-outright-win) — and even the fleet's remaining boats, much lighter than a Paradox, were themselves only averaging about 3 knots despite short early bursts into the teens; a Paradox sailed near-continuously, stopping only for a few hours' rest every couple of days, was judged capable of windward work up to about 25 knots, a capability judged necessary regardless, given how often New Zealand itself sees wind that strong.[3] No Paradox is recorded as having actually entered the 2015 race; the exchange was speculation prompted by that year's tracker.

## The 2026 entry: Team Forget Me Knot

The 2026 Race to Alaska fleet included, for the first time on record, an actual Paradox entry: **Team Forget Me Knot**, sailed by **[Jolyn Barley](/people/jolyn-barley.md)** of **Comox, British Columbia**, registered on 9 April 2026 and listed as the **smallest boat in the race at thirteen feet**, among a field otherwise made up of much larger trimarans, monohulls, and catamarans up to thirty-five feet.[4][5][1] The race put the contrast at the head of its own fleet statistics — "**Smallest boat: 13 feet. Team Forget Me Knot … sailing a Paradox. What's a Paradox? Google it**" — in a fleet of 16 trimarans, 37 monohulls, 2 catamarans, an outrigger sailing canoe, 4 kayaks, 4 rowboats, a paddleboard and a pedal craft, of whom 14 sailed alone.[5]

The entry is a natural fit for the design's premise — a fully enclosed micro-cruiser propelled by its lug rig and a [yuloh](/components/yuloh.md), needing no motor by the rules or by habit — and a notable outing for it, pitting a boat built for shoal Everglades and coastal cruising against open, cold, tidal northern water. That a Paradox was on the entry list at all extends the design's real-world résumé beyond the [Texas 200](/merits/texas-200.md) and [Everglades Challenge](/merits/everglades-challenge.md) to one of the toughest engine-free races in North America.

## The boat as entered: thirteen feet ten, with a pedal drive *and* a yuloh

The race's registration records the boat as a **Paradox of 13 ft 10 in**, crewed by one, boat number 66 in the printed fleet list — the design's full length, where the team page and the fleet-statistics post both round it to thirteen feet.[6][4] Its declared human propulsion was "**Hobie Mirage Drive and Yuloh**" — both, together.[4]

That pairing is the unusual part. A Paradox carries a yuloh as standard, and the design's builders had examined a **Hobie Mirage Drive** for it twice, in December 2006 and again in October 2008, and rejected it both times: the drive unit would block the flat 6 ft × 3 ft 6 in cabin floor, its trunk would have to rise above the waterline and intrude through the sole, and a fin drive was judged ineffective on a hull as heavy as a Paradox — the yuloh winning on lever arm, on the standing sculler's view, and on price (see [pedal power and why a Mirage Drive does not suit Paradox](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#pedal-power-and-why-a-mirage-drive-does-not-suit-paradox)). The 2026 entry is the only Paradox on record declared with a pedal drive fitted **alongside** the yuloh rather than instead of it, and it was declared for the one race where propulsion in a calm decides places.[4]

## What the race recorded

Preparation was still under way nine days before the start: local press on 5 June 2026 placed Forget me Knot, "from the Comox Valley," among three Vancouver Island teams "in their last week of preparation" for a race leaving Port Townsend on 14 June, in what the paper called the largest field in the race's history at around seventy-five boats registered.[10]

The race's official Stage One results then record the entry as **DNS — did not start**: the only such entry in a sheet of seventy-one rows, of which sixty-seven finished the Victoria qualifier and three retired, and Team Forget Me Knot appears nowhere in the Stage Two results in any category.[7] The race gives no reason. A Paradox was entered, measured, numbered and prepared for the course, and there is no record of her on it.[7]

## The attempt before it: 2024, on a Hobie Tandem Island

The Paradox entry was a **second** attempt under the same team name. Barley had entered the 2024 race solo on an **18 ft 6 in Hobie Tandem Island**, a pedal-driven sailing trimaran, and withdrew partway north after leaks in that boat's hull and both amas and other equipment failures.[8] The withdrawal statement the race published on 19 June 2024 closed, "you can bet I'll be back to complete it one day … I will be just that much more prepared for next time!" — and the Paradox entry two years later was that return.[9]
