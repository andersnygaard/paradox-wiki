# Self-righting and capsize recovery

[Paradox](/paradox.md)'s narrow beam and low, secured ballast (see [beam, freeboard, and stability](/design/beam-and-stability.md) and [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)) combine to give her an unusually high angle of vanishing stability: multiple independent sources put her self-righting point at 160° to 165° of heel, and the design is intended to recover from a full knockdown without any action from the crew.

## Angle of vanishing stability: 160 and 165 degrees, from independent sources

An early figure, dated 1996, put Paradox's self-righting point at about **120 degrees** of heel — a rough estimate, not a settled value.[1] Later figures, expressed in proper naval-architecture terms, run substantially higher. One source gives an **Angle of Vanishing Stability (AVS)** — the heel angle at which the upthrust of the centre of buoyancy and the downthrust of the centre of gravity come into line — of **160 degrees**, alongside an "above average" ballast ratio; on that account the boat self-rights via buoyancy from the coachroof combined with ballast strapped to the floor and the convex curve of both fore-and-aft decks, with wave motion itself helping to flip a capsized hull back upright, and the short, stout mast and low-aspect sail not expected to greatly impede the process.[2]

The designer's own figure, given in a published interview, is **165°** — "better than many modern offshore keel boats" — with the boat's heavily crowned deck making her unlikely to stay inverted (she could, if needed, be righted by hand like a capsized dinghy), and 2 inches of foam insulation providing positive buoyancy if she is ever holed.[3] The same 165° figure is independently corroborated years before that interview and from a different angle: a builder's recollection of having received "a graph, either with the plans or with Don [Elliott]'s articles" shows a correctly ballasted Paradox self-rights from 165 degrees of heel — the earliest record of the figure appearing on a builder's own paperwork (the plans themselves, or the construction articles) rather than only in the later interview, strengthening its provenance as the designer's own stated claim.[4]

Independent naval-architecture software offers a third, technical confirmation: a Paradox modeled from the plans in "Nautilus" boat-design software, with the designer's own stability figures run through it, checks out at **100%** — on the assumption that the boat's Vertical Center of Gravity is as low as believed, roughly 120 mm above the design waterline. The designer likely did not use a computer for the full stability calculation himself; a complete hand calculation of this kind is "a monstrous task" rarely undertaken before computers became available.[5]

A basic stability question — how stable Paradox is, how much ballast she carries, whether she is self-righting, and whether her non-self-draining, enclosed cockpit/cabin causes a problem — has a full answer pointing to two independent sources for the same figure.[6] A stability curve published separately (in the smallboat group) showed Paradox's **range of positive stability is about 160 degrees**, consistent with the 165° figure from the plans/construction articles and the later interview.[7]

A later question on the same topic drew the same figure once more, sourced this time explicitly to the plans' own stability curve: **165 degrees**.[43][44] The same exchange added a further mechanism behind that figure: the **mast itself floats when submerged**, adding to the boat's ultimate stability once knocked down — corroborated independently by a correspondent who had previously built a free-standing mast of his own, used the same engineering calculations to check Paradox's mast design, and judged it strong.[45]

## The mechanism, in an outside designer's words (2008)

The 165° figure also reached print from **outside the Paradox community**, in an Amateur Yacht Research Society journal article by [Sven Yrvind](/people/yrvind.md) — a designer who had sailed the boat himself and studied her for a decade. His value here is not the number, which was already on record, but the **explanation of where the range comes from** in a keel-less boat:

> Unlike the ordinary cruising boat, Paradox has **no heavy ballast keel** making her stability obvious. Matt uses less obvious means, like her **cruising load kept low** and her **flat bottom**, which, a bit like a multihull, **moves a lot of buoyancy to lee at small angles of heel**. At large angles of heel, Paradox's righting moment comes from a **good height-to-beam ratio and the buoyancy of her superstructure**. This shape gives Paradox positive stability at up to 165 degrees of heel — **better than most conventional keelboats**.[49]

That splits the curve in two: a **multihull-like form stability** from the flat bottom in the early degrees, handing over to **superstructure buoyancy and the tall, narrow section** once she is far over — the same coachroof-buoyancy mechanism the sources above describe,[2][3] here set in an explicit two-stage account and tied to the design's proportions rather than to its ballast. It also independently attests the comparative claim in near-identical terms to the designer's own ("better than many modern offshore keel boats"),[3] from someone with no stake in selling the design.

## Firsthand knockdown accounts

The fullest firsthand answer comes from "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)": the stability curve suggests she self-rights from 160°, and in practice she **pops back up quickly from 90°**, while standing on the rail and leaning out only pulled her over about **30°**. Her ballast at the time was 80 kg water plus 100 kg lead, plus anchor and heavy stores, all stowed under the floor. On the non-self-draining question: during normal sailing, most water aboard comes from wet feet; the only time significant water came aboard was when she was **knocked down to 90°** with the hatch open a crack, letting in about 2-3 litres. A Paradox's ability to be bailed from inside without opening the hatch is one of the design's "cunning" features — see [bailing and bilge pumps](/operations/bailing-and-bilge-pumps.md) for the pump installation and how much real use it gets.[8] A photograph on Little Jim's own website documents the boat's stability further.[9]

A plotted righting-moment-versus-heel-angle curve for Paradox (or possibly Little Cruiser) was independently recalled as being on file in the group's photo archive, in a folder labeled "Longyard" — the same stability data discussed above via the published curve and Little Jim's own field measurements.[10][11][12]

The same "wet feet, not flooding" record was restated again six years on, in the context of a comparison against Sven Yrvind's own far more expensive foam-composite hulls: across six years of sailing "Little Jim," the only time any real quantity of water came aboard was the same incident recalled above — the mast dipping into the water and about two litres coming below — with every other wetting no worse than an odd splash or spray needing no more than a sponge. Since Paradox is a coastal cruiser rather than an ocean crosser, the argument ran, she does not need Yrvind's costlier answer to the same problem: her stock plywood-and-foam-lined hull is already rugged enough not to sink even fully swamped. See [boats/little-jim.md — a repeated case against needing Yrvind's approach](/boats/little-jim.md#how-she-sails) and [boats/bris-paradox.md — a 2009 revisit](/boats/bris-paradox.md#a-2009-revisit-still-the-groups-standing-answer) for the fuller exchange this was part of.[13]

The archive's only **quantified field measurement** of a Paradox's righting moment also comes from Little Jim, first reported at the time rather than only recalled years later: at the **Beale Park Thames Boat Show**, part of a Dinghy Cruising Association display, friends hauled the masthead down — in cruising trim, sail hoisted, a crew member aboard at the helm position — until the water stood a couple of inches below the hatch, reading the load on a 25 lb spring balance, which ran straight off its scale — the pull was "well over 25 pounds." Releasing the masthead confirmed **a correctly ballasted Paradox definitely self-rights from 90 degrees**, with a good deal of rolling before finally settling upright.[14] Recalled again eleven years later, the same reading was worked into an implied righting moment of **well over 350 ft-lb**, "more than required to undo the wheel nuts on my car."[15] See [Little Jim — the Beale Park righting-moment measurement](/boats/little-jim.md#beale-park-thames-boat-show-and-a-righting-moment-measurement-june-2009) for the fuller contemporaneous account, and [Little Jim — a 2020 retrospective](/boats/little-jim.md#a-2020-retrospective-the-ayrs-talk) for how the same boat's ballast evolved over nearly two decades of ownership. Little Jim's owner also recorded twice being knocked flat with the sail in the water and recovering unaided both times, including once being knocked to 90° in broken surf and coming upright the moment the surf had passed (see [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) for that account in full).

By December 2007 the account had already travelled well outside the Paradox community. A poster on the messing-about.com design forum, in a general argument about rocker and beam in hull shape, cited "a trip by a UK Paradox sailor knocked over by a wave big enough to put his boat over 80 or 90 degrees" who took "a few liters" in through the barely-open roof hatch, and whose boat "was upright and sailing again even before he'd got his wits about him — quite a performance for a 14ft boat." The retelling is secondhand and names no source, so it cannot be pinned to a particular boat, but it matches the Little Jim record above closely enough to be the same incident reaching a general boat-design audience, and it is the earliest sign in this archive of Paradox's self-righting reputation circulating among designers with no connection to the design.[51]

A further account, at the milder end of the same scale, comes from "[Thor](/boats/thor.md)" in February 2016: knocked down in about 25 knots of wind, she righted herself with no crew action, then lay hove-to, drifting broadside through the chop until sail and tiller were adjusted to get under way again. See [boats/thor.md — a knockdown test and a ballast-placement debate](/boats/thor.md#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016) for the full account and the ballast-placement exchange it prompted.[52]

A second, more violent account comes from the same boat a year later: cutting a corner close to a cape in search of a favorable current, in very shallow water, a breaking wave running parallel to the hull rolled her quickly onto her side while a crew member stood in the hatchway; she righted herself immediately, pushed briefly toward shore with her masthead under water before her sail sprang back upright as the wave passed. The account credited the ballast and the design's ruggedness rather than any crew action, and singled out shallow water crossed by a strong current as exactly the condition that breeds this kind of sudden, changeable breaking wave. Full account: [boats/thor.md — a camping cruise in Spencer Gulf, and a freak-wave knockdown](/boats/thor.md#a-camping-cruise-in-spencer-gulf-and-a-freak-wave-knockdown-february-march-2017).[56] The incident prompted a fellow builder's hypothesis about whether the chine runners could, in a beam-sea knockdown specifically, amplify a roll rather than resist it — a mechanism distinct from, and worth reading against, the ordinary wind-heel case where the runners are understood to help stability; see [chine-runners/how-they-work.md — a further hypothesis](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#a-further-hypothesis-could-the-runners-amplify-a-beam-wave-roll-past-90-degrees-2017).[57]

The same freak wave drew a further reflection two days later, contrasting it with a second, open boat also owned — a Stevensons Pocket Cruiser, the same length as a Paradox but far wider — which would have swamped and ended up floating hull-up in the same situation, held at the surface rather than kept afloat right side up by flotation tanks under her cockpit seats, leaving her occupant clinging on amid further battering against rocks. "Thor" herself, by contrast, recovered immediately once the wave passed under the hull, judged capable of self-righting from "way way over 90 degrees to vertical."[58]

A third account from the same boat, given eighteen months later, adds a deliberately induced knockdown to the two accidental ones above: sailing close by the judges' boat during a local wooden-boat regatta, a sharp beam wave taken under full sail rolled her to almost right angles; she sat for a moment, then abruptly rolled upright again. The same message retold the 2017 Spencer Gulf knockdown above in fuller detail: hunting for a tide race inshore for extra speed at the end of the day, hatch open, an idling outboard masking the sound of the breaking wave until it was already underway — pushed sideways for five to ten seconds as the wave passed, water scooping in through the open hatch, before the sail emptied and she came up "with a vengeance," bruising her sailor against the hatch side; the anchor and a fishing rod went overboard in the process, and clearing the area afterward took motoring through four further large waves.[60] Pressed on the mechanism — whether the square chine or chine runner make a Paradox more apt to trip when struck abeam, and whether the sail skimming the surface rather than fully submerging, or the buoyant mast, kept her from rolling on over — the answer drew a distinction from the two-stage form-stability/superstructure-buoyancy mechanism above: during the active, wave-driven push itself the chine runners have little effect, since any hull shape gets shoved along by a genuinely powerful breaking wave regardless, and mast buoyancy is negligible next to the real reason she stayed recoverable — the ballast held firm in the bilges. Ballast advice sought years earlier from the Adelaide University Engineering Department (outboard rather than central, see [boats/thor.md — a knockdown test and a ballast-placement debate](/boats/thor.md#a-knockdown-test-and-a-ballast-placement-debate-february-2016)) was restated as the deciding factor in trusting the boat to always roll upright after a knockdown.[61][62]

## When self-righting could fail

Properly secured ballast makes the odds of a Paradox turning turtle and staying that way effectively nil — though that should not be overstated as an absolute guarantee.[16][17] Plenty of real-world conditions can keep any boat from righting itself again after a capsize: a Paradox's ability to sail in very thin water means an owner may take her into shallows where strong wind and breaking waves, combined with a soft, muddy bottom, could let the mast stick into the bottom and hold the hull inverted — the same "stick in the bottom" failure mode seen on capsized multihulls, normally recoverable only with a powerboat's help or a lucky wave, freed only by a boat's own crew doing something clever. A knockdown with halyards or sheets tangled in a bottom obstruction, combined with a leaking hatch and the resulting hydraulic pressure from a filling hull, is another scenario that could defeat an otherwise strongly self-righting design. In short: a design built to be markedly unstable upside-down and to self-right automatically still cannot fully rule out every combination of circumstances a sailor might eventually encounter.[18]

On the multihull-turtling and thin-water scenarios specifically, the risk is small in practice: a Paradox spills wind and settles rather than staying pinned down the way a multihull's mast can, and rigging tangled on an obstruction is something to cut free rather than fear, after which the hull would most likely come back up, swamped but bailable. Matt Layden's own boat has needed help from a crew member before — Matt himself was dislodged from the hull while helping her right after a knockdown, by grabbing the cabin-top rail — and the mast is built to slide out of its step under load in any case, so entanglement is less likely to hold the boat down than it might first appear. Fitting the foam flotation as designed is the real safeguard, so that if a capsize does happen the hull works with the crew toward self-righting rather than against them; a 72-hour overboard bag with a flare gun is further personal insurance. As a reminder that groundings and mishaps are not unique to a small, shoal-draft boat like Paradox: a grounded 30 ft powerboat once needed a full tide cycle and outside help to get off a sandbar it had gone hard aground on while surrounded by curious onlookers.[19]

### A sister design that did not come back up: an Enigma near-inversion (2016)

The clearest real-world illustration of the hatch caveat comes from outside the Paradox fleet, and from a professional designer sailing one of Matt Layden's other boats. In March 2016 **Graham Byrnes** of B&B Yacht Designs capsized a chine-runner **[Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md)** in a bad jibe in strong wind; she "came close to a complete inversion" and **did not right herself** — she was worked back onto her side by a companion outside the hull, then bailed with a cut-down water bottle.[50]

Three things about it are worth separating, because the episode is easy to read as contradicting everything above and does not.

- **She was not a Paradox.** Enigma is a light-displacement boat that "relies on her crew's weight as ballast," carrying bagged sand and gear rather than Paradox's fixed lead plus water ballast (see [Enigma — design particulars](/matt-layden/enigma.md#design-particulars)). The self-righting claims on this page are made for Paradox's own ballast arrangement.
- **The hatch was not closed.** Byrnes reports that "it would have been easy to swim out through the submerged hatch," and that an air pocket remained inside — an open aperture with the boat on her side and a crew member inside. Every self-righting figure on this page assumes a **closed** boat, which is why Matt Layden's own standing instruction to a later Enigma owner was never to sail her without her compartment hatches fitted (see [Enigma — a February 2013 flotation retrofit](/matt-layden/enigma.md#a-february-2013-flotation-retrofit-sealed-compartments-tea-jugs-and-secured-gear)).
- **It is a single event, reported by the person it happened to**, with no measurement of heel angle and no controlled repetition.

What it does establish is the practical shape of the failure the caveat above describes: not a boat that turns turtle and stays there, but one that stops short of inverting, floats stably on her side long enough for the crew to get out or stay put, and needs an outside push to come back. That is a materially different outcome from a Paradox's documented recovery from 90° unaided, and the difference is attributable to ballast and to a closed hull rather than to hull shape, which the two designs largely share.

An unrelated secondhand report from the same forum, nine years earlier, gives the Paradox side of the same comparison, and it is worth noting mainly because it shows the record travelling: a correspondent with no connection to the mailing list recounted reading of **a UK Paradox sailor knocked over by a wave far enough to put the boat "over 80 or 90 degrees,"** taking on "a few liters... through the barely-open roof hatch," and finding her **"upright and sailing again even before he'd got his wits about him."**[50] That matches the [Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md) knockdown account already on record here — 90° with the hatch open a crack, 2–3 litres aboard — closely enough to be the same event reaching an outside reader secondhand, and is treated as corroboration of its circulation rather than as an independent incident.

## Stability under crew load: standing at the stern

Stability at the extreme stern also came up directly, in the context of a raised hoop cage proposed to keep the mainsheet clear of a mounted outboard (see [rig and sail — a horse fitted to clear the outboard](/components/mainsheet-outboard-fouling.md#a-horse-fitted-to-clear-the-outboard-and-the-mainsheet-angle-physics-behind-it)). A safety concern about the cage design was raised: reentry over the stern past it would force a sailor to hop rather than step down, raising the body's center of gravity at an awkward moment — while a Paradox seemed unlikely to capsize as a result, the sailor doing the hopping might go over.[20] The direct answer: **stability is not a practical problem for a Paradox**, even standing at the stern — Little Jim had carried **three adults standing on the aft deck at once** without any trouble.[21] Pressed on whether that held true **underway**, and not simply at rest,[22] the three-adults test was confirmed to have been conducted with the boat under sail, though without being tied on and without the sail fully hoisted at the time — a lapse in safety practice worth correcting in future.[23]

## Design intent: the deck arch, and a stability chart on file

Matt Layden designed Paradox to be markedly unstable fully upside-down, not merely self-righting from a heel — the deck's pronounced arch follows directly from that design goal, so that the same weather capable of producing a full knockdown is also expected to supply enough wave and swell action to turn the ballasted hull back upright quickly, before more than a trivial amount of water gets below.[24] A note on drawing sheet 9 states plainly that the vents are intended to prevent significant water intrusion in any conditions, including a full 180-degree knockdown.[25] There are no turnbuckles anywhere in the design — the mast is freestanding — and the righting force comes from the ballast called out on the plans together with the buoyancy of the hull's foam insulation; no full 180-degree knockdown has been reported on the list.[25] A plotted stability curve across a range of heel angles, filed as `parastab.jpg`, is held in the group's Files section under "Study Plans."[26] A second, differently named upload of what appears to be the same diagram — "Paradox Stability Diagram," assumed but not confirmed to be Matt Layden's own work — was posted to the group's Files section in August 2018, again showing self-righting to about 165 degrees; posting it prompted a fresh version of the standing question this page already answers above, asking whether any owner has actually suffered a real knockdown, capsize, or roll-over and confirmed the boat self-righting in practice, and noting that a closed hatch would surely make a real difference to the outcome.[59] The question drew a direct answer within the same exchange — Thor's own deliberate-capsize and reef-knockdown accounts, and a pointer to Little Jim's already-documented case — set out under [firsthand knockdown accounts](#firsthand-knockdown-accounts) above. The trunk cabin's own profile has also been credited with helping the same self-righting behavior from a full inversion — see [pilothouse — trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin) for that design-rationale exchange.[46] A later exchange on the same thread proposed a competing safety consideration instead: a raised deck's own greater buoyancy aloft would cut the odds of a knockdown escalating into a full inversion in the first place, rather than only affecting how well the boat recovers once already there;[47] a separate view doubted that cabin shape decides the outcome at all, holding that a properly ballasted Paradox should not capsize outside a wave breaking directly over her, a case in which the cabin would matter little either way.[48] Neither claim was tested against the trunk-cabin credit above or against each other — see [pilothouse — trunk cabin versus a raised-deck cabin](/components/pilothouse.md#trunk-cabin-versus-a-raised-deck-cabin) for the fuller exchange, including its separate strand on cabin windows.

Whether a closed stern vent could form an airlock through the mast-drain path during a full inversion — and whether that would force water in through the cabin hatch instead, if the hatch were by then underwater — along with how the tiller-opening's position relative to the waterline bears on the outcome, is an open question with no answer on record.[24]

## A 2011 comparison: a heavier hanging load needed to heel "Odyssey" than "Little Jim"

A capsize test performed on the newly launched "[Odyssey](/boats/odyssey.md)" heeled her only to about 45 degrees under a 240-260 lb load hung from the masthead, and needed a further, separate test with her owner aboard to flip her to 90-100 degrees, past which she could not be forced down any further — a range consistent with the 160-165 degree self-righting figures established above.[40] Watching video of the test, one owner could not get his own boat anywhere near capsizing by the same mast-hanging method, and wondered whether the difference came down to the tester's own weight or to ballast; "Odyssey" was already carrying ballast at the plans' upper design level at launch, with more added since. See [boats/odyssey.md](/boats/odyssey.md#a-capsize-test-and-a-stability-comparison-against-little-jim) for the fuller account.[41]

## A 2011 restatement of the 2006 knockdown account

The same 2006 90-degree knockdown recounted above (hatch open enough to let a webbing strap through, only about two litres of water coming in, the harness-strap gap the only real leak point) was retold once more in 2011, unchanged, in answer to a prospective owner's question about whether an inversion would let water up through the closed sliding hatch; the design keeps out anything met in normal sailing, though the hatch is not fully watertight under real pressure. The same capsize-test video was linked again in support. See [hatch — a 2011 canvass for a possible commissioned rebuild](/components/hatch.md#a-2011-canvass-for-a-possible-commissioned-rebuild-hinged-versus-sliding-and-a-washboard-alternative) for the fuller exchange this restatement was part of.[42]

## Comparison to ordinary sharpies and dories, and the record for tiny ocean crossings

A prompt that certain keeled dories have crossed oceans "numerous times"[27] drew out a fuller distinction: Paradox's self-righting, self-rescuing design is not typical even among boats of a similar hull form. A sharpie — the family Paradox itself belongs to, a flatter-bottomed, less-flared derivative of the traditional dory (see [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md)) — is generally stable only to about 35 degrees of heel; enclosing that hull and sealing the crew inside, in the manner of a kayak, is what gives Paradox her self-righting and self-rescuing behavior past that point, including recovery from a full 180-degree knockdown by releasing the halyard from inside.[28] An open, unenclosed 26 ft Glen-L sharpie of similar proportions (beam-to-length ratio about 1/3) heels progressively to about 45 degrees before capsizing outright to 90 degrees and lying flat, and will not right itself again without several crew members standing on the keel or the raised beam and leaning their weight to counter it — the same limitation shared by most open dories and sharpies generally, and by older, pre-self-righting ships' lifeboats, whose crews could only cling to the hull and wait for rescue once capsized, a limitation modern self-righting lifeboats address at a cost that puts them out of most individual sailors' reach.[28]

Not every open hull fares identically. A dory built with a keel is reckoned capable of self-righting, at around a 1/4 beam-to-length ratio;[29] wider, flatter dory-type hulls built without a keel remain merely very stable rather than self-righting or self-rescuing — the San Francisco Pelican, a dory design with a sampan bow and an extremely wide 1/2 beam-to-length ratio, is one such boat, and has nonetheless sailed at least once from California to Hawaii in her 16 ft form.[28]

The extremes of what a tiny hull can survive at sea, self-righting or not, are catalogued on microcruising.com's own "Famous Small Boats" page, which lists ocean-crossing microcruisers down to **5.4 ft** — the smallest listed, which crossed from Newfoundland to England in 1993 — and records at least one builder planning an ocean crossing in a boat just **3 ft 11 in** long.[28][30] The exchange left one prospective builder, weighing Paradox against these alternatives for the first time, satisfied that his choice of the design for a first-time build had been the right one — see [Rodney Cannon](/people/rodney-cannon.md) for his fuller build record.[31]

The same self-righting, self-rescuing case was restated again in 2009: Paradox is capable of rolling completely over "like a Kayak" and righting herself — a knockdown likely severe enough to be "a scary experience," but one she would probably ride out without even losing her rig, provided the halyard is released quickly.[32] Reacting to the freshly reported Beale Park test above, the same comparison was drawn once more, this time naming Bolger's "Famous Micro" as a fellow kayak-like, theoretically self-righting and self-rescuing design — with a plain admission that nobody so far had found the nerve to actually test a full inversion and recovery firsthand.[33]

## A 2010 proposal for a bolt-on keel, declined

A proposal to fit Paradox with a small bolt-on fin keel — modelled on a vintage racing-sharpie design called "Missile," judged easy and inexpensive to build, and precedented by the San Francisco "Great Pelican"'s own external, cement-filled box keel, fitted for her California-to-Hilo passage — was floated as a way to make the boat self-righting and self-rescuing "just like Bolger's Micro."[34] It drew a direct question in reply — wasn't Paradox already designed to self-right and self-rescue? — alongside willingness to fit a removable version if one were actually needed.[35] The answer was flat: Paradox is already self-righting and self-rescuing, more so than the Micro, and a keel would only detract from her performance.[36] A follow-up attempt to read the stability chart directly estimated a turnover point around 60 degrees of heel, worsening past 70 degrees with loose interior contents, going by where the chart's Center-of-Gravity and Center-of-Buoyancy arrows appear to converge — offered with an open admission of possibly misreading it.[37] That reading was corrected in turn: the chart in fact shows self-righting to 165 degrees of heel, fifteen degrees short of fully inverted, restated alongside the same Beale Park righting-moment test recorded above, including its own YouTube video.[38] See [Little Jim — a further 2010 account of the same test](/boats/little-jim.md#beale-park-thames-boat-show-and-a-righting-moment-measurement-june-2009) for new detail the retelling drew out about the test itself and what stayed put aboard. A comparable test aboard Matt Layden's own [Enigma 460](/matt-layden/enigma.md#enigma-460-a-longer-plywood-cabin-variant-2007) corroborates the design family's self-righting margin from a second boat: her owner's own video shows her popping back upright from well over 100 degrees of heel.[39]

## A 2016 proposal: lead ballast, a bolt-on bulb keel, and a buoyant mast, all declined

A newcomer not yet sold on a keel-less boat's offshore stability proposed three modifications aimed at improving capsize recovery: lead added into the gap between the chine runner and the hull side, glassed over, to lengthen the righting-moment arm (though doubted to help a full 180-degree roll); a removable, bolt-on lead bulb keel of 10-12 kg near the aft end of the keel, for offshore passages only; and as buoyant a mast as practical, or a tube run through or alongside it fitted with an inflatable bladder at the masthead and a small compressed-air cylinder to refill it after repeated knockdowns.[53]

The reply was direct: the problem being solved does not exist. Ballasted according to the plans, Paradox self-rights from 165 degrees of heel — the same figure established above — and no realistic sailing condition will produce a capsize past that point; the worst case is a 90-degree knockdown, and the boat is up again before the crew can do anything about it. The point was illustrated with the same Beale Park masthead-load video already recorded above, its spring balance running off scale, alongside a second video of the same boat.[54] Persuaded, the newcomer dropped all three modifications and built to the stock design, ordering plans and Don Elliott's building manual rather than any of the proposed hardware.[55]
