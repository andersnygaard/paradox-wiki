# Beam, freeboard, and stability

Compared to most boats, sharpies are narrow for their length, and [Paradox](/paradox.md) follows the type: her beam is just under 48 inches, not counting the chine runners.[1] See [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md) for the fuller question of what else does and doesn't make her one. The first, intuitive assumption anyone makes about a hull this narrow is that a wider boat would be a better boat overall, and Paradox's whole beam-and-freeboard question is really an argument against that assumption — written out from first principles, checked against a named stability text, and afterward confirmed by an actual stability curve obtained from the boat's own designer.[1]

## The case against added beam

Some hulls add beam specifically to buy upright stability, but that beam is not free: it increases wetted surface, which in turn demands more sail power to drive the hull through the water, and once heeled over a wide hull presents more of its bottom area to wind and wave than a narrow one does.[1] The real cost shows up at the extreme end of the scale: a wide, low-freeboard hull, once knocked onto her beam ends, goes over quickly, and the same beam that resisted heeling in the first place then makes her very hard to right.[1] A narrow, high-sided boat may have to reduce sail earlier than a beamier boat of the same length, but she is both harder to put on her beam ends to begin with, and more likely to right herself, and sooner, if she does go over.[1]

## A design text on slim hulls and high freeboard

John Teale's *How to Design a Boat* is cited directly on both points. On beam: "in the last analysis and a total knock-down the slimmer vessel is likely to right herself, where as the beamier boat may remain capsized and upside down."[1] On freeboard: "high freeboard, however undesirable for windage and whether caused by high top sides or high deck work, does marvels for maximum righting moments and range of stability."[1]

## An outside restatement: the beam ceiling for a ballasted hull (2007)

The same argument was reached independently a decade later, and from outside the design's own community, when a newcomer on a boatbuilding forum put the intuitive objection in its bluntest form: that Matt Layden "seems to take a boat with marginal room and make it nigh well unbearable," and that a beamier hull would supply the buoyancy all that rocker is there for, add stability, and let the boat carry more sail.[7]

The answer, from a designer at an unrelated boatbuilding firm, states the constraint as a **ceiling** rather than a preference: in order to be self-righting when knocked down in a heavy sea, a ballasted micro-cruiser of this kind **cannot be too wide**, because **"there is a maximum width for a ballasted boat before it will have a second stability point... upside down."** Paradox is offered as the worked example — 1,410 lb displacement, and **"just about as tall as it is wide."**[8] That is the same conclusion the *Building Paradox* analysis reached from Teale [above](#a-design-text-on-slim-hulls-and-high-freeboard), arrived at without reference to it, and it names the failure mode the earlier account only implies: not that a beamy boat is *harder* to right, but that past a certain beam it acquires a genuine second point of stability inverted.

The reply also draws the consequence for the rest of the design, and it lands on the shallow draft. Past that beam ceiling, "it would make more sense to use a keel instead of adding more and more ballast to the bottom — **but then there goes your nice shallow draft** and coastal cruising ability, which is what Matt designed his micro cruisers for in the first place." Comfort can be traded for self-righting ability, but not without also trading away the shoal draft the whole type exists to have.[8] The [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) are explicitly held to be **independent of the beam question** — they resist leeway "with hardly any trade off in draft," and would work as well on a wider hull.[8]

A later contributor to the same thread proposed the opposite reading of the narrow beam — that it is deliberate precisely **so that the boat heels more**, immersing the lee chine deeper and putting the heeled hull to work as a lifting surface, with the high height-to-beam ratio then delivering the self-righting as a bonus rather than as the primary goal.[9] It is a hypothesis rather than a finding, and no source in this archive confirms Matt Layden reasoned that way round; but it is consistent with the measured heel optimum recorded at [chine runners — how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) and with the graduated heel account [below](#an-independent-trials-graduated-account-of-heel).

## Checked against the designer's own stability curve

The beam-and-freeboard analysis was worked out on paper first, and only afterward checked against the boat itself: a stability curve for Paradox was subsequently obtained from the designer, and it confirmed the analysis — comparing the two diagrams showed the boat to be actually more stable than expected going in.[1] The accompanying beam-comparison diagram survives only in a badly garbled reproduction; its own caption text cannot be transcribed reliably, so none of the diagram's figures are quotable here.[1]

## Two consequences already built into the design

Beam and freeboard are not the only place this stability logic shows up. Because Paradox is sailed from a closed cabin rather than an open cockpit, a knockdown on the beam ends does not throw the crew into the water the way a knockdown can in a boat with an open cockpit.[2] And because Paradox is a heavy boat for her length, she sits deep in the water, so the hull side itself counts as lateral resistance alongside the chine runners — daggerboards, centerboards, and fin keels are not the only surfaces that provide it.[3] See [chine runners — how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) for the fuller lateral-resistance picture, [hull scantlings](/design/hull-scantlings.md) for the plywood thicknesses behind that weight, and [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) for a real-world heel-angle account from inside the closed cabin.

A third consequence follows from the same flat, wide bottom shape: because the bottom is **completely flat across its width**, with rocker only fore and aft, Paradox dries out **level from side to side** when the tide leaves her.[4] That transverse flatness is a separate axis from the boat's documented fore-and-aft trim on the beach — deliberately beaching stern-first to angle the cabin sole so a sleeper's head lies higher than the feet (see [rudder and chine experiments — beaching stern-first](/components/rudder.md#beaching-stern-first-a-sleeping-comfort-technique-and-why-it-doesnt-damage-the-rudder)) is a fore-and-aft adjustment; sitting flat athwartships is a property of the hull shape itself, needing no positioning at all, and it is what keeps a dried-out night comfortable side-to-side regardless of how the bow-to-stern trim is managed.

## Size, handling, and the coastal-cruiser definition

Beam and freeboard sit inside a larger argument about size generally. Size matters mainly for handling: small enough to be trailered easily, launched and retrieved without fuss, and squeezed into a crowded anchorage; small enough to tie up at a dinghy dock rather than swim ashore or inflate and deflate a tender; small enough to be beachable and to go up narrow rivers and inlets; and small enough that the mast can be lowered by one person.[5]

A coastal cruiser is defined against an offshore cruiser on exactly this size question. An offshore boat crosses oceans and spends many days or weeks at sea; it cannot estimate the conditions it will meet, and once it is a few days out it cannot exit those conditions, so it has to be built to deal with the worst, and provisioned without any chance of restocking for long stretches.[5] A coastal cruiser sails along or within reach of a near shore; passages of 24 to 48 hours allow predictable weather forecasts, so the chance of being caught out in extreme weather can be safely reduced.[5] A coastal cruiser therefore does not need to be large — "in fact it's better to be small."[5] See [blue-water suitability](/design/blue-water-suitability.md) for the fuller coastal-vs-offshore classification debate this definition feeds into.

## Against "size determines seaworthiness"

A separate opinion holds that only large boats are safe, and that size alone determines seaworthiness — a claim disproven many times over by small boats that have demonstrated real seaworthiness. Yankee Girl, Tinkerbelle, Mermaid, and Chidiock Tichborne are named as proof, and the most extreme case on record is Sergio Testa's Acrohc Australis.[5] The honest concession runs the other way: a small boat is without question more uncomfortable, slower, and less weatherly than a larger one — but not, on that evidence, unsafe.[5]

Paradox's self-righting point has been documented at 160° and 165° of heel by several independent sources, including an early, lower 1996 estimate later superseded — consolidated at [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources).

## An independent trial's graduated account of heel

A sail trial aboard ["Little Jim"](/boats/little-jim.md) at Chew Valley Lake in October 2006 gives the archive's most explicit graduated account of how heel angle changes the boat's behavior, and, coming from an independent non-owner trial, corroborates owner testimony from outside.[6] Upright, with both chines in the water and little wind in the sail, she is at her least inspiring — though probably no slower than other short, heavy-displacement craft — and stability is high.[6] Heeling radically changes the underwater shape, unlike a round-bottomed hull, which is the mechanism behind everything that follows.[6] At about **20 degrees** of heel the windward chine clears the water, bringing a noticeable increase in speed and willingness to go to windward, and the **optimum sailing angle is between 20 and 35 degrees** of heel.[6] She must heel past **55 degrees** before water laps the top of the cabin windows — read as ample warning to free the sheet or roll in a reef well before that point.[6] The same trial records that she will self-right if capsized.[6] (The source prints these heel figures with a percent sign — "20%," "35%," "55%" — where a degree sign is evidently meant: other angles in the same article, such as 45° and 90°, are set correctly with "°," so the readings above are transcribed here as degrees.) See ["Paradox" — DCA Bulletin 193](/references/dca-paradox-gilchrist.md) for the full trial.
