# Blue-water suitability and comparison to Sven Yrvind's boats

[Paradox](/paradox.md) is officially classed as a coastal cruiser rather than a blue-water (open-ocean) boat, unlike the Swedish "Bris"/Sofia microcruisers[1] — a classification that firsthand cruising experience and a real build using the "Bris" construction method have since partly overturned. The comparison is the fullest recorded case for weighing Paradox's design philosophy and offshore capability against another microcruiser designer's body of work, rather than against Matt Layden's own other designs (Little Cruiser, Swamp Thing — see [/matt-layden/index.md](/matt-layden/index.md)).

## Structurally different approaches to offshore capability, not simply "better/worse"

The "Bris" boats are **technologically much more complex** than Matt Layden's, even though the two hulls have much in common **hydrodynamically**. The key difference is structural:

- The "Bris" boats are built from **high-tech composite fabric and resin**, not polyester/fiberglass, letting them **absorb impact damage** (striking a log, a sleeping whale, or a floating shipping container) and "bounce back" — a resilience not established for Layden's plywood boats.[2]
- The **sail-plan strategies** also differ: both tend to "under-power" their boats deliberately, but the "Bris" design breaks the sail area up into multiple smaller component sails rather than Layden's single simple rig (see [rig and sail](/components/rig/index.md)).[2]

## What conventional blue-water features Paradox and "Bris" both forgo

What separates a **coastal cruiser** from a **blue-water cruiser** in general comes down to a standard checklist for a "conventional" blue-water boat: heavier construction, fewer and smaller windows, a smaller cockpit, real sea berths, more water/fuel capacity, and generally more displacement, draft, and sail area than a coastal cruiser. None of it applies cleanly to unconventional microcruisers like Paradox and "Bris": **neither has a cockpit**, both are already built heavy for their scale, each has only **one real berth** (which may be too wide to serve as a proper sea berth), and total capacity is capped by the boat's small size regardless of design intent.[3]

The circumnavigation account *500 Days* illustrates the tradeoff at this scale: sailed in a boat just under **12 ft**, regularly spending **40-50 days at sea** without starving or running out of water — proof capacity is survivable at this size — but "a rather miserable experience much of the time": waves broke over the boat even in fair weather, forcing long stretches below with the hatch closed in poor ventilation, the single wide berth was uncomfortable to wedge into during storms, and the boat was shipwrecked and ran aground repeatedly (without harm, in an aluminum hull). By comparison, the **14 ft "Arrandir"** (a boat once featured on the microcruising forum) counts as "palatial."[3]

## Sven Yrvind's specific design choices, weighed against Matt Layden's

Beyond the general structural/impact-resilience comparison above, the concrete design choices separating the "Bris"/Sofia boats from Layden's are:

- **Divinycell foam-sandwich laminates** are judged superior to plywood laminates on strength, buoyancy, and flexibility together.
- A reluctant return to a **centerboard** rather than chine runners, for better performance in large offshore waves (contrast Paradox's [chine runners](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md)).
- The hulls are deliberately designed to be **highly unstable when inverted** — aided by a **very short mast** — so a capsized boat rights itself quickly rather than settling upside down.
- A **multi-sail plan** — a tops'l gaff rig plus jibs, with a spinnaker/ballooner-type sail for light air — spreads sail area across several smaller sails rather than Layden's single simple rig.
- The hulls are **double-ended**, adding buoyancy at the stern.

These designs are also **far more expensive and complicated to build**, a cost accepted as the price of maximum safety.[4]

An alternative reading treats these differences as matters of **materials philosophy rather than design philosophy**, and separately disputes the double-ender's buoyancy rationale on hydrostatic grounds: a double-ender does **not** carry more buoyancy at the stern than a transom-sterned boat of the same length, beam, and draft — a transom (a box shape) encloses more volume than a canoe stern (a triangle shape) — and a double-ender has **less** carrying capacity than an equivalent transom-sterned boat overall.[5]

A counter-argument holds on both points. The "Bris"/Sofia material choice — sticking with expensive divinycell and epoxy despite a deliberate search for a cheaper alternative meeting a sledgehammer-test standard — reads as a genuine **design-driven** decision rather than a materials-driven one, shaped specifically by the insulating properties North Sea sailing demands and largely irrelevant to warmer-water designs like Paradox. On stern shape, two possible reasons for the double-ender choice sit alongside the volume argument above: a canoe stern carries less tendency to be overloaded, so it rises more easily in a following sea without being pooped; and a cultural preference among Norwegian/Scandinavian designers for double-enders. This account of the reasoning remains partly conjecture absent a fuller account from the design's own side — the "Coastal vs Blue Water" question closed without a final reconciliation between the two positions.[6]

## Glen Maxwell's case for blue-water capability, from personal experience

Arguing from personal experience rather than a technical hull comparison, Paradox is considered a **blue-water vessel**, with no hesitation about **personally solo-sailing her great distances**. That judgment rests on **36 years of sailing experience** and **over 30,000 miles of blue-water experience**, mostly in boats under 50 ft (most around 30 ft), and on feeling **safer in "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)"** than in a number of bigger boats, "just because I'm sitting in her and not on her" — framing Paradox's enclosed cabin (see the trunk [Paradox](/paradox.md)) as a safety asset rather than a limitation.[7] It stands as the strongest personal endorsement recorded of Paradox's offshore capability, complementing (but distinct from) the boat's demonstrated rough-water performance in [chine runners and windward performance](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md) and on [Exuma Sound](/components/rig/index.md). **Matt Layden has sailed thousands of miles in Paradox himself.**

A firsthand data point at true blue-water scale confirms the picture: crewing a roughly 38 ft chine-runner sharpie **"Hogfish Maximus"** across the Atlantic to Portugal by way of Bermuda and the Azores in 2004 (see [the Horta report](/people/glen-maxwell.md#a-report-from-horta-and-chine-runners-proven-on-an-ocean-crossing) for the full passage account), the **chine runners worked well even at this larger size**, with the daggerboard needed for well under 48 hours of the whole crossing — used only when hard on the wind and strapped in — and the boat otherwise sailing to weather fine without it. The boat pounded no more than racing boats sailed for comparison, moved comfortably, and was quiet enough below that the crew had to stay deliberately alert to deck conditions. Set against a Tortola-to-Jacksonville passage aboard a Hans Christian 38, the chine-runner sharpie proved the more comfortable of the two, with markedly better roll damping — direct evidence that the chine-runner hull form scales up successfully to genuine ocean-crossing duty, not just coastal and island-hopping use.[8][9][10] "Hogfish Maximus" itself was judged too large for personal taste, but a **21 to 25 ft Paradox** would suit ocean voyaging well; reaching 25 ft, however, was guessed to need a total redesign rather than a simple stretch.

## A rival designer's self-righting/capsize concern

A designer working on a **trailerable boat for the Great Lakes** considered Paradox for it but judged its **chine-keel form could trip the boat in steep breaking waves** — though allowing Paradox "should be able to recover." For that design, **self-righting capability** was rated the most important characteristic of an offshore small boat, followed by **full flotation** (which very few offshore boats have). A **dory hull** was chosen instead, which heels more than Paradox but was judged to "ride better" in the worst conditions, on advice toward a flatter bottom than the "Alpha-Beachcomber" variant first drawn. The "Bris" hull shape (a flat bottom known of but never seen pictured) was weighed too, alongside a **chine-height model** built to develop hull panel shapes.[11] This is the fullest record of an outside designer weighing Paradox's chine-runner hull form specifically against **capsize/self-righting** behavior in breaking waves — a different angle from the structural-resilience and personal sailing-experience arguments above, which do not address stability in extreme conditions.

## A day-sailer misconception corrected

A once-common assumption — that Paradox was "a day sailer only, no reason why, the size I guess"[12] — captures the "coastal cruiser at best" perception the blue-water case above pushes back against. Paradox is "certainly more then a day sailor," and **Matt Layden has sailed thousands of miles in her**;[13] that alone has been enough to convince at least one prospective builder to buy plans.[12]

A separate recollection, without a cited source, holds that **Matt himself endorsed Paradox as a coastal cruiser only** — a detail that sits in direct tension with the "blue-water vessel" framing and "thousands of miles" claim above, never formally reconciled.[14] The **microcruiser forum** at microcruising.com is hosted by builders who "cruised the Bahamas extensively with Matt" — corroborating, from a different angle, the close association with Matt documented elsewhere in this bundle.[15]

## Matt Layden's own range: Nova Scotia to the Bahamas

A data point directly relevant to the coastal-vs-blue-water question, relayed secondhand (believed but not firsthand): Matt **lived aboard his own Paradox for some two or three years while cruising from Nova Scotia to the lower Bahamas** — a considerably longer and higher-latitude range than the Bahamas-focused cruising documented above (see [interior bins and hinged floor](/components/interior.md) and [Matt Layden (biography)](/matt-layden/biography.md)). If accurate, it favors a "blue-water vessel" reading over the recollection that Matt endorsed Paradox as a coastal cruiser only — though the two claims remain formally unreconciled, since neither is a firsthand or documented statement from Matt himself.

Matt's own design intent, as understood from years of building and sailing a Paradox himself, ties the two readings together rather than forcing a choice between them: Matt spent years **"perfecting"** the boat, wanting a boat for **shallow-water cruising in the Bahamas**; although designed primarily for beach cruising, he also **made her safe for sailing offshore** because reaching those islands means crossing the Gulf Stream.[16] On this reading, Paradox's offshore capability is not a separate, bolted-on feature but a direct consequence of the specific coastal cruising ground she was drawn for.

A further, independent Gulf Stream data point restates the Gulf Stream/Bahamas crossing as the standard benchmark for Paradox's open-water capability, calling it routine and "longer and rougher" than a Catalina crossing. See [Southern California sailing and the Catalina Island crossing question](/operations/southern-california-and-catalina-crossing.md).

## Dave Bolduc's firsthand verdict: "tough coastal cruisers," not offshore boats

The fullest firsthand answer to the whole debate comes from [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md), from firsthand offshore sailing with Matt Layden and ~10,000 miles logged in [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), stating the classification plainly:

> "I am not endorsing Paradox or Little Cruiser as offshore sailing craft… they are tough coastal cruisers that can handle their share of bad weather."

and:

> "Paradox, Swamp Thing and Little Cruiser are coastal cruisers, and they probably should be snuggled up some creek when it's blowing that hard out there."

This largely **corroborates** the recollection that Matt regarded the boats as coastal cruisers, while still crediting real heavy-weather capability — the boats have "sailed in 8 foot plus breaking seas offshore with Matt and the boats always handled beautifully," and both boats **cross the Gulf Stream to the Bahamas routinely** (see [Gulf Stream crossing](/operations/gulf-stream-crossing.md)). The original [Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md) — "probably a little more seaworthy than LC" — has ~3,000 miles on her with no mishaps.

The underlying **seaworthiness thesis** is that hull-*bottom* shape matters little: "the shape of the bottom of a monohull has little to do with its seaworthiness… sturdy and water tight, and sufficiently ballasted to be self-righting, it will prove to be a reasonably safe ocean boat." Keels are actively distrusted (they "became wedged between the coral heads"), and larger sharpies — [Hogfish](/people/chris-morejohn.md), Loose Moose, Romp — stand as proof of flat-bottom seaworthiness (see [sharpie lineage](/design/sharpie-lineage.md) for what does and doesn't make a hull like this one a sharpie in the first place).

On the practical envelope: using a Kestrel wind meter the **windward limit** was fixed at roughly **22–27 kt and 6–8 ft seas** ("pretty much the practical limit for our small boat to windward"), with downwind runs manageable to ~26–32 kt under two-to-three reefs; the heavy-weather strategy is to **retreat to shallow, protected water** rather than push offshore ("How big can a wave get in a few feet of water?").

A firsthand owner's-eye summary of what that envelope means in practice: Paradox **"can work to windward, but not handily as a Wayfarer or a Mirror dinghy,"** and without an engine there is no guarantee of reaching a windward objective — but the boat is very strong, can take the ground, and can be sealed from the elements for relative comfort; with food and drink aboard, it does not matter if plans are changed by weather, or the boat is stranded on a mud bank until the next tide.[17]

On knockdowns: two Layden boats have been knocked down and **self-righted** — [Terrapin](/matt-layden/terrapin.md) (off Maine) and [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) (rolled completely in the Gulf Stream) — while Paradox and Little Cruiser have never been knocked down in firsthand experience "even in seas of 10-12 feet," though "it still could occur." The designer's claimed **self-righting to 165° of heel** (see [weight and ballast](/design/weight-and-ballast.md)) underpins that record.

A direct comparison against an open-cockpit boat of similar overall size makes the same watertight-cabin point concretely. Two near-disasters aboard the open boat — caught in a breaking-seas race off a headland where any wave breaking into the cockpit could have swamped her, and caught in a squall off a pier that nearly capsized her — are set against Paradox: **"virtually unsinkable,"** her enclosed cabin and absent cockpit making her watertight with the hatch closed, and even with the hatch open it would take "a big curler" to get any real volume of water into the boat; she is judged "a hundred times stronger" than the open boat, and was designed for **drying out on beaches**.[18]

## A racer/cruiser comparison, and Bill Serjeant's verdict

Compared against the **Dix-designed Mini Transat 650** — a fast, purpose-built 21 ft transatlantic racer with a large sail area and a fixed deep fin keel — as an alternative project, the two designs are "as different as chalk and cheese": Paradox, under 16 ft LOA, is built for **coastal cruising with shallow-water capability**, plus the ability to make an occasional short offshore passage, while the Mini Transat is purpose-built as an ocean racer.[19] Adding limited kevlar/carbon-fiber composite reinforcement to key components — mast, rudder fittings — would not change the underlying verdict:[20] Paradox is no doubt *capable* of crossing an ocean, but she was **not designed for that purpose** — the same coastal-cruiser-with-real-heavy-weather-capacity verdict reached elsewhere in this file.

## Structural strength as the real limit, not distance from shore

A related reframing of the same question sets aside rating Paradox's offshore ability directly and instead names the underlying design tradeoff: a boat built for serious **coastal cruising** — reefs, groundings, being bounced on a beach — arguably needs to be **stronger** than a boat built for open ocean alone, since it must survive direct contact with the bottom that a true offshore boat is designed to avoid. One concrete data point for that strength is the Paradox's **rudder pintle**, at **15 mm** diameter — unusually robust for a 14 ft boat of any kind.[21]

## What remains unresolved

- No published, named source (naval architecture text, stability curve, or official rating) has been cited on either side of the coastal-cruiser vs. blue-water classification; the case for each rests on opinion and personal experience.
- The specific concern that Paradox's chine-keel form might trip the boat in steep breaking waves (above) has no recorded rebuttal.
- The recollection that Matt Layden once endorsed Paradox as a "coastal cruiser only" and the contrary "blue-water vessel"/"sailed thousands of miles" claim are not reconciled against each other, and neither traces to a firsthand, documented statement from Matt himself.
- What concrete changes, if any, would make Paradox more suitable for blue-water/ocean passages as opposed to coastal cruising remains an open question, as does whether "Zoë"'s construction would need changing for an Atlantic crossing.
- Whether a genuine 21-25 ft ocean-going Paradox derivative is buildable without a full redesign is unresolved: the Atlantic-crossing experience aboard "Hogfish Maximus" (above) suggested the size range would suit ocean voyaging well, but the immediate reaction to the idea was that reaching 25 ft would take a total redesign, not a simple stretch of the existing lines.

## Seamanship over seaworthiness: a professional builder's endorsement

A sharp distinction between **seamanship** and **seaworthiness** cuts through the question of how far offshore a Paradox could safely venture.[22] "Seaworthiness" is a label a vessel earns only in hindsight — the same is true of the most impressive acts of seamanship — and what matters more is a **seamanlike attitude**, gained through personal experience, that governs what an individual sailor judges to be safe. The danger of "dragging a foot on the beach" ranks greater than the risk of open sea room 75 miles out, where distance from other recreational craft, commercial shipping, fishing gear, shoals, and land-influenced weather lets a sailor sleep with less worry — anxiety-induced fatigue being a sailor's worst enemy. There is **no reason the Paradox as designed would not be capable of transoceanic voyages** at the hands of an experienced sailor, while final judgment on blue-water passages properly waits on having built and sailed one's own boat. The design itself draws particular praise as "a masterful design" refined through years of the designer's own personal experimentation — it would be presumptuous to think of "improving" upon it.[23] As one builder summed up the same appraisal, "all that is left is to have an equally evolved sailor."[24]

## A 2004 revisiting: Paradox against Sven Yrvind's "Sofia"

Two years after the original coastal-vs-blue-water debate above, the Paradox/"Bris" comparison reopened from a would-be-owner's rather than a designer's point of view. Side-by-side photo files compared Paradox to **"Sofia,"** a design evolved from the "Bris" boats — Sofia's hull specified in **2 in-thick Divinycell foam-sandwich** construction, like Bris, though a builder with only a kayak behind them considered substituting thinner half-inch foam and conventional fiberglass rather than committing to an ocean crossing. Paradox was judged the simpler construction project, with full plans already available, and one "could always leave the wife behind" if Sofia's cabin — cut down further by her bow centerboard trunk — proved too cramped for two; seeing an existing Paradox in person had also surprised at how small the boat really was for two-person use.[25]

A parallel Paradox build then underway (see [source and cost](/references/building-plans.md#why-paradox-resists-easy-enlargement) for the fuller build narrative) reflected a wider interest in small-boat design beyond Paradox alone, singling out the **"Elver" canoe yawl** as a boat well liked, with a deck already half strip-planked — "a Labor Of love."[26] Those build photos were followed through to launch, the sight of "goldplated" builds reading as intimidating by comparison, alongside a welcome for anyone building with materials already on hand.[27]

The most practical answer to the original Paradox-vs-Sofia question is simply to **scale up the existing Paradox plans** — already drawn in metric, making enlargement arithmetic straightforward — with a builder able to plausibly recoup the cost of the work by then selling the enlarged plan set, provided Matt Layden and the plans distributor agreed to it (see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) for the established scaling rules of thumb).[28]

There are, in fact, **no formal building plans for Sofia at all** — only the hull and deck were drawn, without specifying any particular cabin layout, leaving each builder to lay out a deck configuration; one Sofia rendering circulated on the list was built from 2D CAD files of the hull (including the curved side deck), which did not cover the rudder, centerboard, or mast, so only the hull and deck in that model are accurate — the rudder and centerboard were mocked up from sketches and photos on the designer's website, and a **stretched version of the Paradox cabin** stood in for a Sofia cabin.[29]

A finished Paradox seen in person can look either too small for a larger sailor or the sailor too large for it, and Sofia can look too difficult to build without prior boatbuilding experience. A parallel weighing of small-cruiser alternatives from that same period — Bolger's **Micro** and **Birdwatcher**, and the bateau.com **"Adelie"** — is covered in [Alternatives for a larger sailor](/similar-boat-designs/alternatives-for-a-larger-sailor.md#bolgers-micro-birdwatcher-and-the-adelie-alternatives-for-a-larger-sailor).

## Flat panels and the "brute hammer" of a breaking wave

A structural safety concern distinct from the stability/self-righting and materials questions above, raised from a traditional European boatbuilding design background: a **round hull** tends to "frustrate the power in the waves," while Paradox's **flat bottom and side panels** look, to that eye, like anvils inviting the full force of a breaking wave — a candidate explanation for why hard-chined "ducks" are comparatively rare among small offshore boats. The latest round-hulled "Bris"-lineage design reads as immediately safer by comparison, with Paradox looking like a "Monday morning product" going only by conventional design training — a concern raised as an open invitation for anyone with real storm-tested experience aboard a Paradox to argue against it. An independent naval-architecture software model, built from the plans by a builder comparing Paradox against his own design, separately confirmed Matt Layden's own stability calculation for Paradox as fully correct, assuming a vertical center of gravity of roughly 120 mm above the design waterline — a computation Layden most likely worked out without a computer, given what a substantial undertaking that would have been.[30] No direct rebuttal to the breaking-wave concern is on record, leaving it an open counterpoint to the flat-bottom seaworthiness case made elsewhere in this file and in [seamanship over seaworthiness: a professional builder's endorsement](#seamanship-over-seaworthiness-a-professional-builders-endorsement).

## From design comparison to a real build: the "BRIS Paradox"

The "Bris" comparison eventually stopped being purely theoretical: a Paradox was built using Sven Yrvind's own **"BRIS" foam-sandwich composite method** rather than plywood, the only Paradox on record built to that specific method. See [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md) for the full build history and material/construction detail.

## Surviving a full roll or pitchpole in breaking seas

Whether Paradox could survive being rolled or pitchpoled end-over-end in 15-20 ft breaking seas — the kind of sea state a rough Catalina-ferry crossing can produce, alongside the parallel question of a Hawaii or Long-Beach-to-Baja passage (see [heavy-weather tactics — a recurring question](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#securing-the-crew-during-a-knockdown)) — has no rolled-boat account on record.[31] The nearest answer is a prediction: the design would soon see genuine long-distance voyages, being "as capable, or more so, than many other small boats that have crossed oceans."[32] No long-distance passage-making record beyond the Gulf Stream/Bahamas crossings documented elsewhere in this file had been set at the time of that prediction.

## A catalogue of conventional offshore features, weighed and mostly declined

A standard checklist of offshore safety gear — a sprayhood, leecloths/seatbelts, a small windvane, a spare emergency rudder, a foam-filled mast, toerails and handholds, and reinforced cabin windows — has been weighed against Paradox's own design,[33] and most of it is judged unnecessary for a boat sailed entirely from an enclosed cabin rather than an open cockpit. A **sprayhood** adds nothing once the hatch can simply be closed, and its loss of forward vision outweighs the occasional teaspoon of spray that reaches the deck in otherwise good conditions — more water coming aboard is itself the signal to retreat below. **Leecloths** offer unclear benefit, though seatbelts or wedged inflatable cushions could help keep a sailor's center of gravity close to the cabin sole in a roll. A **spare/emergency rudder** is dismissed as a "catch phrase contingency" better served by carrying repair tools than a full replacement part — a **yuloh** can stand in for a lost rudder in a pinch, though the boat loses lateral resistance and balance without it — and the design's actual rudder and hardware are already judged "incredibly robust" if built to plan. A **foam-filled mast** has no case behind it: the plans' mast is wood, not hollow metal, and already air-filled. **Toerails**, already part of the design, and the hatch slides on the cabin top already serve as handholds; a season sailed without any toe rail confirmed the rail's footing/handhold value is real (see [boats/enuf.md](/boats/enuf.md#toe-rail-is-it-needed-and-where-to-source-one-in-the-uk) for the fuller exchange on toe-rail material and shape). On **windows**, the better question is what strength is actually "enough" rather than assuming a component inadequate — a reef knot tied in a 10 mm offcut of the boat's own lexan produced no cracking, and a sledgehammer would tear the cabin apart before the glazing gave way.[34] A builder then finishing his own interior favored traditional framed, locking rubber-channel windows — epoxy-filleted into the cabin and tabbed to the deck with cloth rather than through-bolted, which makes replacement easier and doubles as a gasket for storm covers — over thicker lexan held by many screws, each a stress point; for him the stock window and cabin plan would suffice for coastal sailing decided on a case-by-case basis, but a genuine ocean crossing would call for a better window system still.[35]

A **windvane** could be fitted forward of the mast without much difficulty, but self-steering becomes far harder once the wind comes abaft the beam, and a forward-mounted vane would sit in dirty air exactly there[36] — the point of sail where a vane matters most, though still the only mast position that would avoid being fouled by sheet or boom regardless. Matt Layden's own account from the Watertribe discussion forum bears on the underlying self-steering question directly: "Paradox is designed to steer herself relative to wind direction with the helm locked, and does a pretty good job of it unless the wind is gusty or flukey" — confirmed from firsthand sailing experience, at least for the gusty-conditions caveat.[37] (A fuller windvane-fitting exchange, including a trim-tab sketch, is at [self-steering](/operations/self-steering.md#weighing-a-tillerpilot-against-a-genuine-wind-vane).)

**Overall verdict.** None of the checklist items ranks near the top of a serious modification list; the first step toward serious offshore sailing is a couple of seasons' experience in the stock boat, since "the skipper is usually the weakest link."[38] Paradox was designed as a minimal coastal and estuary cruiser for one person — she could cross an ocean in the right hands, but far more suitable yachts exist for that specific purpose, the same coastal-cruiser-with-real-capability verdict reached elsewhere in this file.[39]

## Window strength: a decades-long 1/4 in lexan track record, and one collision

Quarter-inch lexan, as specified in the plans, has proven strong enough in practice across three of Matt Layden's own boats — Terrapin, [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md), and the original [Paradox](/paradox.md) — over thousands of miles sailed. The clearest field test came when Little Cruiser was struck broadside in her port windows by a 17 ft powerboat travelling at roughly 15 kts down a narrow creek too tight for both boats to pass; the powerboat's rubrail struck the lexan directly before bouncing off the heavier cruiser and into the mangroves, with nobody hurt, and the glazing came through intact, suffering only a cosmetic rub mark that buffed out almost entirely.[40] A prospective builder weighing whether a Paradox might also serve as a life raft has floated beefing up the stock glazing regardless — 3/8 in lexan, wider hanging knees for mid-span support, and a reinforcing ring around the frame bolt holes — as cheap insurance rather than a response to any known failure.[41]

## A round-Britain question, and whether Paradox is a dinghy or a micro-yacht

A UK circumnavigation attempt by an unrelated small open boat then sailing around Britain prompted a comparison against Paradox's own accommodation and seaworthiness.[42][43] The other boat's enclosed accommodation is minute next to Paradox's: either two can sit, just, or the berth can be made up and a sailor slides in feet first, with headroom to turn over only if hips aren't too wide; she does carry a cockpit, and a tent could in principle be rigged over it, but she was priced high enough that she was not expected to sell well.[44][45]

Paradox is not a racing boat, but she should be capable of a round-Britain attempt with a competent skipper — kayaks have circumnavigated Britain successfully, but no dinghy is known to have done so, raising the further question of whether Paradox is properly classed a dinghy at all, or a micro-yacht instead. Unlike a boat built for sailing on the edge, a Paradox mostly sails herself, with her skipper along for the ride rather than actively racing her.[43][46]

The market an independently built small cruiser like this competes against is a crowded one: broadly similar boats are already available secondhand for a fraction of a new build's cost, making them hard to sell regardless of individual merit, with a wave of used boats expected onto the UK market after a particularly busy sailing summer.[47] A Bolger "Birdwatcher" — the same shoal-draft, no-cockpit design weighed against an enlarged Paradox around the same period (see [Alternatives for a larger sailor — Bolger's Micro, Birdwatcher, and the "Adelie"](/similar-boat-designs/alternatives-for-a-larger-sailor.md#bolgers-micro-birdwatcher-and-the-adelie-alternatives-for-a-larger-sailor)) — was separately listed for sale on eBay UK around the same time, reportedly owned by another Dinghy Cruising Association member.[48]

## A high-latitude query from Argentine Patagonia

A prospective builder from the Argentine Patagonia coast — Rawson, Chubut, on a broad river mouth opening onto the open South Atlantic (43°21′S, 65°03′W), a region of strong wind and sea but almost no local sailing tradition beyond rowing and occasional windsurfing — asked whether Paradox could handle conditions there before committing to a build.[49] The answer, from a correspondent at a comparable southern latitude in New Zealand: Paradox is, for its size, stronger and safer than most other boats, though conditions vary enough by area that knowing what other craft the local fleet actually sails would help judge the fit.[50] Further detail on the local waters and a request for as much data as possible to plan the build followed, with no reply on record beyond the initial reassurance.[51] Construction began that May, on a boat named ["Cachivache"](/boats/cachivache.md) — see [people/alberto-callejon.md](/people/alberto-callejon.md) for the builder and [boats/cachivache.md](/boats/cachivache.md) for the build itself.

## A 2008 Marina del Rey-to-Honolulu plan, and the answer it drew

A newcomer building to plan in Southern California, new to both boatbuilding and sailing, announced a firm intention to sail his own finished Paradox from Marina del Rey to Honolulu — either as a further step beyond a planned 2010 trial run to the Bahamas, or, should that deadline slip, as the goal outright — explicitly inviting the list's "experts" to talk him out of it if enough of them judged a "properly built to plans" Paradox unequal to the crossing. It is the concrete case the 2004 rolled-boat question above had only asked hypothetically, [whether anyone had sailed a Paradox to Hawaii, or Long Beach to Baja and back](#surviving-a-full-roll-or-pitchpole-in-breaking-seas).[52]

Reaction split cleanly along seamanship-versus-seaworthiness lines. One reply questioned only the build's own timeline, not the passage's feasibility.[53] A fuller reply set the boat question aside altogether: building a first boat, learning to sail her, and even a routine crossing to nearby Catalina Island already carry real risk and a real learning curve for a first-time owner, and almost everyone who builds a boat without first having owned one or two ends up at least somewhat disappointed with the choice; flying out, buying a bigger secondhand boat, booking passage on a freighter, or crewing in an organized ocean race were all offered as better routes to the same experience first (see [Paradox as a first boat](/operations/first-boat-suitability.md) for the wider first-timer's-build discussion this echoes).[54] The prospective voyager conceded the point in reply to both correspondents, planning to work through every suggestion first and decide about Hawaii only afterward — motivated as much by an old acquaintance of nearly 20 years living in Honolulu as by the voyage itself, and noting the crossing itself takes only about three weeks.[55][56]

Two established, well-traveled Paradox builders then converged on the same verdict already reached in [seamanship over seaworthiness: a professional builder's endorsement](#seamanship-over-seaworthiness-a-professional-builders-endorsement): the boat itself would very likely make the crossing given the right conditions, but the skipper is the weak link, and the very fact of asking the question is itself evidence of not yet being ready to go. One recommended deliberately signing on as crew for an ocean passage first — a yacht club's crew-wanted bulletin board suggested as a practical way in — to be exposed to the sea's real conditions under an experienced skipper before trusting his own judgment on the question.[57] The other recommended the same order of operations from the builder's own side: build the boat, make first a few short cruises and then a few longer ones, and read firsthand accounts of small-boat ocean crossings in the meantime — in his own reading, most describe a miserable experience despite completing the passage safely, the same verdict [the *500 Days* circumnavigation account draws above](#what-conventional-blue-water-features-paradox-and-bris-both-forgo).[58]

The builder agreed with both replies, reporting he had already begun taking short day sails on a friend's boat and had signed up for sailing classes that October, with no problems so far handling the boat's motion underway; conceded the boat might well be ready before he was, and that further preparation might yet cool his own taste for the trip, but held — amid a major change in his own personal circumstances — that the idea "sure looks like a Good Time."[59] A lighter alternative closed the thread out: simply flying to Hawaii instead, as one correspondent had done himself for a Maui holiday, day-tripping there by rental car and bike rather than boat — declined only on a personal dislike of flying, hot-air balloons excepted.[60][61]

The flying-versus-sailing joke that closed the thread above drew several more replies before it, too, ran its course. A fellow private pilot volunteered the same dislike of commercial flying;[62] the builder himself traced his own aversion further back — a farming childhood familiar with crop-dusting aircraft, and a career since as a professional magician that had put him on plenty of commercial flights regardless — settling that he would rather drive or sail than fly commercially again;[63] a reply recalled decades of bush flying around the Canadian Arctic in aircraft no newer than a 1950s DC-3, concluding that flying itself is fine, "it's the landings that might get you," while allowing that weighed against a commercial jet or an LA freeway at rush hour, the Hawaii-bound Paradox passage above might actually come out ahead;[64] and a newlywed capped it with his own near-miss returning from a Hawaii honeymoon — a nearly botched landing that shut the airport down while the crew worked out what had gone wrong — enough on its own to make him swear off flying for good and pick a Paradox passage over it without hesitation.[65]

Two further, independent replies answered the original proposal on its numbers rather than its seamanship. One set the sea-time counsel aside for a rough calculation instead: a roughly 2,300-mile passage at a realistic 3 kt average works out to well over a month continuously at sea in all weather, measured against a 140-mile round trip just completed in a 22-footer on the other side of the Atlantic — glad enough, even then, to reach solid ground again at the end of it; a liferaft was called essential equipment, with nowhere obvious aboard a Paradox to stow one; and the Donald Crowhurst affair — the catastrophic 1968 solo round-the-world race attempt that ended in the sailor's own likely suicide after faking his position — was raised as the cautionary case of solo ambition outrunning readiness.[66] The other offered encouragement by comparison instead, judging Paradox the better sea boat next to a 14 ft West Wight Potter — a design that has itself made a notable ocean passage — and linking a published account of it;[67] a further reply added a second West Wight Potter, the larger 19 ft version, and its own voyage account, to the same comparison.[68] The builder closed the whole exchange simply, resolving to build the boat, build his seamanship alongside it, "and we will see."[69]

## A 2010 revisit: the coastal-cruiser classification restated, and a long-passage provisioning rebuttal

New to the group, to microcruising, and to Paradox altogether, one correspondent in September 2010 asked how the design's own history bears on open-ocean safety and comfort — independently finding, and judging "very informative," this file's own earlier Coastal-vs-Blue-Water debate above.[70][71] The designer's coastal-cruiser classification was restated directly in reply: Paradox has better open-water survivability than many small craft that have made ocean crossings, certainly better than a West Wight Potter, and, since her crew is never exposed to the weather in an open cockpit, is markedly more comfortable underway as well.[72]

Weighing whether to commit at 59 years old, the same correspondent judged a project on the scale of Bolger's "Wee Seal" — a five-year build, for a sailor prone to over-analyzing every aspect of a project — too great an undertaking, but found Paradox's roughly one-year build timeline, and her ability to be trailered and beached, considerably more attractive.[73] A completed Paradox bore the estimate out directly: the build itself took about 18 months of evenings and weekends, judged the norm for a builder who does not take a sabbatical partway through. From firsthand cruising experience, coasting was judged the more engaging choice regardless of the boat's demonstrated open-water capability — once out of sight of land, sailing becomes "just boring" short of genuinely stormy conditions, where watching the passing coastline gives a continuous, visible sense of progress.[74]

The same thread reopened the coastal-vs-open-water safety question from a different angle: island-hopping or crossing a wide bay can pose a challenge as great as the open ocean itself, and, per Sven Yrvind's own observation, a coastline can threaten a boat more than open water does — particularly when caught upwind of an unfamiliar, rocky, shoal-infested headland. That is exactly the condition Paradox is best suited to survive: she can duck into a shallow, tiny cove and remain upright in the surf even if the tide goes out from under her, with her crew safe and dry inside provided everything is secured according to the designed set-up. Phil Bolger once suggested that the safest response to being caught in a storm off a lee shore, in a small design like this, might be to drop the sail, deploy the anchor, and lie down in the boat's lowest point — advice that, for a design already self-righting, shallow-draft, and watertight like Paradox, amounts to already being inside one's own "life boat." The design's only real drawback for this kind of sailing is limited storage capacity for genuinely long off-shore voyages.[75]

That storage limitation drew a direct rebuttal: 30 days of freeze-dried meals, snacks and desserts included and sealed in nitrogen pouches, now pack into a single 5-gallon bucket for under $80 at Costco and similar stores, and with a hand-pump, lifeboat-type watermaker aboard, a Paradox could feasibly cross long stretches of open water regardless.[76]

Two years after [the original proposal above](#a-2008-marina-del-rey-to-honolulu-plan-and-the-answer-it-drew), the same builder returned to the idea, prompted by the freeze-dried-food-and-watermaker exchange just above: still seriously weighing whether to sail his own finished Paradox from Marina del Rey to Honolulu, expecting an answer either "next year... or maybe the following." ## A 2010 coda: a proposed keel declined, and two owners' endorsements

The same 2010 thread drew a proposal to bolt on a small fin keel, modelled on a vintage racing-sharpie design called "Missile," and precedented by the San Francisco "Great Pelican"'s own external, cement-filled box keel — a coastal dory design that has nonetheless crossed to Hawaii — as a way to make Paradox self-righting and self-rescuing "just like Bolger's Micro."[78] The idea drew a direct question back — wasn't Paradox already designed that way? — with willingness to fit a removable version if one were actually needed.[79] The answer from an established owner was flat: Paradox already self-rights and self-rescues, more so than the Micro, and a keel would only detract from her performance.[80] See [self-righting and capsize recovery — a 2010 proposal for a bolt-on keel, declined](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#a-2010-proposal-for-a-bolt-on-keel-declined) for the fuller exchange, including a restated 165-degree stability figure and further detail drawn from a repeated telling of the design's own Beale Park righting-moment test.

The thread closed with two owners' endorsements rather than further debate. A finished Western Australian Paradox's builder, skeptical of the design's likely performance while he was still building her, reported having since sailed some pretty wild conditions and found her performing very well every time — years of experience deepening his appreciation for how much thought Matt Layden put into even the simplest-looking detail, and his advice to anyone unsure being to trust the designer and build to plan rather than deviate. See [Small Fry — a 2010 testimonial: trusting the design](/boats/small-fry.md#a-2010-testimonial-trusting-the-design) for the fuller account.[81] Nearing the end of his own two-year build, one correspondent found the testimonial reassuring given how much controversy surrounds the design, having chosen Paradox "on faith" — the word of actual owners over the untested opinions of others — and having found building her easier than a folding kayak he built alongside her, despite no real woodworking background; the plans felt overwhelming at first, but building one step at a time let the design "begin to talk to him." See [Wizard Spawn — nearing completion, and a reflection on building "on faith"](/boats/wizard-spawn.md#nearing-completion-and-a-reflection-on-building-on-faith-october-2010) for the fuller build account.[82]

## A 2011 coda: a request for a fuller rough-water account

A further correspondent's closing message on the topic, in March 2011, judged the sharpest practical difference between Paradox and most other microcruiser designs to be exactly this open-water capability: most such designs suit only sheltered or nearly-sheltered waters, built as open or partially open boats, where Paradox is not. The only account he knew of anyone actually sailing a Paradox in rough conditions was [Little Jim's own](/boats/little-jim.md), and he asked for a fuller written account of the design's performance in genuine coastal-passage conditions.[83] Small Fry's own firsthand [Cape Leeuwin rounding](/boats/small-fry.md#a-firsthand-account-rounding-cape-leeuwin-march-2011), told days earlier on the same mailing list, stands as a second such account.

## A 2011 recurrence: an Atlantic-crossing hypothetical, self-righting restated to 120 degrees, and a former Faith owner's own appraisal

A newcomer's hypothetical — prompted by published accounts of a 12 ft circumnavigation and a Hobie-cat Atlantic crossing — asked what would happen to Paradox in a storm at sea, whether an ocean crossing is genuinely possible, and what would need to change (a fixed keel and heavy ballast?) to make her more seaworthy, or whether she would simply roll over in heavy conditions.[84]

The consensus answer restates rather than revises the coastal-cruiser-with-real-capability verdict reached [elsewhere in this file](#seamanship-over-seaworthiness-a-professional-builders-endorsement): Paradox could very likely make the crossing, though "totally un-fun," and success turns on having the right sailor, weather, and route rather than on any change to the boat herself.[85][86]

A fuller technical case restated Paradox's self-righting/self-rescuing capability, giving an estimate of roughly **120 degrees** of heel provided crew are lashed to the floor and the boat is sealed against water intrusion — releasing the sheet, so the sail no longer holds the water down, should let her pop back upright. That figure is consistent with Don Elliott's own early (1996) estimate rather than the later, more precise 160–165° figures documented on [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources), and is not reconciled against them here. Provisioning is restated as no longer the limiting factor it once was, repeating the same freeze-dried-meals-and-hand-pump-watermaker case [already recorded above](#a-2010-revisit-the-coastal-cruiser-classification-restated-and-a-long-passage-provisioning-rebuttal), leaving confinement itself as the real hazard: weeks spent mostly immobile in a hull "the size of a large bathtub," with a real risk of the muscle atrophy that comes from long confinement to a bed, possibly needing physical therapy on reaching land. An Eastern-US-to-Azores passage, helped by the Gulf Stream and the prevailing westerlies during part of the year, has been made in as little as 26 days, though five weeks is closer to the average. A modified 16 ft "Great Pelican" sharpie has already sailed San Diego to Hilo, Hawaii — extremely stable and capsize-resistant, but, unlike Paradox, **not** self-righting or self-rescuing once fully loaded and over, more like a small catamaran in that respect. The international "Around in Ten" competition — whether a 10 ft boat can circumnavigate the globe — and Paul Fisher (Selway-Fisher)'s purpose-built 8 ft "Micro 8" ocean-crossing mini yacht were pointed to as the current benchmark for what a boat this size can be asked to do.[87]

The same question drew a firsthand appraisal from "[Faith](/boats/faith.md)"'s former owner and builder: an ocean crossing would not be recommended, since far better-suited boats exist for the purpose, but it could be done — given the right person, the right weather, the right route, and the right time of year, a crew could make it "to tell the tale," with a foolproof self-steering system the single greatest asset such a passage could have. Even an empty barrel can cross an ocean under wind and current alone, and there is no reason an empty Paradox could not do the same.[88] The exchange closed with a firm declaration of intent to build.[89]

By January 2018 the hypothetical still had no realized answer: asked directly whether a Paradox had ever made a transatlantic-scale crossing, a two-boat owner with years of firsthand sailing experience knew of none, pointing instead to the longest passages actually made by that date — the Florida-to-Bahamas Gulf Stream crossings documented above, one of them extended to Cuba and back, and a full transit of the Hawaiian island chain (see [Dave Skaife's voyage to Cuba](/merits/skaife-cuba-voyage.md) and [Dave Skaife's Hawaiian island cruise](/merits/skaife-hawaii-cruise.md)). Full firsthand seaworthiness account: [people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md](/people/andre-francois-bourbeau.md#paradox-and-gorfnik).[90]

## A 2018 test case: 370 nautical miles of open Pacific to Lord Howe Island

Weeks after [the hypothetical above went unanswered](#a-2011-recurrence-an-atlantic-crossing-hypothetical-self-righting-restated-to-120-degrees-and-a-former-faith-owners-own-appraisal), a concrete distance-and-route question arrived: whether Paradox could manage a 370 nautical mile open-Pacific passage from Port Macquarie, New South Wales, to Lord Howe Island, asked by a newcomer finishing a second small-boat build and planning a Paradox as his next project, who allowed that skill and preparation account for only half of any voyage's outcome.[91]

The replies converged on a could/would/should distinction: many less seaworthy boats have made similar trips, so Paradox could very likely do it; whether it would succeed depends entirely on the conditions met at the time; and whether it should be attempted at all is a judgment call for the sailor alone, not something an outsider can answer. The same reply drew a parallel to routinely being asked whether a similarly small boat could cross the English Channel to France — yes, but pointless when a ferry exists, since crossing open water out of sight of land is found tedious next to coasting along in sight of the shore.[92]

A boat-design comparison supplied a second, independent yes. A clinker-plywood copy of the Ellam/Mudie "Sopranino" — the pioneering light-displacement design that crossed the Atlantic in the 1950s, and had itself been sailed from Sydney to Lord Howe Island by a previous owner — is judged similar enough to Paradox in innovation and seaworthiness that, with a carefully chosen weather window, no fixed schedule, and Ellam/Mudie-level seamanship, Paradox should manage the round trip too. Sopranino herself logged 10,000 miles against the roughly 1,800 nm this round trip would need; the design theory behind both boats, in the two designers' own words, holds that a boat built light enough will lift over the tops of waves and survive, rather than being built strong enough to fight through them.[93]

Firsthand experience supplied the case against complacency. One owner's account: offshore conditions feel safe, but "giant waves are another thing" — having been knocked down twice, once deliberately and once by accident in a 120-degree roll that shoved him along the cabin and let water in through an open hatch, at a cost of some gear; the boat's practical range runs to about 6 knots flat out and 4 knots average in a moderate breeze, and the deciding questions are whether enough food and water, and enough fitness for a long stretch without rest, can really be carried and sustained.[94] The same sailor had already put a smaller figure on the same fatigue question two years earlier: 23 nautical miles in a single day is tiring, and anyone contemplating a stretch out of sight of land for that long needs sea experience, Paradox experience, stamina and, above all, a carefully chosen weather window.[95]

A second firsthand account set the romantic idea of an ocean passage against its reality. The furthest sailed on this account was 25 nautical miles, about 7 miles offshore, taking 13 hours at an average of 2.5 knots — slow, boring, and lonely even in benign conditions, worse if seasick, and "like a washing machine" were the weather to turn rough. A tiller pilot is treated as mandatory, since ocean currents can outrun the boat's own speed; food needs packing for roughly twice the expected passage time, a passage of this length likely running 7 to 14 days; cooking is difficult once the boat is moving continuously, and a long stretch with the hatch closed is "like living in a coffin." The single most serious offshore shortcoming identified this way is gear failure: fixing a problem with the rig means climbing out onto the hatch top, and the added weight up there makes the boat markedly less stable exactly when a repair is most needed. The suggested test before attempting a genuine distant passage is a deliberate 50 nautical mile run straight offshore to a GPS waypoint and back — nominally 100 nm, but more likely 150 nm once tacking and drift are allowed for, taking 2 to 4 days — long enough to force a real decision on whether ocean voyaging suits the sailor, or whether a larger boat would serve better.[96]

A third firsthand account confirmed the same picture from direct experience: a rough day in a Paradox is exhausting, and the boat "never stops gyrating"; 25 nautical miles out and the same distance back the following day is manageable, and 50 nautical miles over two days is that account's own record, briefly surfing to 7 knots on a following swell while fighting an accidental gybe the whole way, arriving so tired as to collapse rather than properly fall asleep. The lesson drawn is that a long passage in a boat this size demands real fitness — though a shorter, three-day coastal passage, sailed point-to-point rather than out-and-back, is judged genuinely enjoyable even in light air, and the GPS-waypoint test above was endorsed as a sound way to build up to one.[97]

## A 2019 test case: a Force 6 rescue, and skipper endurance as the real limit

A further firsthand account, the following year, reframed the practical-range question already worked through above rather than adding another distance figure: **[Roselt Croeser](/people/roselt-croeser.md)** uploaded a written account of being towed in from about 12 nautical miles offshore in **Force 6** conditions aboard "**[Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)**" — the story itself is not preserved in the archive, only the covering message — and drew a general lesson from it rather than a fixed number: Paradox "certainly looks after its skipper," but beyond the hull's own limits, how many hours of rough, dangerous, potential-rollover conditions a passage can take comes down to the skipper's own endurance and mental state.[98]

A reply from **[Nick Bell](/people/nickolas-bell.md)**, of "**[Thor](/boats/thor.md)**," corroborated the same trade-off from his own experience, restating the 2016 Coast Guard tow already on record (see [boats/thor.md — a second Coast Guard tow, ballast resolved, and a Wardang Island lap](/boats/thor.md#a-second-coast-guard-tow-ballast-resolved-and-a-wardang-island-lap-april-2016)) with two points of general advice drawn from it: accept a tow if sea rescue offers one rather than wait to be asked — "never say no at sea" — and always ask for a long towrope, since the short one used on his own tow let the two boats pitch out of step and snatch and jerk the hull for over two hours. The cockpit around him has since been padded, a direct response to the bruising taken in the August 2018 knockdown already on record (see [boats/thor.md — a deliberate capsize test, and mast buoyancy versus ballast in a knockdown](/boats/thor.md#a-deliberate-capsize-test-and-mast-buoyancy-versus-ballast-in-a-knockdown-august-2018)).[99]

A further reaction to the account recalled — by its own admission, perhaps imprecisely — a maxim attributed to Phil Bolger: that small boats simply cannot sail to windward once a seaway reaches 25 knots or so, and will be very uncomfortable and sail poorly in it regardless. The tow bore that out, compounded by a small boat's limited capacity for whiling away long hours waiting for conditions to ease, and by seasickness, which alone explains asking for one.[100]

Nick Bell's own way of coping with exactly that kind of wait credits temperament as much as any feature of the boat: an easy sleeper who naps readily even on day sails, carries fishing gear and books as distractions, and treats staying in port as itself part of sailing rather than a failure to get moving.[101]

Croeser's own reply, a few days later, answered a further correspondent's question about a drogue directly: it would have helped, but only for a couple of hours, after which he would have been very close to a lee shore fully exposed to the open ocean — the real difficulty, in his judgment, is sailing into a substantial sea with a small boat at all, not any particular shortcoming in the chine runners, which he rates "as good as it gets on a small boat." He weighed the ordeal against his own prior ownership of a 25 ft Vertue (four tonnes): the bigger, heavier boat would have coped better with the conditions, but had still been hard work in her own right. Heaving-to through the worst of it felt comfortable and safe; his real concern was the rudder, which took loud, audible strain as the bigger waves pushed the boat backward — the standing lesson he draws from it is that a Paradox's rudder has to be built as strong as it can possibly be made.[102]

## A second 2019 test: a deliberate overnight trial, ten nautical miles out

A second, independently undertaken 2019 test approached the same question from the opposite direction — not an emergency but a planned trial of deliberate offshore exposure, scaled back from an initial 100-mile plan to a single overnight, anchorage-free stay 10 nautical miles out once the real time and swell exposure of the fuller version sank in. It settled two points of general planning advice: that a Paradox's realistic passage-planning speed is close to 2.5 knots, well under the boat's nominal 5-knot maximum; and that towing a drogue while lying ahull only eases the boat's motion and only avoids drifting onto a lee shore if she is also being actively sailed under a scrap of sail at the same time — under drogue alone she cannot make any way to windward at all. The trial's own verdict matched the skipper-is-the-limit framing reached elsewhere in this file: the boat and her build were never in doubt, seasickness aside, and what onlookers before the trip had predicted — capsize, structural failure, drowning — came nowhere close to matching what actually happened.[103]
