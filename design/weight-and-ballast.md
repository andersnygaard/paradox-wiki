# Weight, ballast, and displacement

See also [heavy-weather tactics](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md) for a real-world heel-angle data point (deck almost awash, "felt pretty solid") and the still-open question of a safe maximum heel angle in a seaway.

The [Paradox](/paradox.md) is deliberately a **small, heavy boat with a lot of stability**: most of its weight sits **below the waterline, in the bilge**, so it stays low and stiff. The figures below come from a boat built "extra heavy"; a lighter build is possible. Matt Layden's plans page showing all the sheets of ply and thicknesses is at `http://www.tomah.com/delliott/paradoxlayout.jpg`.

## Loaded-weight breakdown (Don Elliott's boat)

Weight carried in the **bottom 10 inches** of the hull:

| Item | Weight |
|------|--------|
| Plywood (3/4 in) and lumber — tank structure at the bottom | 120 lb |
| **Lead ballast** — 10 × 40 lb pigs, strapped down | **400 lb** |
| Roving and resin | 75 lb (could be ~50 lb) |
| Water — two tanks, kept full when cruising | 150 lb |
| Food (about a week's supply) | 80–100 lb |
| Battery | 45 lb |
| Anchor and line (two carried) | 40 lb |
| **Subtotal in the bilge** | **~930 lb** |

Weight above the bilge / floor:

| Item | Weight |
|------|--------|
| Plywood and structure above the bilge (hull weight ~400 lb total) | 200 lb |
| Mast and misc. gear above deck | 35 lb |
| Crew and their gear (stow low in bad weather for max stability) | 200 lb |
| **Subtotal above the floor** | **~235 lb + crew** |

**Total ≈ 1,365 lb** loaded, on this heavily built boat.[1] That left roughly 45 lb of spare capacity (about what the rudder weighs, or half that).

## Ballast

- **Lead:** 400 lb, as **ten 40 lb pigs strapped down** in the bilge. Matt Layden allows **less** if desired — carrying more food/equipment lets you reduce lead, as long as any weight is secured.
- Ballast, tanks, and stores together are what put ~930 lb into the bottom 10 inches, giving the boat its stiffness and its expected ability to "plough through" a [chop](/operations/index.md) rather than be knocked about.
- Beyond buying lead pigs new, scrap lead shaped like pipe discarded in a skip (dumpster) is usable as ready-made ballast pigs.[2]
- **Free scrap lead is readily available**, and often better value than buying it: tyre-fitters' shops routinely accumulate buckets of old wheel-balance weights they pay someone to haul away, a source that supplied 17 lead bricks (25 lb each) for the price of two pints of beer. Ballast carried is not fixed for good — it gets swapped out over time as other "useful" equipment comes aboard and takes over part of the weight-budget role.[3] Sourcing scrap lead locally is generally cheaper than traveling any distance to collect someone else's surplus, once travel cost is weighed against simply finding a wheel-balance-weight source closer to home.[4][5][6]
- **"How much ballast" is not a single well-defined number.** A one-line lead-weight figure is a slippery thing to compare between boats, since it depends on what is being counted as ballast: water ballast, always-carried heavy gear such as ground tackle, and general stores/equipment can all be bundled in or left out depending on how the question is asked. A poll asking Paradox builders how much ballast weight worked best for them, offering choices from 150 to 400 lb, drew the observation that the same boat could honestly be answered anywhere from 100 to 350 lb depending on which of those categories counts.[7][8][9]
- **One owner's full weight inventory, itemized.** Pressed for a concrete answer to that same poll, one owner's full load-out was: 100 lb of lead, a 50 lb battery, an electric outboard, three anchors with chain, drinking water carried on top of the water ballast, a second battery for lights, and tinned food. A Paradox's real advantage is that **extra weight isn't a problem** — if a piece of gear might be useful, it can simply be brought along. On some short trips the water ballast is skipped entirely; the boat still sails just as well, though it feels a little more tender (possibly only an impression).[10] Old wheel-balance weights come with their own steel retaining clips: melting them down (a bread tin makes a serviceable mould) lets the steel float free to be skimmed off, leaving clean lead for casting into ballast bricks.[11]
- **A dissenting view: the poll itself was solving a non-problem.** Countering the poll's premise, almost every builder sails a Paradox without enough ballast the first time or two anyway, without harm, since those early outings are short test sails — better, in this view, to start building and defer the ballast question until the hull is actually in the water once or twice.[12]
- **Final poll result: a 50/50 split between the two middle choices.** The poll closed with only two votes cast, one for **250 lb** and one for **300 lb** — both toward the lighter end of the ten-choice, 150-400 lb range on offer, and neither at either extreme.[13]
- **A water-ballast alternative to a fixed heavy bottom, for trailering and light air.** Rather than hauling a permanently heavy bottom around on the trailer, sailing in light winds, or manhandling the boat up a beach, the same self-righting effect can in principle come from water ballast filled and drained as needed — a tradeoff some builders weigh directly against choosing a lighter, Enigma-style design over stock Paradox's fixed-weight bottom.[14]
- **A 2010 restatement of the stock figure, and a determination technique.** Asked what ballast suits ordinary one- or two-person cruising trim, excluding the water tank's own water,[149] Stephen Yahn gave his own practice as **400 lb**, "the way this works" for him — squarely within the stock range already established above.[150] A separate answer to the same question gives a practical method for finding the right figure on any given boat: have someone watch from outside while she is loaded down to the waterline marks shown on the plans, since experience favors carrying too much ballast over too little.[151]
- **A 2013 restatement of the general principle, and a further declining figure.** How much ballast is needed depends on what else is aboard: the plans' 180 kg figure is presumably for an otherwise empty hull, and adding more anchors, motors, tools, batteries, food, and gear should reduce it correspondingly. What is critical is that the boat floats to her lines — too little weight and she is tippy and may not self-right; too much and she is slow. By 2013 the same builder whose ballast had earlier come down to 36 kg after adding a folding bicycle (above) reported carrying about **50 kg** of lead, sourced free from a garage as old tyre balance weights classed as hazardous waste (the shop otherwise paying to have them hauled away), for the cost of a 6-pack; lead in the form of flashing would fit easily along the rear of bulkhead 3 below the floor, where it would hardly be noticeable.[162]
- **A 2015 restatement of the plan figures, with a full worked example.** Read directly off the plans: the specified **70-180 kg (154-396 lb)** of lead pigs, plus the **70-litre (154 lb)** water tank when full, together put the suggested ballast at **308-550 lb** with tanks topped up; when the tanks run dry, the gear a long cruise accumulates under the floorboards is expected to make up the missing 154 lb, which is likely why the plans give a lead-pig range of 242 lb rather than a single figure — read this way, the design sails best with something between **300 and 550 lb** of ballast overall. One owner's own boat carries **300 lb of permanently fixed lead (bricks)** plus **250 lb of movable ballast** — tied-down batteries and extra anchors, plus canned goods, tools, and bottled water stowed under the floor — which alone gives her the feel of a keel boat when loaded; **50-75 lb of camping gear and the crew's own body weight** above floor level are not counted as ballast in this reckoning, even though they probably contribute.[177]

## Moving ballast underway, and keeping drinking water free of contamination

Beyond how much ballast to carry, keeping it low and knowing where to place it matters underway. The heaviest items belong lowest and centred amidships, with lighter gear such as clothing or fenders stowed higher or toward the bow and stern; ballast itself can be shifted as conditions change — moving water jugs and fuel to windward and sitting further forward in the cabin on a long board to windward carries more sail longer and lets the bow bury for extra pointing, while sitting all the way aft suits sailing off the wind.[179]

Built-in water tanks carry a real risk on a cruise to unfamiliar ports: topping one off with bad water contaminates the whole supply — on one occasion, expecting R.O. water at a Bahamas marina, the tank instead took on water contaminated with diesel — and a tank run low far from civilization also means losing a significant share of the boat's own ballast. The preferred alternative is many small water jugs stowed inside the water tank itself, so any one leak stays contained: a cruise typically starts with a couple of cases of bottled spring water, the empties then either kept in the bow for extra flotation or refilled locally from a 2.5-gallon collapsible jug, with sand, shells, or coloured stones used to fill whatever space the emptied bottles leave, doubling as ballast in their place.[179]

A second, independently worked-out water system reaches the same contamination-avoidance goal by a different route: 30 litres kept in 1.5-litre bottles under the floor, plus a 10-litre jerry can fitted with a hand pump as the immediate "ready tank" in an aft compartment — five pump strokes is the exact amount for a cup of coffee. As the ready tank empties it is topped up from the under-floor bottles, which are then refilled with seawater to keep the same ballast weight in place, so any bad water stays separate and usable only for washing, never drinking. The water-ballast tanks themselves were converted to general storage some years back — a conversion floated, but not confirmed carried out, in an earlier exchange above — now holding spare anchors, chain, and tools alongside the water bottles. Typical fresh-water use in ordinary English summer weather runs to about 1.5 litres a day; on a warm-enough coastal passage, a seawater wash-down substitutes for a full bath.[180]

## Real-world under-ballasted handling

How much the lead ballast matters in practice is well documented across several boats. Sailed with only ~75 lb of ballast aboard (a small fraction of the ~400 lb design figure), "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)" "ghosted" along fine but was noticeably tender; loaded for a week-long overnighter (full water tanks, a week's stores) she was still **about 100 lb short** of stiff enough — "a little too tender" for confident forward movement on deck to handle anchor or dock lines, though day-to-day walking about would already be easier with that much added.[15]

A matching cautionary data point runs the other direction: removing 100 lb of lead from forward of amidships on "Little Jim" (chasing an unfounded worry about being overweight) moved her center of gravity far enough forward to bring on considerable weather helm and destroy her tracking ability outright — promptly reversed by putting the lead back.

A maiden-sail account supplies a third data point on the same theme: launching without enough ballast aboard to reach the boat's roughly 9 in full cruising draft left her sitting on her waterline but noticeably tender. The standing advice is not to hesitate to ballast a Paradox all the way up to her full cruising displacement, since the boat "loves the weight" and keeping the chine runners submerged as deep as possible is what maximizes windward performance.[16][17]

"Little Jim" herself was once found tender too, and the question was settled empirically on a calm day by holding the mast and leaning weight out over the side to observe the result — a firsthand mast-lean-out heel test photographed and posted under "Stability & Balance" on a build website. In ordinary sailing trim, close-hauled and sailing well, "Little Jim" heels to about **20 degrees**, the same steady figure reported repeatedly elsewhere from actual passages.[18] A related, smaller-scale data point on the same boat: sailing without the water-tank ballast in light conditions is workable, but still has a **noticeable effect on initial stability** even with the full fixed-lead load aboard — see [transom deck plate — the fill/suction copper pipe: sizing, timing, and the fittings that connect to it](/construction/transom-deck-plate.md#the-fillsuction-copper-pipe-sizing-timing-and-the-fittings-that-connect-to-it).[19]

A further, independent case came via a letter posted to designer Jim Michalak's own website: a Paradox owner outside the mailing list, dissatisfied with her windward performance, had fitted a leeboard and judged it a real improvement.[159] Photographs showed a modified tiller and a different-shaped rudder, so departures from the stock plans plainly extended beyond the sail alone — but if the visible line where her paint changes colour marks the actual waterline, she appears seriously under-ballasted, a condition that would by itself account for much of the reported leeway regardless of any fitting added to compensate for it.[160][161]

A further account ties the same principle to windward pointing rather than just heel: with her water ballast pumped out to lighten her enough for a grounding recovery, a Paradox stopped pointing upwind altogether, crabbing sideways instead of gaining ground to windward — direct confirmation, under real conditions rather than at the dock, that ballast weight (and the resulting chine-runner depth) is what the boat's windward ability depends on. Full account: [components/rig/sail.md — a grounding recovery, and the sprit sail's effect on centre of effort](/components/rig/sail.md#a-grounding-recovery-and-the-sprit-sails-effect-on-centre-of-effort).[194]

## Filling the tanks from the sea, and eliminating free-surface effect

A **fully filled** ballast tank has no free-surface effect at all — the sloshing, and the stability loss that goes with it, only arises when a tank is partly empty, so topping the built-in tanks all the way up removes the problem outright rather than merely reducing it.[163] One owner fills his boat's ballast tanks directly from the sea with an electric pump, rather than treating them as a drinking-water supply, keeping potable water in a separate container instead — judged the easiest method he has found for topping up.[163]

## Waterplane sensitivity

The hull's waterplane is such that the boat **sinks about 1 inch per 200 lb** added, so an extra 100 lb lowers her only about 1/2 inch. Builders are urged to stay close to the design displacement, though it can be exceeded somewhat — a very similar boat is cruised **two-up** with gear and stores (see [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)), so it must go over it some.

## Checking trim against the plans' waterline marks

The plans give a concrete field check for correct trim: with the sailor sitting in the boat, a second person checks the waterline at bow and stern against **sheet 2** of the plans — the bow should sit about **50 mm below the surface**, and the transom should be just kissing the water. Ballast is adjusted until both marks read true, a check worth repeating every season as more equipment goes aboard and shifts the boat's loaded trim.[191] A photographed example matching this description closely — transom just kissing the water, the waterline at the rudder bolt, the bow fully immersed — was achieved by moving about 30 kg of lead forward of bulkhead 2; see [operations/sailing-characteristics.md — a 2018 recurrence](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#a-2018-recurrence-tiller-fatigue-running-and-two-rudder-position-ideas) for the fuller exchange this photograph was part of.[193]

## Installing the lead ballast

Two methods for securing lead ballast are on record.

**Tapped hardwood blocks.** Threaded fastenings hold the lead down into **hardwood blocks (not oak)**, bonded to the hull with epoxy, then **tapped (threaded) after bonding** rather than beforehand — the tapping can't be done on the bench because the blocks must be drilled and aligned once assembled in the boat. This avoids splitting the hardwood blocks or the chines, which would otherwise create a lot of extra rework, and is a quick, safe procedure. A stronger but slower alternative is to bore the holes oversize, fill with epoxy, and set coarse-threaded bolts in the re-drilled holes, waxed for removability — "by far the strongest method." Wax or oil near a boat under construction is worth handling carefully in general, since either can contaminate a joint and cause an epoxy bond to fail.[20][21][22][23]

**Waxed machine screws.** Drill one size **smaller** than the fastener and drive a **machine screw** directly (no tapping needed) for strong holding power; **coat the fastener with wax, then dip in epoxy** before driving, for even better holding.[21]

**Sheet lead bedded in a sand/epoxy infill.** A third method, proven on a prior 25 ft Folksong: lay sheet lead against the hull bottom and hold it down permanently with a mix of **sand and epoxy resin** poured around and over it, rather than (or in addition to) screwing it down.[24] Sand serves two purposes at once — it is both the **cheapest available heavy infill material** for bulking out epoxy and a legitimate way to add **substantial extra ballast weight** cheaply, the same reasoning behind sand-filled bags used elsewhere as movable trimming ballast.[25] Any sand used **must be dried hot and then cooled before mixing** into the resin, to drive off moisture that would otherwise compromise the cure.[24] The sheet lead itself could equally be screwed to the bottom on its own, but without the sand/resin infill nothing is actually holding it in place; sand is not a structural reinforcement in the way glass cloth is, and is not a substitute for it — it is added specifically as cheap ballast bulk, not strength.[26][27] One practical caution: a sand/epoxy pour of any real volume **generates significant exothermic heat while curing**, worth planning for in a confined bilge space.[26]

**Ballast stowage detail.** On one boat, the **forward-compartment lead** serves as **trim weight to counterbalance the battery**, held in place by **heavy nylon straps (with bolts)** in a tight fit. All lead is **sealed in heavy plastic** with **rubber padding top and bottom**, and reportedly "will never move in normal use."[23]

These building steps were also written up as an article for **MAIB** (*Messing About In Boats* magazine), in addition to the existing ["Building the Paradox" articles](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md).[23]

Lead-installation photos (referenced but with no caption text): `http://www.tomah.com/delliott/par1.jpg`, `par2.jpg`, `par3.jpg`, `par4.jpg`.[20]

## Reaching the ballast fastenings after the floors are in

The plans' overlapping floors can make the **inner** ballast fastenings hard to reach once the boat is otherwise finished, a problem separate from how the lead itself is secured (above). One solution avoids threaded fastenings through the hull bottom entirely: drill and tap directly into the top face of each lead pig, then bolt on a small plate that itself bolts down into the top of the chine log. The fit does not clamp the lead rigidly, but shows no sign of the pigs shifting over time, and is expected to hold even if the boat were inverted. Ballast should not be fixed down at all until after the first few sails, since moving individual pigs around while trimming is the normal way a Paradox gets balanced correctly — see [the practical, real-world answer to how much ballast to fit, and where](#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) above for the same launch-light-then-trim method.[28][29]

That same arrangement — top wooden retaining pieces bolted through into a wooden block glued to the floor near the mid-section, and separately into the chine log — lets the pigs be shifted fore and aft as needed, but leaves the real access difficulty at the two bolts hidden under the flap that projects from the ballast-tank top. A **removable plank** in place of that flap was proposed as the fix, rather than continuing to work around a fixed flap.[30]

Cutting **1 in access holes** directly above the bolt heads, as an alternative to a full removable plank, was considered and rejected: fixed side pieces at that scale would still make the bolts themselves ticklish to reach, and would also block off the water inlet/outlet fitting where a PVC tube joins the copper pipe with a jubilee clip — a joint that may itself need attention at some point. Weighing routine ballast access and adjustment against that pipe-joint maintenance need, the preferred solution is to build the **sides of the water-ballast tank top as separate, removable "planks"** rather than a fixed flap with only small drilled holes — giving full access to both the lead ballast and the jubilee-clipped plumbing whenever needed.[31][32]

## Sourcing lead cheaply, and why scrap has largely replaced it

Buying new lead at retail scrap-metal prices is expensive enough (around $0.45/lb quoted across several US tire shops, which routinely take old wheel weights) that builders have looked for cheaper alternatives. Old car-battery lead has been floated as one option, with the observation that UK councils and scrapyards typically charge to dispose of batteries, so a boatbuilder asking around a garage workshop for old batteries would likely be given them for free; breaking batteries open to recover the lead carries real hazards (the sulfuric acid electrolyte, corrosive enough to serve as an effective weed killer) and calls for common-sense precautions.[33]

In practice, the economics of battery and scrap-metal recycling have shifted enough to close off this route in many places: household recycling centers now often take used engine oil, batteries, and scrap steel or iron for free, reselling it on to industrial recyclers rather than paying the person dropping it off — removing the incentive to break batteries open at all, and leaving the health risk (both to the builder and to household members nearby) with no offsetting reward. Facing the same high retail lead price, the alternative settled on was **scrap steel plate**, sourced cheaply through an aerospace-industry surplus outlet rather than recovered lead.[34]

## Ballast material choice: lead against concrete, iron, and other alternatives

Lead remains the ideal fixed-ballast material as drawn in the plans, and is straightforward to cast into pigs using tins of the kind used for baking bread as ready-made moulds.[35] Whether a mould benefits from a release-agent coating first — waterglass and drywall joint compound have both been raised as candidates, potentially doing double duty against scorching — was raised as an open question, unanswered on record.[154] The built-in, epoxy-lined **water ballast tanks** the plans otherwise call for are, by contrast, "a real bind" to build well, with their vents and fill/empty pipes adding real construction time; a more versatile alternative is loose water containers stowed in the same space, which can be swapped out for other heavy gear as needed rather than being permanently plumbed — see [Aussie Paradox](/boats/aussie-paradox.md) for a built boat using this approach.[35]

For a builder without lead on hand, the case against substituting **concrete** comes down to density: lead weighs a little over **11 times** the weight of water for a given volume, well ahead of iron pigs (originally estimated at about 2.5 times the volume of lead needed for the same ballast weight, later corrected to **somewhat less than twice** that volume), rebar-reinforced concrete (roughly **3 times** the volume), and plain concrete (under **3 times** the density of water, needing almost **4 times** the volume of lead for the same weight) — a real cost in stowage space on a boat as tightly packaged as Paradox, even before accounting for keeping the center of gravity in the same place. For comparison, gold runs about **13 times** the density of water and platinum about **21 times**, though neither is a remotely practical ballast material.[36][36a]

**Sourcing lead as wheel-balancing weights** remains a practical, low-cost route: tire shops routinely have buckets of them, and while asking for scrap lead by the pound may draw an odd look, most shops are willing to part with it. Melting the weights down into pigs (a bread tin serving as an improvised mould again) is not difficult in practice — one builder reported converting roughly 1,000 lb of scrap lead into 10 lb pigs this way, done outdoors or in a well-ventilated space, "a relatively pleasant job... with some cool beer nearby."[36]

Water carried as ballast is itself a consumable worth accounting for separately — typically another **150 lb or so** when the tanks are full — so a builder substituting loose containers for less than the full designed water volume should factor that difference into the total. Whatever the ballast material, the overriding rule is to keep the **center of gravity** close to where the design puts it (the plans call for **70-180 kg, or 150-400 lb, of lead**) at the same overall weight; more ballast generally helps sailing and stability, at the cost of the cruising stores the boat can carry before becoming overloaded.[36a]

A further real-world data point on the lead-versus-concrete tradeoff: **79 cast-concrete bricks at 5 lb each**, just under 400 lb total, were bought at a garden store for about $20 — well under lead's going scrap-metal price of roughly $1/lb, itself risen once tire shops stopped giving old wheel weights away for environmental reasons. Concrete was judged to share about the same center of gravity as the plans' own lead specification, but not the same **gyrational inertia** — the lighter, less dense material expected to roll the boat faster than lead ballast would, a trade-off accepted deliberately, with a reserve of salvaged lead billets from an earlier boat project kept on hand to shift later for trim. See [boats/ironsides.md — a ballast correction: concrete bricks, not lead, and a gyrational trade-off](/boats/ironsides.md#a-ballast-correction-concrete-bricks-not-lead-and-a-gyrational-trade-off) for the fuller build account.[37]

Launched with that ballast aboard, the boat tacked cleanly and, in the builder's own judgment, sailed as "a good sailboat should" for the wind on hand that day — though he judged the total might not prove enough in a stronger breeze, an open question left for heavier air to answer, while the boat's actual weight at launch, 395 lb, matched the 79-brick estimate above almost exactly. See [boats/ironsides.md — the launch](/boats/ironsides.md#the-launch-15-october-2008) for the fuller first-sail account.[38]

A follow-up exchange added supply-side detail and a technique note to the concrete-versus-lead comparison above. By 2008, sourcing lead as scrap wheel weights (the low-cost route described above) had grown harder: tire shops were shifting to steel weights for environmental reasons and no longer parting with the lead they still had on hand, itself fetching upward of $1/lb where available — leaving a builder still collecting his own ballast lead a few pounds at a time to weigh pouring cement or scrap-steel blocks instead, per George Buehler's *Backyard Boatbuilding*, though judging that method likely harder to move or remove later than loose, replaceable bricks.[39] Answering with his own concrete-brick solution in full: 80 pre-cast bricks bought at Lowe's for $0.25 each, 5 lb apiece — 400 lb of ballast for $20 total, enough to launch right away — held in place with small wooden blocks and wedges cut to keep them from shifting, with a plan to reuse the bricks elsewhere in the yard later if lead and money ever came together.[40] See [boats/ironsides.md — sourcing ballast on a budget](/boats/ironsides.md#sourcing-ballast-on-a-budget-concrete-bricks-against-scarce-pricier-lead) for the exchange in full.

The same thread drew two further alternatives. One reply reasoned that if ballast is going to cost real money anyway, it may as well buy something with a second, heavier purpose — a dismantleable storm anchor of the kind Little Cruiser carries, or a good battery — rather than plain dead weight bought for no other reason; the same builder's own ballast had meanwhile come down to about 36 kg (75 lb) of lead, after a folding bicycle became his boat's last big addition of gear.[41] A separate builder answered the same thread with **steel weight-lifting plates** as his own settled ballast material, chosen specifically to avoid keeping toxic lead near a young child aboard — bulkier than lead for the same weight, but readily available in a range of sizes and easy to rearrange and re-secure for trim.[42] See [boats/ironsides.md — the same thread continued](/boats/ironsides.md#sourcing-ballast-on-a-budget-concrete-bricks-against-scarce-pricier-lead) for the fuller exchange.

## Improvised and exotic ballast materials: gym weights, sandbags, and tungsten

Old gym weight-lifting plates have been floated as a possible cheap ballast source — cast iron in most modern production, though older equipment was sometimes lead — worth checking at high-school gyms, pawn shops, and secondhand sporting-goods stores.[43] For a builder without ready access to lead, any denser-than-water material works as ballast at a cost in stowage space: gold would do even better than lead (see the density comparison above), but where lead itself is too costly or hard to find, a simple, non-permanent alternative is heavy plastic bags filled with beach sand once the boat is grounded, used to trim her down onto her lines for the rest of a trip — the boat only performs correctly once brought down to her lines, whatever the ballast is made of.[44] Tungsten sits at the exotic end of the density scale, **19.25 g/cm³** against lead's **11.34 g/cm³**, and can be bought for **$15-35 per pound** in bulk — a curiosity rather than a practical ballast material at that price.[45] A further real-world density comparison, drawn from a builder's own non-Paradox small-boat ballast: cast iron runs to roughly **450 lb per cubic foot** against concrete's roughly **150 lb per cubic foot**, with firebrick floated as another possible ballast material worth considering.[46][47] Scrap steel salvaged from a railroad-track pile has also served as ballast, in slabs weighing roughly **50 lb apiece**.[176]

Lead sourcing is harder still outside North America and Europe: a South African builder unable to get any locally drew a bare suggestion of **scrap steel bars** in its place.[198] Barbell weights, floated above as a possible cheap source, have a real maintenance cost of their own once actually installed: [Raffles](/boats/raffles.md) carries them as her fixed ballast, easy to add or remove, but after more than a decade in the bilge they had begun to rust — bead-blasted clean and finished in **powder coat**, they came up looking as good as new for about **$250** total.[199]

## The ballast pig layout, in full (Don Elliott)

A dedicated, illustrated account of a lead-ballast installation — the fullest single record of how the individual lead weights, or **"pigs,"** are sized, placed, and secured — appears in the "Building the Paradox" [booklet](/references/building-the-paradox-articles.md) series.[48][49]

**Quantity and trim.** The plans specify **70 to 180 kg (155-400 lb)** of ballast, the amount within that range depending on how heavily the boat will otherwise be stored: a light traveler should use the higher figure, a heavily stored boat the lower one, and in no case should total displacement (hull, ballast, stores, gear, and crew together) exceed the design figure. A light-traveling build chose the **maximum, 180 kg (400 lb)**. Ballast location affects trim, but Paradox is heavy enough as a design to be fairly insensitive to it — only slight relocation of individual pigs is typically needed to trim her once afloat.

**Pig sizing and layout.** Pigs weighing **18 kg (40 lb) each** are one workable size, though smaller pigs can be cast for a lower total ballast figure. A suggested layout (see `http://www.tomah.com/delliott/ballast2.jpg`) places **three pigs each side at the forward end of the food locker** (54 kg/120 lb per side) and **two more pigs each side forward of that, by the water tanks** (36 kg/80 lb per side) — 180 kg (400 lb) total between those two rows alone, with the remainder positioned afterward based on the boat's actual floating trim. All pigs should sit **as far outboard as possible**. Lead should never be handled bare-handed, uncovered, and hands should be washed well afterward regardless.

**Isolating lead from the hull structure.** Anything bearing directly and permanently on Paradox's floor or plywood structure needs to be isolated from it to prevent rot; a thin rubber mat under the ballast is one way to add that precaution beyond the interior's epoxy coating. **Two coats of epoxy** on all interior surfaces is generally adequate, except where moisture could stand — such as at the bulkhead/floor joint, which warrants an epoxy fillet plus at least **four coats total** of epoxy (any more being wasted). Don't overlook Paradox's drain scuppers, shown on the plans, when coating.

**A fiberglass floor barrier under the pigs.** Beneath the ballast pigs, two layers of **fiberglass cloth** make a quick, abrasion-resistant water barrier (see `http://www.tomah.com/delliott/ballast1.jpg`) — sand the floor first and mark the cloth's footprint in advance, since that mark becomes hard to find once the cloth is wet. Two ways to wet the cloth out: lay it directly on the floor and brush epoxy onto the top surface (epoxy penetrates two layers of cloth without difficulty), or the neater alternative of wetting both layers out on a sheet of glass first and then lifting and laying the wet assembly onto the floor as a unit — wastes a little more epoxy but leaves a smoother finish; either way, watch for drips while moving a wet layup.

**A favorite epoxy-mixing container.** A square plastic mixing container works well for epoxy generally: its corners give accurate pouring, its low, wide, flat base makes it more stable and less tippy than a cup, and its wide top lets a stir stick or brush rest across it without tipping over. It is reusable — any epoxy that dries inside lifts out afterward as a single hardened film, leaving a clean container, unlike disposable paper cups.

A follow-up **"Ballast Installation part 2"** article covers the pigs' actual installation, technique, and required tools.[49]

## Ballast placement: outboard vs. centered

An open question concerns why the lead ballast sits **outboard** (against the chines/bottom at the sides) rather than **on the centerline**: with outboard ballast, a rolling motion could set up a **torque/oscillation problem**, whereas centered ballast with buoyancy further outboard (i.e. in the topsides/chines) would let the boat "pivot with the ballast" while the outboard buoyancy helps stabilize it — the opposite of the Paradox's actual arrangement. Possible explanations: the single, centered water tank might be a factor, there might be a stability gain not obvious at first glance, or the placement might simply not matter much. This is a distinct question from the already-documented **quantity** and **installation method** of the lead (above) — it concerns lateral **placement** of the ballast mass within the hull section. See also the related [sailing characteristics and rig balance](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md) question.[50]

A related, narrower question: whether the space between bulkheads #2 and #3, outside the water tanks and below the floorboards/side bins, is where the lead pigs are stowed, and whether a smaller gel-cell battery could go there on each side instead of one larger battery in the storeroom — a **fore-and-aft placement** question, distinct from the lateral outboard-vs-centered one above. See also [interior bins and hinged floor](/components/interior.md) for the battery stowage **behind the port bin panel**, further forward, between bulkheads #1 and #2.[51]

One full, worked ballast layout on a boat under construction: 11 kg (roughly 23 lb) lead pigs distributed either side of the centreline, including a pig against bulkhead 2 balancing a 12v 55Ah battery stowed to port at the same bulkhead; two lidded compartments against bulkhead 3, each taking up to 22-30 kg of lead; anchors and chain (about 20 kg) further aft, alongside space for 30 litres of water; food storage bins running from bulkhead 2 to 3; and the crew member's own weight seated or lying between bulkheads 2 and 4 — for a centerline total, battery included, of roughly 66 kg before counting anchors, water, stores, or crew.[52]

The practical, real-world answer to how much of that ballast to fit, and where, is to under-ballast at first rather than commit to a final figure on paper: launch with an empty hull, load her with all cruising gear, then add lead until she balances level to her designed waterline before permanently mounting the ballast in place. Builders generally start on the light side, which usefully shows the chine runners' true performance as ballast is added later and the boat is brought down progressively closer to her final lines. The illustrated ballast articles (above) remain the fullest reference for the installation itself once the total and placement are settled this way.[53][54] This is the same load-then-trim method used on "Zoë."[55]

A 2009 variant on the same doctrine reversed the polarity: rather than launching light and adding ballast until right, one account recommended launching **heavily overballasted** and then **removing** weight progressively until the boat performs well, arriving at the same empirically-tuned endpoint from the opposite direction — offered specifically to a builder scaling the design up far beyond any figure discussed above. See [design/larger-paradox.md — a far larger, unsanctioned build](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-far-larger-unsanctioned-build-doubling-every-stock-dimension-for-a-live-aboard) for the fuller exchange.[56]

The same lateral-placement question recurred in 2006, prompted by a plan to use reclaimed sealed lead-acid telecoms batteries (roughly 70-90 kg) as combined electric-outboard power and ballast, and puzzled that the plans put the lead outboard toward the sides rather than on the centerline, seemingly leaving half the ballast outboard of the center of buoyancy when heeled.[57] Builder experience answered the question the same way as before: only the **fore-and-aft** position of the lead has ever needed adjustment in practice; nobody has found reason to question its **lateral** placement.[58] For an electric outboard's battery bank specifically, mounting it immediately forward of bulkhead #3, embedded in the side locker, was suggested as workable.[58] This 2006 recurrence follows an earlier, 2004-2005 proposal along the same lines; see [gel-cell batteries as ballast, an electric outboard, and the Bird Watcher II precedent](#gel-cell-batteries-as-ballast-an-electric-outboard-and-the-bird-watcher-ii-precedent) below.

A distinct **naval-architecture explanation** settled the underlying physics: splitting the ballast evenly either side of the centerline, spaced about equally from it, keeps the **center of gravity of the lead itself centered** regardless of the split — so the ballast still acts as dead weight holding the boat down on her lines, letting the **hull form** supply stability, rather than acting like movable live ballast shifted tack to tack (which would actually require the weight to move). A secondary effect of spacing the ballast outboard is a slight **damping of rolling motion**; weight in the extreme ends (bow/stern) slows a boat down, but since the athwartship spacing involved here is small, the performance cost of splitting the lead outboard-versus-centered is negligible.[59] This is the fullest technical answer on record to the outboard-vs-centered question raised earlier in this section, and it resolves the question in favor of the design as drawn: even split, outboard ballast behaves for practical purposes like centered ballast.

**An intuitive illustration of the physics.** A simple test makes the outboard-versus-centered question tangible without any calculation: hold a roughly 4 ft dowel with a matched pair of weights first bunched together at its centre and pivoted up and down, then moved out to the two ends and pivoted again — the same total weight clearly resists the motion far more once spread to the ends, the same **moment-of-inertia** effect that governs how ballast spread across a Paradox's beam behaves compared with ballast concentrated on the centerline.[60] The same demonstration recurred using two lead bricks in place of the dowel: spreading them apart from the centreline leaves their combined centre of gravity unchanged, so the righting moment stays the same whether the ballast sits at the centreline or spread to the sides, while the increased moment of inertia takes more energy to set the boat rolling — the wider the ballast is spread, the more comfortable the ride.[61][62]

A further account of the same roll-damping effect draws a sharper distinction between what spreading ballast **fore-and-aft** does and what spreading it **athwartship** does: weight spread lengthwise reduces a boat's tendency to pitch — an advantage in calm water, where less energy is lost to pitching and the boat sails faster, but a drawback in rough water, where too little pitch leaves her wetter and slower — while weight spread across the beam, as Paradox's ballast is, should instead slow the **roll**. Paradox's well-known resistance to rolling, especially running downwind, is often credited to the chine runners, but ballast placement may play a part in it too, by the same **radius-of-gyration** principle a figure skater uses to speed up a spin by pulling the arms in.[63] The complementary **fore-and-aft** rule of thumb is to keep ballast near the boat's longitudinal centre, preserving buoyancy at the bow and stern for a sea-kindly motion — a principle the design already follows as drawn, with fixed lead and centreline-located water ballast alike needing no further adjustment once fitted per plan.[64]

A 2016 exchange, prompted by a newcomer's proposal to add lead specifically into the gap between the chine runner and the hull side to improve self-righting, restated the same principle once more, this time from firsthand sailing experience rather than theory alone: placing ballast further outboard can improve the boat's dynamic roll characteristics and "feel," but does not by itself change the **static righting moment**, since an outboard weight on one side is offset by an equal heeling moment on the other — the righting moment depends on the ballast's total weight and how low it sits, not on how far athwartships it is spread, so long as the placement stays symmetrical. Once actually capsized, the only thing that matters is that the heavier side ends up on top, balanced awkwardly on the boat's own curved deck rather than resting flat.[184][185][186]

A further 2016 recurrence, from a builder about to smelt 400 lb of lead into ingots, put the fore-and-aft question in blunter practical terms: the plans call for most or all of the lead immediately aft of bulkhead 3, but scanning the archive turned up some builders who appeared to have placed some of it forward of bulkhead 3 too. Building with no outboard motor and using water ballast, with a 40 lb deep-cycle battery stowed just forward of bulkhead 2, the plan was to add a heavy anchor and chain forward of bulkhead 2 to starboard specifically to balance the battery — the same battery-and-lead cross-balancing pattern already recorded for "Zoë" above.[187]

A reply treated ballast placement as adjustable rather than settled once and for all: move it — along with the motor, stores, and crew — until the boat trims properly, sails well, and tacks successfully, adding that shifting crew weight itself forward when changing tack has also been used as a further trim aid.[188] A settled real-world answer to the same question came from "Little Jim": her lead sits forward of bulkhead 3, though it moved around before settling there, having at one point been bolted to bulkhead 4 instead; with no outboard fitted, her anchors sit either side of bulkhead 4 and her battery next to bulkhead 3, the important thing being to get the trim right as shown on the plans, adjusting the ballast to compensate as heavier equipment has been added over time.[189]

A further worked example, from a boat carrying a full water ballast, close to 400 lb of lead, and an outboard mounted off-centre on the port stern: one 32 lb lead block was simply left out of the port side to offset the outboard's own weight there, balancing the boat's lateral trim without any calculation beyond matching one weight against another. See [people/kreig-missouri.md](/people/kreig-missouri.md#ballast-an-outboard-and-tacking) for the boat's fuller ballast layout.[200]

**Built-in water tanks versus separate portable containers.** The same 2006 lateral-ballast question also came with a plan to do without the plans' built-in water tanks, worried they would become a hidden **rot trap**, and to carry a smaller quantity in shallow portable plastic containers instead, doubling as part of the ballast.[57] The practical objection to that substitution is finding **containers shallow enough** to fit the tank space: cans lying on their sides leak through their tops unless those are guaranteed watertight, and a flexible bladder of the kind sold inside boxed wine was floated as a possible alternative, with metal or concrete ballast taking the tank space instead and drinking water kept separately in ordinary containers elsewhere.[65] A New Zealand chandlery's rigid plastic tank was suggested as a stock-sized option to fit the space,[66] but on checking the actual dimensions it proved both too wide and too high for the built-in tank location.[67] Two further practical answers were offered for avoiding the built-in tanks: reusable 2-litre carbonated-drink bottles (as already used successfully on a different small boat), with online camping-supply stores suggested as a further source of purpose-made water containers;[68] and a 5-litre rigid container kept topped up for ready use and refilled from two or three 10-litre roll-up containers stowed under the floor beneath the seat — using flexible, collapsible containers rather than rigid cans to sidestep the leak-prone-cap and wrong-shape problems raised above.[69]

**A 2013 recurrence: converting the tanks to storage rather than water.** The same substitution was pushed a step further by a sailor who lives aboard for stretches of up to 20 days without reprovisioning and already avoids putting drinking water in the built-in tanks at all — wary the epoxy lining might taint it, and that the ballast effect would diminish predictably as it was drunk down — keeping instead 30 litres of water in two 10-litre containers, one aft and one forward, with the rest in smaller bottles tucked into odd corners. Since the roughly 70 litres of tank space could equally well hold food, water, anchors, chain, and tools, converting the tanks to storage outright was floated as a way to gain that space without cutting the actual ballast weight, provided the loose gear substituted for it added up to the same total.[165] A reply questioned whether that total had in fact been weighed against the volume of water the tanks would otherwise have held[166] — the arithmetic being straightforward, since 70 litres of water weighs 70 kg, a figure that chain, bottled water, and canned food would need to reach collectively to replace outright; [Petite Lady](/boats/petite-lady.md), built with no water ballast at all and her stores kept under the floor in its place, was offered as a working precedent that the substitution is workable.[167] The one real cost identified was to the tanks' second role as a rain-catchment reservoir (see [provisioning and water](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md#self-flooding-sea-water-ballast-tanks-feasible-but-risky)): pulling out the plumbing would give up the ability to replenish drinking water from rain, though that ability could be kept even after a conversion by disconnecting the fill hoses at their lower ends and using them directly to refill water bottles instead.[168]

**A further December 2013 exchange: trailer-loading tradeoffs and a sand-ballast-bag alternative.** Converting the tanks from water to fixed ballast increases trailer loading; water ballast's own tradeoffs run the other way — the plumbing is complicated, and drinking water carried in the tanks is lost as ballast as it is consumed, at roughly 11 times the stowage volume of lead for the same weight, though it does keep the trailer weight down. Drinking water is better kept in several small containers (2- and 4-pint bottles) than one large one, so that any contamination is confined to a container or two that can simply be thrown away.[169] One owner's own built-in tanks are already full of sea water and overloading the boat; converting them to storage would let existing stores, anchors, tools, food, and drinking water move to the lowest point near the centre of the boat for better sailing stability — trailer weight unchanged, sailing displacement reduced, a "Good Thing" for an already-overloaded boat.[170] A further principle was raised in the same exchange: stability is not the only thing ballast is for, since Paradox also needs to be down on her lines to avoid excessive leeway, her hull itself acting as her keel.[171] A distinct movable-ballast alternative was offered alongside it: since sand weighs about 100 lb per cubic foot against water's 65, sand-and-watertight bags sized to weigh about 20 lb each when filled could be made up in advance, filled just before launch, stowed after launch to trim the boat, and dumped again while waiting to use the ramp — made the right diameter, a bag's own lid then helps hold it in place.[172] The same exchange also produced a tangential chine-runner-material suggestion — making the runners themselves out of lead, with something added inside a double-planked bottom — raised without further discussion; see [chine-runners: how they work](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) for the established reasoning on runner material and shape.[173]

**A January 2014 close to the same exchange.** Asked directly why one boat had gone back to wet ballast after trying the tanks a different way, the answer given a few weeks later filled out the picture: the tanks were never really used for storage in the first place — with all cruising gear, including a bicycle, aboard there is already plenty of empty stowage elsewhere — and the lead had only ever been an interim fix, installed at the same 70 kg weight the water ballast it replaced would have carried (and, unlike water, staying neutrally buoyant in a swamping), while reliable, genuinely watertight inspection hatches were sourced. Once found, water ballast returned for good, now run actively rather than passively — electric pumps fitted inside the tanks to empty them, a loose pump on a wire to refill — so the tanks can be pumped dry to lighten the load for a long row, refilled once sailing is possible again, and left empty to save 70 kg on the trailer. Full text and citation also at [boats/little-jim.md](/boats/little-jim.md#a-return-to-water-ballast-for-easier-handling-out-of-the-water-by-2010).[174][175]

**A 2016 recurrence: water ballast abandoned outright for stowage.** The same substitution recurred once more, this time as an accomplished fact rather than a proposal: a boat not built by its own owner had its water ballast done away with entirely, freeing roughly 70 litres of space for heavy stores in its place — the same figure as the stock tank capacity established above. Full text and citation also at [components/interior.md](/components/interior.md#storage-capacity-after-fit-out-firsthand-inventories-and-small-stowage-ideas).[190]

**A battery-in-wood caution.** Independent of placement, anyone planning to use lead-acid batteries as ballast was warned to seal their compartment unusually thoroughly — heavy epoxy and glass coating, plus frequent rinsing with plenty of water and a final neutralizing/buffering rinse — since even a trace of leaked battery acid reaching bare wood will destroy it faster than rot or insect damage.[70] Sealed batteries mounted in any orientation still carry the same risk if cracked or seriously overcharged, making a good long-term installation worth real care regardless of battery type.[71]

**A four-chamber pumped water-ballast proposal.** The stock design's fixed **70 kg (70 litre) water ballast** figure, already established above, was the baseline against which one further water-ballast idea was measured: splitting the under-floor space into **four separate chambers**, each independently fillable and drainable, for a combined 400-550 lb of adjustable ballast — filled via plumbing and a transom-mounted pump, drained by four bilge pumps, with a 2 in PVC pipe per chamber brought up to the side decks for hand-pump backup should the battery fail. The stated advantages were a lighter boat on the trailer and ballast adjustable underway, sailing or yulohing, at the cost of added plumbing complexity and lost under-floor storage. No build or trial of the proposal is recorded.[72][73]

A related trim exercise, worked through while replacing water ballast with lead in the same compartments, illustrates how many separate weights a Paradox's designer expects to balance against each other: lead in place of the water tanks, anchors under the floor behind bulkhead #3, roughly 30 kg of water beneath the floor forward of bulkhead #4, an outboard motor on the transom (~13 kg), and the crew's own weight anywhere between bulkheads #2 and #4 — a "pretty puzzle" left to be sorted empirically on launch day rather than solved on paper in advance.[74] On the fore-and-aft trim question more generally, two rules of thumb are in tension: one favors keeping the transom clear of the water to reduce drag on a flat-bottomed hull, the other favors a bow held slightly high to help her climb over waves — the only firm agreement being that sitting perfectly level is *not* the goal.[75] The physics explanation above was enough to resolve the original confusion, after "a sheet of paper covered in sketches and equations" had left the question no clearer.[76]

## Gel-cell batteries as ballast, an electric outboard, and the Bird Watcher II precedent

An earlier, independent proposal considered pairing a stretched Paradox (a 15-20% wider hull, extra beam used for wider side bins) with **deep-cycle gel batteries** in place of some of the lead ballast normally carried forward and aft of bulkhead #3 — the batteries doubling as a power source for an **electric outboard**, autohelm, and radio. The batteries-for-ballast logic holds up: fitting an outboard as electric lets the batteries substitute for some lead. An autohelm is a different matter, since Paradox was designed to sail without constant attention to the helm in the first place — a course check roughly every five minutes, as part of ordinary look-out routine, is normal practice and needs no device to do it automatically.[77][78]

The one recorded field data point on an electric outboard aboard a Paradox: a **35 lb thrust** electric motor aboard "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)" pushed her along quite well, though no GPS was aboard at the time to log an actual speed.[79]

A later, independent case for battery-ballast worked the numbers through a different example design (Phil Bolger's "Old Shoe," a small square daysailer needing roughly **200 lb of ballast**): two or three deep-cycle batteries at 12V/100 amp-hours apiece (so, say, 24V at 100 amp-hours) could substitute for that lead outright, and — kept within a safe long-term 20-amp discharge rate — deliver about **480 watts (a bit over 1/2 hp) continuously for 5 hours**, or up to roughly 1 hp for short bursts, more than enough for the 1-3 day sails most owners actually make, plus quiet running near wildlife and enough reserve to run a small fan overnight in buggy climates. The batteries-for-ballast case was judged sound in principle, with the recharging question — staying independent of marinas without room aboard for a generator — as the real, unresolved practical obstacle.[80][81]

Phil Bolger's own **Bird Watcher II** design confirms the battery-ballast concept has already been built into a real boat: she uses batteries for ballast and stability, and carries enough solar panel area to keep them charged except during extended heavy use. Bird Watcher II looks considerably larger than Bolger's "Old Shoe," but is in fact easier to trailer and launch, and her extra deck area is there specifically to carry enough solar panels. She is also designed to row, and can be driven by a yuloh or by a 25 lb, 2 hp four-stroke outboard stowed in a waterproof nacelle.[82]

This 2004-2005 exchange is the earlier of two recorded electric-outboard-battery-ballast proposals; for the 2006 recurrence using reclaimed sealed lead-acid telecoms batteries, see [ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) above, and for a further, independent 2013 proposal along the same lines, see [auxiliary outboard power — a further 2013 exchange](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#a-further-2013-exchange-an-owners-own-generator-research-and-a-solar-charging-feasibility-check). For an electric outboard's own selection, mounting, and use — as distinct from its batteries' ballast role — see [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md#electric-outboards-and-batteries-doing-double-duty-as-ballast).

## A 2009 revisit: an in-service battery, and sizing figures

The same batteries-for-ballast idea recurred once more in November 2009, raised independently by a reader still working through the archive, drawn by how heavy batteries already are and by the marine electric-propulsion options now on the market, while still expecting some lead pigs would be needed on hand to fine-tune trim.[83] By then the idea was already a going installation rather than a proposal: one boat carries an **80Ah AGM battery, about 12 kg,** fitted forward of bulkhead 3 in the gap between the water tank and the hull side, driving a small trolling motor good for roughly **8 miles at 2 knots** (stowed inboard between uses, kept clear of the compass) and charging the boat's other electronics besides — called on only occasionally, since a yuloh handles most short-distance work, but valued when needed.[84]

A caution followed: batteries stand only **8-10 in** high, and the main ballast mass needs to sit near the boat's midsection rather than be split forward and aft, since splitting weight that way would reduce the bow's and stern's ability to rise to a wave — workable on some boats, in this view, but not judged ideal for a microcruiser.[85] A further reply suggested the smaller-format deep-cycle batteries built for mobility scooters might suit a microcruiser's tighter space better than full-size units — the kind used aboard a larger sharpie, where extra battery capacity backs up solar power in the absence of an onboard generator, with the outboard's own charging capacity kept in reserve rather than run routinely.[86] Worked sizing figures followed for that smaller format: roughly **16-18Ah cells run about 6-7 in square and only 3 in thick**, so ten of them (five per side), each weighing around **14 lb**, would mount flat and low for a combined **140 lb** of ballast.[87]

The installed 12 kg figure for the 80Ah battery was then challenged as implausibly light,[88] and traced back to a direct substitution: it had replaced one of the boat's own existing 12 kg (25 lb) lead ballast bricks, and a side-by-side comparison of the two at the time had borne the figure out by eye.[89]

## Independent weight and displacement estimates

Several independent weight estimates for real Paradoxes and near-sisters converge on a **designed displacement of 1,400 lb**, while showing real boats vary well below and somewhat above that figure depending on build weight and how fully stored/ballasted they are:

- **A detailed loaded breakdown** — the ~1,365 lb figure (above), on a heavily built hull.
- **A trailering figure** — a fully loaded weight of around **410 kg** (~900 lb), well below both a ~650 kg bare figure quoted elsewhere and the ~1,365 lb (≈620 kg) loaded figure above. See [trailering — tow weight and vehicle capacity](/operations/trailering.md) for the full discussion.[90]
- **"Zoë," estimated** — empty weight with ballast on the order of **650 lb**, possibly as high as 750 lb; loaded (a month's stores, full water tanks) roughly **900 lb**; **designed displacement 1,400 lb**. An intention to actually weigh her was never recorded as carried out.[91][92][93]
- **"Little Jim," as-weighed** — weighed with the glassing finished and cabin construction just beginning: just under **600 lb**, meaning roughly half of the finished design displacement would still be added as stores, ballast, and crew. This is the only recorded weigh-in of a hull at this specific stage.[94]
- **Little Cruiser** — the boat weighed **1,000–1,100 lb partially loaded** when about to be trailered, but climbs to roughly **1,600 lb** once loaded with all cruising stores. The practical answer for Paradox matched the design figure: "Paradox should displace around 1400 lbs when cruising for maximum performance," warning that a lighter-than-design boat (around 1,000 lb) would let the chine runners "not dig in as deeply," hurting her ability to carry sail.[95]
- **A restated design-displacement figure, in full sailing trim.** The most direct answer on record to "how much does a Paradox weigh, loaded and unloaded": design displacement in full sailing trim is **1,410 lb (640 kg)**; subtracting the crew and **70 litres (70 kg) of water ballast** gives the boat's own trailering weight — a figure closely matching the 1,400 lb design displacement already established above.[96][97][93]
- **"BRIS Paradox," as-weighed.** The foam-sandwich "BRIS Paradox" hull was weighed complete except for mast, boom, and yard, battery, and other small gear, but **including its 70 kg bronze bottom plate** (serving as both chine-runner shoe and ballast): a total of **326 kg** — heavier than expected, though the Divinycell-cored "ParaPlastic" construction likely displaces on the order of 100 kg more than an equivalent plywood hull owing to the core's own thickness.[98] See [the "BRIS Paradox"](/boats/bris-paradox.md) for the full build.
- **"Scout," as her owner stated it in print** — **1,460 lb designed displacement**, given in a magazine account of a 50-mile engine-less sail to explain why she is "not the lightest girl at the dance, nor the ultimate paddle/yuloh machine," and offered as the reason hand propulsion cost real effort while still being possible at all. It is the highest of the owner-stated figures and sits some 50 lb above the 1,410 lb design figure above; no working is given for it, so it is best read as one owner's rounded understanding of the design number rather than as a measurement of his own boat. See [Scout](/boats/scout.md).[181]

What "design displacement" itself means is worth stating precisely: a quoted displacement figure (such as the 1,410 lb/640 kg above) represents the boat floating at her designed waterline, not the full volume of water she would displace if entirely swamped. **Displacement** is the weight of the craft at her designed waterline, while **"light ship"** — an underused but useful term — is her empty weight; the difference between the two is her useful load (people, stores, and gear).[99][100]

A 2019 exchange restated the same distinction with a concrete cruising-weight example. Asked directly whether the widely quoted **1,410 lb (640 kg)** figure was a loaded or a lightship number, and what lightship weight other owners were actually getting, the reply confirmed that figure is the boat's own finished weight fully loaded with water and lead ballast — not her lightship weight — and described a practice of not reducing the lead to compensate for stores taken aboard, canned food included; carrying provisions this way commonly puts long-distance cruising weight at around **1,600 lb**, matching the loaded figure already recorded for Little Cruiser above.[195][196] A further reply broke the same 1,410 lb figure down concretely — reached only once lead, water, bodies, gear, and stores are all added to the bare hull — and, as a lighter-than-average sailor who needs more lead of his own to bring her down to her marks, put his own empty-hull guess at under 1,000 lb, never having actually weighed it.[197]

### Weight versus displacement

By Archimedes' principle, a floating boat always displaces exactly her own weight, including crew and stores — she can displace less than she weighs by sinking, but never more.[101] For a displacement boat like Paradox this means the weight of water displaced always equals the weight of the boat and everything in her, a distinction naval architects draw between a boat's own **"liveweight"** (the boat itself) and the **"deadweight"** of everything she carries, crew included.[102]

Matt Layden also entered the original Paradox in the 2003 WaterTribe Everglades Challenge, sailing her from and back to his home in Stuart, Florida — see [original Paradox — cruising record](/boats/original-paradox.md#cruising-record) for the full race writeup.[94]

### An unanswered 2013 question: hull weight, rigged

A request for a ballpark figure for a Paradox hull's weight — rigged, but without stores, ballast, water, or ground tackle aboard — went unanswered on record.[164] The closest figures already established above are Don Elliott's own boat, whose hull alone (excluding the rig) comes to roughly 400 lb, with mast and miscellaneous gear above deck adding a further 35 lb, and "Little Jim," weighed at just under 600 lb with glassing finished but cabin construction only just beginning — not a like-for-like comparison, since neither figure isolates the same "hull plus rig only" combination asked for.

## Rebalancing ballast for a heavier outboard

Fitting a **4 hp four-stroke outboard, weighing 65 lb**, to a Paradox is heavier than ideal for the boat, though workable. One documented setup: an existing ballast of **400 lb in 25 lb blocks**, positioned **between bulkheads #2 and #3**, with most of the weight already **shifted aft, close to bulkhead #3**. This is a second, distinct outboard-power data point alongside [auxiliary outboard power](/operations/auxiliary-outboard-power.md) (a smaller 2 hp Evinrude considered for conversion to external fuel, and a separate, unanswered question about whether a similar outboard fitted to Little Cruiser had proved unsatisfactory), and the first case where a poor-windward-performance complaint — a boat that "doesn't want to tack or sail to windward" — is tied explicitly to a **ballast-placement** concern rather than to rig trim, chine runners, or crew technique.[103][104]

**Diagnosis: too much weight forward causes "weathervaning."** One diagnosis holds that if the ballast (or other weight) sits **too far forward**, the boat will **"weathervane"** — turn to point into the wind — matching the symptom above. (That boat's ballast was in fact reported mostly shifted **aft**, not forward, so the diagnosis does not fully reconcile with the layout given.)[105]

**Center of gravity on the plans.** The plans give a ballast **quantity** and general **stowage location** (between bulkheads #2 and #3, in the bottom) but no explicit center-of-gravity figure to check against — a detail easy to overlook (see [mast rake](/components/rig/index.md) for a similar case of a plans detail missed at first). The answer: the boat's **CG will be very close to the area marked "C/A" at the waterline on page one of the plans** — a specific location on the plan sheet rather than a numeric offset. Whether "C/A" stands for "Centre of (wetted) Area," by analogy with how a boat's underwater lateral-plane centroid is sometimes labeled, is unconfirmed.[106][107][108]

**Worked lever-arm method for rebalancing ballast.** A general method for calculating compensating ballast, based on moment arms (lever arms) from the boat's CG:[109]

1. Establish the boat's baseline trim (CG located as intended) before adding the new weight.
2. Multiply the **added weight** by its **distance from the CG** to get its moment (e.g., adding 65 lb — matching the 4 hp outboard's weight — 7 ft from the CG gives a moment of 7 × 65 = 455 lb-ft).
3. To counteract that moment with **compensating ballast** placed a chosen distance forward of the CG, divide the moment by that distance (e.g., 455 ÷ 3 ft ≈ 152 lb of ballast needed 3 ft forward of the CG).
4. The **further forward** of the CG the compensating weight can be placed, the **less** extra ballast is needed to counteract a given moment.

This is a general "how to calculate weight shift" method rather than a Paradox-specific number, to be applied using a boat's actual outboard weight/distance and ballast-placement options.

Matt Layden's stability calculation has also been independently confirmed by naval-architecture software — see [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources).

## The designer's layered ballast: water tanks, stores, and lead together

The lead-pig discussion above reflects how *builders* on the list ballasted their boats; [microcruising.com](/references/microcruising-website.md) shows that [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own boats use a **layered** ballast scheme in which fixed lead is only one component, alongside **water-tank ballast** and **the ship's stores acting as working ballast**:

- **Water ballast.** Every one of Matt's cruising boats carries water ballast: [Paradox](/boats/original-paradox.md) is listed with **"70 liters of fresh water ballast,"** [Swamp Thing](/matt-layden/swamp-thing.md) is "fresh water ballasted," and [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) carries **30 gallons in four tanks** — "all of them used water ballast." The water is kept in **individual square cubes** fitted to the bilge (so tainted water can't spoil the whole supply, and depleting cubes gradually reduces ballast), and Little Cruiser's tanks **rain-fill automatically** through toe-rails that channel water into deck holes.[110][111]
- **Lead too.** Water is not the whole story: the 2000 Small Craft Advisor interview says Little Cruiser is "**ballasted with several hundred pounds of lead** along with an equal amount of weight in gear stored securely in the bilges" — i.e. lead *and* water *and* stores.[112]
- **Stores as ballast.** "In Matt's sharpies most of the ship's stores make up the ballast"; Little Cruiser is "designed to reach her proper cruising trim only after all the stores were aboard," with the heaviest cans stowed low and amidship — so the crew must **re-shift and top up ballast as stores are consumed** over a long cruise (see [provisioning and water](/operations/provisioning-and-water.md)).[113]

"[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" shows the same principle at work over many years: launched with about **70 kg of water plus 180 kg of lead**, her fixed lead was later cut to roughly **50–70 kg**, progressively replaced by weight that also does useful work — batteries, anchors, chain, tinned food, even a bicycle — each stowed low and doubling as ballast, exactly the stores-as-ballast approach Matt Layden's own boats use (see [AYRS meeting talk](/references/ayrs-meeting-video.md)).[114]

A December 2009 mailing-list exchange records the same progression contemporaneously, a decade before the AYRS talk retold it: replying to a prospective builder's question about how much lead to order against the plans' wide range,[147] the same owner gave his own boat's figures to date — started with about 140 kg of lead plus a further 40 kg in reserve, settled her on her lines by loading and shifting ballast after launch, and by then had already cut the fixed lead to about 50 kg as several anchors, a battery, tinned food, and a bicycle took over part of the load.[148]

A second reply to the same question, four months later, gave a different owner's own cruising load-out instead: roughly 100 kg of lead plus a battery of about 30 kg, an anchor, food and water, enough to put the boat down on her lines by the time her sailor is aboard, with no adverse effect on sailing beyond a slower response to gusts and an anchorage made extremely comfortable even where otherwise unremarkable — full text and citation also at [boats/small-fry.md](/boats/small-fry.md#citations).[152]

This layered approach helps explain the wide spread of loaded-weight figures above (a heavily-built, 400-lb-lead boat at ~1,365 lb versus the ~410 kg / ~900 lb trailering figure for a lighter boat): how much *lead* a boat carries varies, because water and stores carry much of the load. The designer also claims a striking **self-righting** result from this low, secured mass — see [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md#angle-of-vanishing-stability-160-and-165-degrees-from-independent-sources) for the 165° figure and its independent corroboration.[112]

## How a Paradox feels underloaded

An unballasted, unloaded hull rides too high to get proper directional grip from its chine runners or the stability benefit of deeper immersion, and can feel alarmingly tender as a result, independent of any flaw in the design itself. At the launch of "[Little Jim](/boats/little-jim.md)" (see [Little Jim — launched](/boats/little-jim.md) for the full account), it was impossible to tell, standing on deck and leaning out against the mast, whether the boat had reached the roughly 10 degrees of list seen in a photo of the same test done elsewhere, or something much closer to 45 degrees — she felt unpredictably tender, with no ballast, stores, or water aboard yet.[115]

This is a **known, recurring problem**: **"not enough ballast to sink the paradox to its lines. Some have declared the Paradox unsailable due to trying to sail it empty, bobbing on top with no chine leverage for directional stability and no immersion to cause stability."** This matches the design's already-documented sensitivity to loading (the hull sinks about 1 in per 200 lb, above).[116]

A corroborating, though less alarming, real-world data point comes from "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)," sailed with **no water aboard** and doing "just fine" — though [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md) remarked she looked "a little low in the water" regardless, suggesting even the designer reads a Paradox's trim by eye rather than by a fixed rule. See [Zoë — sailing with no water aboard, and a tiller stop](/boats/zoe.md) for the fuller account. A third data point comes from "[Whisper](/boats/whisper.md)": leaning out on deck the same way, "you could get the mast wet if you tried hard enough" — an experience matching Little Jim's own uncertain, possibly-45-degree estimate rather than the photographed 10 degrees seen elsewhere.[117]

Taken together, these three accounts (Little Jim, Zoë, Whisper) are a practical caution for any builder's own first time afloat, distinct from (though related to) the loaded-weight breakdowns above.

A later exchange, prompted by photographs of "Little Jim" showing how sensitive the boat looked to crew weight distribution, sharpened the underloaded-vs-fully-loaded question further. Having **never sailed without the water ballast**, and, "with some cunning use of the bathroom scales," believed to be carrying the full **1,400 lb** design displacement with one crew aboard, "Little Jim" was expected to suffer in stability if significantly lighter. Even so, in light winds (under Force 2) she could keep pace with a two-up Minisail dinghy, and leeway had never been a problem in light or strong winds alike. The conclusion: at her design weight a Paradox has to be thought of "as a yacht, not a dinghy" — the sums class her as heavy displacement, and the weight rules her out as a beach toy (a crew member would have to keep their toes clear when launching or hauling out).[118][119][120]

A second, independent data point on the light end of the same question comes from "Zoë": sailing with her water tanks empty on several outings made her **"a little more tender," but did not affect her stability that much** — out in 16 kt with about a foot of sail rolled in, "she did just fine." The practical recommendation drawn from this is to sail light rather than bother filling the tanks every time, when conditions allow.[121] A stock-tank owner's own long-run practice bears this out: the tanks get filled at home and towed full, emptied only before retrieval and sometimes not even then — "just more mucking around with no real benefit" once a permanently-ballasted boat is sailed for comparison. The tanks were designed with a Bahamas passage in mind; sailing instead where fresh water is abundant regardless removes most of the reason to bother draining and refilling them at all.[192]

A precise answer to how far "Little Jim" actually stood from her design weight at launch: "from various readings from the bathroom scales and a bit of calculation we were only about **20 kg (44 lb) lighter than the design weight**," a shortfall meant to be carried as **movable ballast** on the next outing. The "not enough ballast" diagnosis above was itself reconsidered in light of this: **"I think the main problem was just too much wind. I am not sure I could have rowed my dinghy very easily in those conditions."** This gives a precise **known-shortfall** figure for an underloaded Paradox at launch (44 lb, against a 1,400 lb design displacement) — a much smaller gap than the general "not enough ballast" framing might suggest — and complicates that diagnosis by pointing to the launch day's **force-6 wind** as at least as plausible an explanation for the uncertain stability as the ballast shortfall itself. Neither explanation is confirmed over the other; both remain plausible readings of the same launch-day account.[122]

Paradox's self-righting range, real-world knockdown-recovery behavior, and the mechanics of bailing her without opening the hatch are covered on dedicated pages: see [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md) for the angle-of-vanishing-stability figures and firsthand knockdown accounts, and [bailing and bilge pumps](/operations/bailing-and-bilge-pumps.md) for the Whale Guzzler/Gusher pump installation and how much real use it gets.

## A proposed bilge-pump reversal for filling flexible ballast tanks (2011)

A speculative technique proposed for topping up a flexible ballast tank without a dedicated fill line: reverse a Guzzler/Gusher-type manual bilge pump's own valve set to draw water IN — from, for instance, the mast-step drain — rather than only pumping bilge water out, as already installed aboard "Zoë" (see [bailing and bilge pumps](/operations/bailing-and-bilge-pumps.md#pump-installation-whale-guzzler-known-as-gusher-in-the-uk)), letting a flexible tank be filled only for those occasions when a large quantity of potable water is actually wanted rather than carried full at all times. No reply or build report settling the idea is on record.[153]

## Sandbags as ballast

Whether sandbags could substitute for Paradox's own lead-pig ballast is an open question. One unconfirmed recollection suggests stones may have been used aboard [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) to replace weight lost as stores were consumed underway, but no firsthand sandbag-ballast report for a Paradox itself is on record; compare the light-displacement [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md)'s own documented use of sandbags below.[123]

## Topping up ballast with sand and rocks as stores are consumed

A related fine-tuning technique for a cruise already under way: as heavier food rations stored under the floorboards are eaten down, sand and rocks gathered along the way can be added under the floor to replace the lost weight and keep the boat trimmed as loaded stores run out.[124]

## Ballast materials, and the light-displacement Enigma (Dave's Wiki)

A [wiki reference](/references/daves-wiki.md) tabulates ballast-material densities for a builder choosing among them on a light boat — **sand ≈ 100, steel ≈ 490, lead ≈ 708 lb per cubic foot** — the point being that a denser material takes up less precious interior floor space for the same weight. It also sharpens the Paradox↔[Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) contrast: where heavy-displacement Paradox carries its ~400 lb of fixed lead low in the bilge, the **light-displacement Enigma "relies on her crew's weight as ballast to maintain stability, and because of her light displacement she could even plane."** The Enigma 360 used **tubular sand bags secured to the floor** (~100 lb) and the Enigma 460 prototype **220 lb of steel plates plus 160 lb of sandbags**; the Enigma was later (quoted, Jan 2009) switched from bulky sandbags to compact **1-lb lead ingots in 12-lb bags** specifically to reclaim cabin space — a concrete illustration of the density trade-off above.[125]

## A laminated sheet-lead bottom as an alternative to loose pigs (speculative)

Early in a Tasmanian build, an idea was floated to build a **sheet-lead** ballast layer directly into the bottom panel — laminating a sheet of lead (of the kind used in local building construction) between a 12 mm and a 6 mm ply sheet to make up the plans' 18 mm bottom, rather than cleating loose lead pigs down inside the hull. West System's own technical staff confirmed a good bond between lead and plywood is achievable, but whether a sheet thin enough to laminate would supply the full ballast figure the plans call for was left uncalculated.[126]

A related alternative: gluing a **metal sheet as the outermost layer** of the bottom (rather than laminated inside it) would add ballast while also serving as a wear surface. **Stainless steel** — bondable directly to the hull, though difficult — was considered before settling on **bronze** as easier to glue and useful as a grounding plate for the mast's lightning protection, at the cost of being the more expensive metal of the two.[127]

Two practical objections were raised, drawing on experience ballasting "Little Jim." First, the plans' **70-180 kg lead-pig** figure (sheet 5) is not a margin for build-weight error — it exists because ballast must be **removed as stores are added** over a cruise, so at least part of any boat's total lead needs to stay easily adjustable rather than being bonded permanently in place (the identical point applies against a steel-bottomed hull; see [a steel hull bottom as combined ballast and impact protection](#a-steel-hull-bottom-as-combined-ballast-and-impact-protection-speculative) below). Second, getting a boat's **fore-and-aft trim** right in practice takes real adjustment after launching — on Little Jim it took **four outings** before the ballast was correctly placed, and only then was it bolted down permanently; a builder committing to a fixed sheet of lead bonded into the bottom would need to be "very confident" of the balance sums beforehand, since there would be no way to shift it afterward — a point taken as usefully clarifying the fore-and-aft-trim risk of a bonded-in-place ballast sheet.[128][129]

On **how much ballast a bottom-sheet approach could safely delegate to fixed weight**, Little Jim's actual, weighed figures give a real-world answer: **120 kg of lead** carried as removable pigs (96 kg — 48 kg per side — just forward of bulkhead #3, plus 24 kg further aft behind bulkhead #4), arrived at by weighing the boat, gear, and crew, then adding lead until the total reached **640 kg**. One pig from each side at bulkhead #3 would likely need pulling if stores grew toward 20 kg, with the boat's overall weight rechecked each spring as accumulated gear crept the total upward. On the underlying weekend-versus-long-tour question: in practice Little Jim had only ever been loaded for weekend-scale sailing (the longest trip, one week, still saw most meals ashore), so these figures describe that end of the range rather than a fully stored long-tour condition.[130]

No sheet-lead-bottom Paradox build, laminated internally or bonded as an outer skin, is recorded anywhere in the archive; like the [steel hull bottom idea](#a-steel-hull-bottom-as-combined-ballast-and-impact-protection-speculative) below, it remains a one-off, unbuilt proposal.

A separate, real-world data point on ballast placement comes from "Zoë"'s own trim, offered in the same exchange: no weight at all aft of bulkhead #4, but about **22 kg forward of bulkhead #2, to starboard**, placed there specifically to offset the weight of a battery on the port side — a practical illustration that ballast placement on a built Paradox is driven as much by balancing fixed equipment weight across the centerline as by the plans' overall fore-and-aft figures.[131]

## Sliding ballast on X-Y tracks (speculative)

Working only from the study plans, before any Paradox was built, one idea floated for **actively rebalancing ballast while under way**: building two intersecting box-section channels into the bilge — one running fore-and-aft, one athwartships — with a ballast-carrying box riding on **skateboard wheels** (stainless, self-lubricating, and cheap in a range of sizes) and pulled along each track by pulleys and a rope, adjustable "on the X-Y planes" with a ratchet to stop the mass rocking loose in a seaway. The arrangement was likened to an X-Y beam scale, and the same skateboard-wheel trick was suggested to equally ease the [hatch](/construction/hatch-hardware.md)'s own sliding action in place of wax. The reasoning was that the scheme might let a builder retune ballast position for different sailing conditions rather than fix it once at launch — the same fore-and-aft/outboard placement questions already worked out empirically above (see [ballast placement: outboard vs. centered](#ballast-placement-outboard-vs-centered) and [rebalancing ballast for a heavier outboard](#rebalancing-ballast-for-a-heavier-outboard)) — but solved by a mechanism rather than by unbolting and re-securing fixed pigs. No reply or build attempt is recorded in the archive, and — like the [sheet-lead bottom](#a-laminated-sheet-lead-bottom-as-an-alternative-to-loose-pigs-speculative) and [steel-bottom](#a-steel-hull-bottom-as-combined-ballast-and-impact-protection-speculative) ideas elsewhere on this page — it remains a one-off, unbuilt proposal.[132][133]

A related speculation, reasoning from greater experience windsurfing than sailing a fixed mast, proposed adapting a **windsurfer's articulated ("Universal") mast joint** to a Paradox-type hull: a canted, swept-back rig of the kind used on a traditional sharpie, married to a track at the mast foot that could adjust the rig's fore-and-aft cant, with the sail itself reefed by rolling it around the mast much as [Matt Layden's own roller-reefing sail](/components/rig/index.md#sail-shape-tacking-asymmetry-and-reefing-behavior) already works.[132]

**The answer: retrofit after launch, not before.** Both ideas did eventually draw a considered reply. On the sliding-ballast mechanism: it might work but could just as easily prove unnecessary, and — since all of a Paradox's internal woodwork except the bulkheads and tank top can be **removed in about 10 minutes** — a track system would not be too difficult to retrofit into a standard build later, once a builder could actually see the interior to work with and had confirmed the need. On the windsurfer-rig idea, the advice was the same in spirit: build the boat to the plans first, sail her, and only then decide whether an improvement is truly needed — "unless you have the standard rig to compare it with then you won't really know if you have achieved anything." That advice was accepted: a "slightly scaled up" but otherwise stock boat was the chosen path, since without a standard rig or a standard ballast arrangement to compare against, there was no real way to know whether either speculative change would actually be an improvement — "how is a person really going to know if something then is an improvement" — with the speculative ideas kept as later options rather than starting points.[134][135]

The self-righting-during-capsize logic behind the sliding-ballast idea was elaborated further, prompted by the observation that a Paradox is already self-righting from 160 degrees: a **quick-release locking mechanism** on the ballast track — for instance a ring that falls off its retaining grooves when inverted and re-engages right-side-up — could make the righting moment fire suddenly, rather than relying on the boat's existing static stability curve alone, since a mass thrown suddenly to one side rights a hull faster than the same mass sitting evenly distributed (an effect likened to a kayaker's Eskimo roll, or to standing out on a dagger board). The actual speed of any such righting effect would depend entirely on how quickly and precisely a real locking/unlocking mechanism could be built, and the whole idea remained speculative, not something to build without extensive prior testing. The exchange closed admiring the finished boats on the list (naming the "[BRIS Paradox](/boats/bris-paradox.md)" specifically) and setting the speculative ideas aside in favor of building to the plan first — with an unanswered follow-up question about the mechanics of the sink-tap water pump-out arrangement described elsewhere in the plans and about the technique for bonding copper sheet to the hull.[136][137][138][139][140]

## A steel hull bottom as combined ballast and impact protection (speculative)

An unbuilt idea floated: build the hull bottom itself from roughly **1/4 in steel** rather than plywood, folding part of the ballast into the structure to lower the center of gravity while freeing up floor storage space otherwise taken by lead pigs. The reasoning was that steel would also be far more durable against grounding on sand bars and rocks, and specifically against striking a partially submerged shipping container — the boat would more likely skid over it than be holed. An open question about bonding epoxy reliably to abraded steel, given the differing thermal expansion of dissimilar materials, went unanswered in the archive. A second question — whether ballast weight is better concentrated amidship or spread toward the ends — was answered indirectly: Paradox's 70-180 kg ballast range (sheet 5 of the plans) is not there to cover build-weight variance; it exists because ballast must be tuned to whatever stores are carried on a given trip, so a steel-bottomed Paradox would still need the same roughly **110 kg (240 lb)** of ballast a plywood boat does — folding ballast into the hull material does not remove the need to adjust it trip to trip, short of grinding away the bottom to compensate for heavy stores. See [aluminum and steel Paradox (speculative) — the fuller technical exchange](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md#gauge-weight-and-the-case-for-steel-over-wood-generally) this idea opened. No steel-bottomed Paradox build is recorded anywhere in the archive; like the [aluminum Paradox idea](/design/ideas/aluminum-construction-idea.md), it remains a one-off, unbuilt proposal — though **steel's density (≈490 lb/cu ft) sits between sand and lead** (see the ballast-material table above), making the underlying premise at least dimensionally plausible.[141][142]

A lighter-weight variant of the same idea recurred independently in October 2007: rather than build the bottom itself from steel, fix a large sheet of **galvanised steel** to the underside of the existing plywood bottom — freeing floor space otherwise taken by lead pigs, adding abrasion protection, and, with scrap and retail lead prices both high, potentially saving material cost and the epoxy that would otherwise bed loose pigs in place. The proposal floated calculating the exact steel thickness needed to match the plans' specified ballast weight, or skipping the calculation on the reasoning that mounting the weight at the lowest possible point might earn some allowance from the greater righting moment.[143] The same objection already raised against a bonded-in lead sheet (above) recurred immediately and just as decisively: ballast has to be shifted, and even removed, as extra gear (an anchor, a battery) comes aboard over time, a flexibility a sheet fixed across the entire bottom would not allow.[144] A follow-up granted the objection but still saw merit in the idea provided some ballast was left free to trim, and raised a further, unanswered question — whether a sheet spread the full length of the bottom, rather than lead concentrated in pigs nearer the middle, would add enough extra inertia near the bow and stern to affect handling.[145] A separate, also-unanswered question wondered whether a steel bottom might interfere with a magnetic compass.[146] No galvanised-steel-sheet-bottomed Paradox build is recorded, just as no sheet-lead-bottom or structural-steel-bottom build is (above).

## A built example: extra bottom thickness in place of some fixed lead

Distinct from the unbuilt steel- and sheet-lead-bottom proposals above, at least one real boat has been built with a bottom panel well beyond the plans' stock **3/4 in**, paired with a lighter fixed-lead load: **160 lb of lead** alongside a **1¾ in plywood bottom**, more than double the stock thickness. No further build detail — reasoning, cost, or how the combination sails — survives on the record.[178]

## A 2012 cargo-capacity question, and a two-extra-passenger data point

A further newcomer, in 2012, asked outright for the boat's weight capacity.[155] The answer restated the design displacement already established above — **1,410 lb (640 kg)** in full sailing trim — and broke down what is left once ballast is accounted for: with little other equipment aboard, **180 kg (400 lb)** of that figure is lead ballast and **70 kg (150 lb)** is water ballast, leaving in theory roughly **180 kg (400 lb)** free for stores, anchors, bicycles, batteries, and the like. In practice, a boat trimmed for a single sailor has on occasion carried two additional people with no noticeable change in sailing performance — though cramped, and not sailed far in that condition.[156] Asked to narrow the answer specifically to occupant weight rather than cargo in general,[157] the reply was that there is no separate passenger allowance: occupant weight is treated exactly like any other added weight, brought aboard and then balanced by removing lead ballast to bring the boat back to its correct design displacement.[158]

## Heavy displacement as a virtue: grounding tolerance, ride comfort, and stowage

A first-time builder part-way through his own hull assembly, weighing what he had signed up for against a previous, much lighter boat that had "sailed over the water rather than through it," remarked that a Paradox is "one hellava overbuilt boat" — already heavy well before the bottom panel or any ballast had even gone on.[182] The reply put that same heaviness as a benefit rather than a drawback: the hull's sturdiness is reassuring when bouncing along a gravel bottom; the weight itself improves ride comfort, giving a steady ride in rough conditions that a lighter boat would not; and the design's reserve of carrying capacity (see the cargo-capacity figures above) means gear can keep being added to the inventory aboard without killing performance.[183]

[179] [Paradox builders mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md) message 8573 (mbolduc / "boldav38" / Dave Bolduc, `paradoxbuilders`, 2015-08-23) — "Re: ballast": ballast in these kinds of boats is usually better more than less; heaviest items go closest to the bottom, lighter things like clothes higher up in nets, heavy items kept centralised amidships, lighter gear like fenders toward the bow or stern; ballast can be shifted underway — on a long passage to windward, shifts water jugs and fuel to windward and sits farther forward in the cabin, carrying more sail longer with the bow buried and pointing higher; sits all the way back when off the wind; doesn't always like using built-in water tanks on a long cruise to unknown parts, since topping one off with bad water contaminates the whole batch — happened once in the Bahamas, expecting R.O. water at a marina but getting water contaminated with diesel instead — and a tank run very low far from civilization loses a significant amount of ballast; prefers lots of small water jugs stored in the water tank, containing any leak; starts a trip with a couple of cases of spring water, the empty bottles then tossed in the bow for added flotation or refilled locally from a 2.5-gallon collapsible jug, with bags of sand, jars of shells, or coloured stones filling any space the empties leave, doubling as ballast.
