# A scaled-down "mini" Paradox (speculative)

Where [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md) and [Building Paradox larger](/design/larger-paradox.md) scale the design up, a proposal ran the other way — scaling it **down** into a quick, cheap single-occupant hull to learn on while a full-size Paradox build was still ahead.

Note: this proposal is unrelated to the established **["Mini Paradox"](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-mini-paradox-working-name-and-the-2005-construction-era-discussion)** working name, which was the 2005 construction-era nickname for what is now known to be Matt Layden's own **Enigma** — a distinct, smaller Layden design actually built and sailed. This page concerns only an unbuilt scaling idea for the stock Paradox hull itself.

## The proposal: a .55-scale hull built quick and cheap

Working from the stock Paradox's dimensions, the idea was to reduce every dimension by a factor of **.55**, giving a hull roughly **7.6 ft long, 26 in wide, and 19 in tall** — small enough that the sides, bottom, and top could each come from a single sheet of plywood, plus some bulkheads and the transom. The build would skip the stock design's rounded cabin top entirely, using 1-by stock for bulkheads instead, built stitch-and-glue for speed. The resulting cockpit would run about 42 in long by 22 in wide, with the sailor sitting inside it, facing forward and somewhat down into the hull; a roped tiller was proposed to avoid reaching behind to steer.[1]

The motivation was not a considered design exercise but pure impatience: no local lake offered sailboat rentals, and the aim was to get on the water, learn to sail, and get comfortable with the Paradox-style rig at minimal cost and effort while full-size Paradox plans and a build area were still being prepared. Left open on the record: whether to use a weighted centerboard or simply add weight to the bottom of the hull for stability.[1]

## Reception: a comparison to the Bolger Brick, and a cheaper-and-quicker alternative

One response set the scale-down idea aside in favor of a different, related route: fitting a **Paradox deck, cabin, chine runners, and sail rig, scaled down, onto a Bolger Brick hull** — a flat-sided, easy-to-build hull form — rather than scaling the Paradox hull itself.[2]

Another suggestion offered an even quicker route to the same underlying goal — sailing something, soon, without a scratch design exercise — by building a **PDRacer** in a single weekend instead; a stock PDRacer forgoes chine runners to stay "class legal," but building outside class rules to add them was floated as "a fun experiment."[3]

A more considered assessment judged the .55-scale idea itself an open question worth trying, since [Matt Layden](/matt-layden/biography.md)'s own **Enigma** is already a scaled-down Paradox "in many respects," making it plausible the concept could be scaled down further still. For simply getting on the water fastest and cheapest, though, two proven, non-Paradox routes were recommended ahead of any scaled-down build: buying a **secondhand small fiberglass boat** outright (far less money than building anything in the same size range), or building one of **Jim Michalak's** designs — optimized for cheap, easy building, reported to sail well, and (in several designs) rigged with a **lug sail**, giving some exposure to a rig with Paradox-like characteristics; the **"Piccup Squared"** was named as one example.[4]

A firsthand caution came from experience building a small, similarly-conceived tiny boat: it proved **not stable**, workable only lying down recumbent (with an unresolved boarding/exit problem), until **pipe-insulation sponsons encased in polythene** were added on both sides — which made it acceptably stable in calm water, but at the cost of moving further and further from the original small-boat concept. The **Bolger Minuet**, a tiny yacht built with a ballast keel, was pointed to instead, as a small design that solves the same stability problem properly rather than by improvised sponsons.[5]

## Resolution: the proposer built a different small boat instead

Weighing the replies, the mini-Paradox scale-down was judged too great a risk of its own — the concern was specifically the reduced beam that scaling down would produce, and, without naval-architecture training, an unproven scaled hull was not worth gambling on. The suggested PDRacer was ruled out on aesthetic grounds alone ("I could not bring myself to 'sail' in something that looked like that").[6]

Instead, within the same weekend, a small plywood **pram with a V-bottom**, designed as a rowboat, was started from plans in John Gardner's book *Building Classic Small Craft* — modified with a taller first bulkhead to carry a mast, side benches instead of a thwart, a centerboard, and a rudder. The result was judged close to the fiberglass-boat and Michalak alternatives that had been suggested, just not flat-bottomed.[6] This closed out the mini-Paradox thread: no scaled-down Paradox hull was built, and the underlying goal (getting on the water quickly and cheaply while a full Paradox build lay ahead) was met by a different small-craft design instead.

## A week later: a 7' 6" Paradox/Enigma rig, and an alternative sail sized against Bolger's Camper

A separate scaled-down idea surfaced the same month, in an unrelated thread: work began on drawings for the rig of a **7' 6" version of Paradox/Enigma** that had already been discussed off-list.[7] In response, a larger alternative was proposed instead: a **150 sq ft sail** sized for **Bolger's Camper design #640** (18 ft LOA, 5' 3" beam, 6 in draft, twin rowing positions, an open two-person sleeping space, a 150 sq ft gaff rig on a tabernacled mast, a walk-through birdwatcher-style raised deck, unballasted as drawn), fitted instead with Paradox's sailplan and chine runners.[8]

## A 2008 recurrence: a "protected day-sailer" idea

A different, later scaled-down idea aimed at a different goal — not a stopgap to learn on, but a genuine "protected day-sailer" rather than a full cruiser — floated while questioning whether [Noli](/boats/noli.md)'s own bottom really came from a single sheet of plywood. Working the concept down as far as possible, it reached about **5 metric sheets total, including decks and rudder** — smaller again than the .55-scale proposal above — with no build reported to follow it on record. See [Noli — a bottom-sheet-count correction, and a separate personal Enigma-lookalike design](/boats/noli.md#a-bottom-sheet-count-correction-and-a-separate-personal-enigma-lookalike-design) for the exchange this idea appeared alongside.[9]

## A December 2009 recurrence: a sit-inside PDRacer for cold-weather car-topping

A later variant on the same underlying idea — grafting Paradox's own features onto a different, already-established small hull rather than scaling Paradox itself — proposed applying the **sit-inside, sail-from-inside cabin concept to a PDRacer hull**: a boat that could be car-topped, in the manner of a PDRacer, yet still be sailed comfortably in cold and wet weather, extending the practical sailing season well beyond what an open cockpit allows.[10]

## A further December 2009 exchange: a scaled-down Enigma-sharpie alternative, and PDRacer/PDGoose hull ideas

A different route to the same underlying goal was floated in reply: scale the **Paradox design itself down** into an Enigma-like chine-runner sharpie, rather than grafting its cabin onto an existing small hull — offered by a correspondent who, since this would be his own first real build, had otherwise decided to stick to the stock Paradox plans, taking liberties only with the electrical system.[11]

One reply judged **Matt Layden's own Sand Flea concept** would fit well on a PDRacer hull as it stood;[12] another judged Sand Flea more a **sleep-inside than a sail-inside** design, and doubted a PDRacer could sail at all once fitted with a genuine cabin, since the result would amount to little more than a cube.[13]

Renaming the thread to reflect the shift away from the building-manual question that had started it, the cube objection was met with a narrower proposal: fitting Sand Flea with only a **minimal canvas cover over the cockpit opening**, much like Enigma 360's own, would be enough to make her a genuine sail-inside design for a relatively short sailor. A number of roughly cubical boats have been *proposed* over the years — Bolger's **Superbrick**, the **Gilbert Skippy** (see the "Around in Ten" site), and the **Selway-Fisher Micro 8 and Micro 10** among them — but none is known to have actually been sailed successfully.[14]

Worked through in more detail, a straightforward build was proposed along the same lines: a **PDRacer or, better still, a PDGoose hull fitted with a Sand Flea deck and an Enigma-360-style canvas cover**. Bolting the deck to the hull, and splitting the deck itself into pieces small enough to fit inside a car, would make the whole boat car-toppable; without that requirement, a **PDGoose hull fitted instead with a solid Paradox-style cabin aft** was offered as an equally straightforward alternative.[15]

Set against the scaled-down Enigma-sharpie alternative raised earlier in the same exchange, the objection was that it simplified the parts of Paradox already easy to build — leeboards or a drop-down daggerboard in place of the harder-to-build chine runners — while leaving the genuinely hard parts unchanged; building a deck and cabin for a simple box hull, by contrast, drew no such reservation.[16]

## Don Elliott's own flush-deck-forward design, and the fate of his archive

The cabin-aft, flush-deck-forward layout proposed above turned out to have already been drawn: Don Elliott had worked through a similar configuration in his own unbuilt "Illusion," developed through extended discussion on the separate Yahoo Smallboats group and growing out of an earlier concept of his own, "Squeezebox," first conceived as a lifeboat — see [Don Elliott](/people/don-elliott.md#a-separate-unbuilt-design-illusion) for the fuller design history.[24] Studying Don's surviving drawings, still hosted in an open web directory, drew genuine enthusiasm — engrossing enough to burn a meal cooking at the same time — along with regret that most of the hundreds, perhaps thousands, of illustrations Don produced over his career were never saved and are now lost; combined with material already available on PDRacers, the surviving record was judged enough on its own to build an interesting small boat from, if only the "shall I, shan't I" dilemma of actually starting could be settled.[25] The reply was blunt: too much deliberation leads to never building anything at all, and an already-sailed example to work from is worth several years' head start over starting from a blank page.[26] A further reader, taken with the Illusion design, separately asked whether Elliott's own description of the conditions she was designed for implied protected-water use only, and whether a pointier bow could be substituted without undermining the underlying idea.[27] The reply held that keeping the hull under 8 ft would cost too much displacement, and that the workable compromise for a boat this small is a hull pointed on the waterline and flat above it, in the manner of Sand Flea or John Welsford's Sherpa — a boat of this kind is not something to take far from land regardless, since Illusion herself is designed primarily for exploring where the water and land meet.[28] The earlier point about procrastination was conceded in turn, with the further, rueful thought that a little less of it years before might have landed a ready-built Paradox instead — [Derek Clark](/people/derek-clark.md)'s own "[Enuf](/boats/enuf.md)," offered to the list back in 2006 — though plenty of sailing had gone on regardless in the meantime.[29]

The decked-PDGoose/canvas-cover idea drew a capsize-safety question in turn, along with a relevant precedent: several PDRacers have completed the **Texas 200** fitted with small night cabins of one kind or another, the most promising of them a boat named "**Ranger**" — though car-topping a boat that size, or fitting it with bolt-on parts, was advised against regardless.[17]

Sand Flea already carries a soft top of her own, shown in photographs from the 2008 Everglades Challenge — an existing precedent for the minimal-canvas-cover proposal above rather than a new idea. Of the never-successfully-sailed cubical boats named above, the Gilbert Skippy's own seaworthiness remains an open question pending an actual sail, and the Selway-Fisher Micro 8 and Micro 10 were judged unsuited to genuine coastal pottering, with no reason for anyone to rush to build one.[18]

A PDRacer fitted with a cabin already exists and sails — pointed to as the "Tenacious Turtle" video — and chine runners were judged the easiest leeway preventer to build, a view formed after a leeboard on a different small boat broke, though whether they would work on a genuinely boxy hull was left open; a light boat of this kind is also easier to manoeuvre on a small trailer than to car-top when sailing alone.[19]

A further reply recalled an earlier personal attempt at the same underlying idea: years before, a chine-runner boat of similar concept — a scaled-down Paradox/Enigma sized to reach a comparable depth of immersion at roughly 250 kg (550 lb) of the designer's own weight, about 7 ft 10 in long and 33 in wide — got as far as a full-size cardboard mock-up that fit well but looked strange and high-sided; a nagging feeling that the design would prove unstable, since there seemed no way to get the weight low enough, meant it was never built, though the idea has never quite been let go of.[20]

Replying directly to the Tenacious Turtle pointer, the same reservation was sharpened: her cabin only serves as stowage while under sail and gives no more than partial shelter overnight, since she is not laid out to let her sailor actually steer from inside the cabin the way Paradox is — a genuine sail-inside design has to solve that, not merely add a cabin on top of an open hull. Apartment living was raised as an obstacle in its own right to any small-boat project that depends on trailer storage, a problem car-topping avoids entirely.[21]

One proposed layout aimed at solving both problems together: a cabin set aft with a flush deck forward, a configuration Don Elliott had already worked through in one of his own designs. Take-apart trailers exist for exactly this apartment-dwelling situation, but were judged likely to prove more trouble in practice than car-topping; a boat built with a solid cabin, to be practical for an apartment dweller, would need that cabin to be removable so the hull could still be moved from car to storage on its side.[22]

A further precedent for solving the storage problem itself, rather than the hull design, was recalled from an old Duckworks article: a builder in Germany paddling local rivers in a take-apart boat that doubled as a bookcase indoors. The more mundane fix offered alongside it was simply finding a friend with yard or garage space — garage preferred, since even a single off-season of outdoor storage was enough to leave dead grass and gully erosion where the hull had sat.[23]

A parallel idea aimed the same underlying concept at car-topping directly, reacting to one of Don Elliott's own Illusion drawings: make the hard shelter itself removable, so a bare, partially decked hull could be car-topped on its own and a separate rigid "topper" fitted only once at the launch site — sealed to the hull with compressible rubber gasketing held down by wing nuts, and finished with a roll-back canvas enclosure carrying vinyl windows for forward and upward visibility under sail; left off entirely for day-sailing in good weather, and adaptable, in principle, to almost any hull, with a folding dolly to reach the water and a small van the best vehicle for carrying it all.[30]

A firsthand objection followed, drawn from experience aboard Little Jim: a forward-facing cabin opening is exactly where cold wind and colder water come from.[31] The concern went further — sailing to windward, such an opening would act as a big scoop — and a different mechanism was suggested instead: a solid canopy that slides forward like an aircraft's and closes overlapping the fixed after part, an idea credited to Kellan Hatch of the smallsailboats group, built perhaps from a small plastic bath.[32] Combined with a flush deck, such a sliding canopy would only need to be slid off before car-topping the hull.[33] A real-world precedent for the mechanism already exists in the Proteus design's own acrylic bubble hatch.[34]

A further concern raised about all such lightweight sail-inside concepts was how to sail them flat at all: rigging an otherwise very stable 20 ft boat to be steered from the cabin, and trying it briefly in modest winds, proved nerve-wracking without the option of shifting weight to the windward side as dynamic ballast, and the experiment was abandoned quickly as a result; a few of Illusion's own drawings seemed to show adjustable side ballast, and it was asked how the similarly box-like PDRacers manage sailing to weather in a chop.[35] A considered answer drew on personal sailing history: early dinghy sailing had demanded constant vigilance over rudder and mainsheet and proved nerve-wracking enough to prompt years away from the water altogether, until an old book on canoe sailing described handling a narrow, theoretically unstable craft from a semi-recumbent position; sailing a Sea Snark the same way proved relaxing rather than alarming, since a hull this small and light spills wind and rights itself as it heels, rarely needing the sheet eased or a luff taken, because the sailor's own weight — greater than the boat's, and carried close to the waterline — simply overwhelms the boat in the conditions such a craft is likely to be out in; Optimist dinghies, only slightly less boxy than a PDRacer, were cited as managing well even in a force 8.[36]

A separate reply to the same flat-sailing concern proposed water ballast instead: Sand Flea herself already carries water-ballast tanks on her sides, precisely because her hull leaves no room to shift crew weight from side to side while sailing — though her own plans have still not been released.[37] The suggested alternative was to adapt an existing pram design rather than build any of the concept boats named above: Bolger's Reuben's Nymph, published in full in one of Harold "Dynamite" Payson's books, needs only a flattened sheer to take a deck, and a lowered central bulkhead — raised in the original to support a rowing seat — to sleep inside. In a hull this small the main source of ballast is judged to be the person aboard, kept low by lying semi-reclined on the bottom, the same principle used when "lining" a loaded canoe down river drops; dropping Sand Flea's own quick-reefing sail and lying flat beneath the deck should, on this reasoning, make her nearly impossible to capsize, and her sealed bulkheads fore and aft on each side, reached through deck plates, already add both flotation and stowage.[38]

## The exchange closes: a pointer to the Smallsailboats group, and a nanocruiser project

The thread closed with a suggestion that it had drifted far enough from Paradox specifically to belong on the separate Smallsailboats group instead, coming from a correspondent already identified above as its own point of contact; over the following holiday season that correspondent intended to work on a small boat of his own along the same minimalist lines, describing it as a "nanocruiser."[39] The reply welcomed the chance to hear more about the project once it was under way.[40]

## A 2010 real-world 90% scale-down, mid-build

Unlike the unbuilt proposals above, one builder is partway through an actual 90%-scale Paradox, running the opposite direction from the recurring interest in scaling the design [up](/design/larger-paradox.md). The scale decision came after the bottom panel had already been cut to the plans' own 3/4 in thickness rather than a proportionately thinner one; rather than treat that as a problem, the extra material was simply accepted as ballast, sitting low in the hull exactly where ballast is wanted.[41]

## A December 2010 recurrence: the reverse question, and a borrowed scaling-physics lesson

A newcomer asked the reverse of this page's usual question — rather than scaling Paradox up, could it be scaled down, or was a smaller design such as Matt Layden's own 12 ft [Enigma](/matt-layden/enigma.md) available in plan form instead?[42] The Enigma question went unanswered directly on the thread; her own plans situation is covered in full at [Enigma — plans](/matt-layden/enigma.md#plans). The reply that came instead addressed scaling generally, borrowing Jim Michalak's own published essay on scaling his AF4 sharpie up 20% to the "AF4 Grande": area grows with the square of the scale factor and weight and volume with its cube, so a 20% linear increase brings a 44% area/weight increase and a 73% volume increase — the same cubic relationship already worked out independently for Paradox's own 20% scale-up (see [Building Paradox larger — a worked 20% scale-up](/design/larger-paradox.md#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement)). Michalak's essay draws the reverse case out explicitly, using a 15 ft rowing dory scaled down 20% to 12.5 ft as an example: capacity would fall from about 400 lb to 230 lb, "and it quickly becomes barely a one person boat" — the general lesson being that scaling a small boat down costs usable capacity far faster than it saves material or length, the same cubic relationship working against a builder in reverse.[43]
