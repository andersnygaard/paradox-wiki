# Junk rig

A junk rig is an alternative to the Paradox's stock standing-lug rig, occasionally discussed as an option for the boat. On the list the question stayed theoretical — sail plans were drawn and argued over, but no conversion was carried through. **Two** junk-rigged Paradoxes were built and sailed outside the list era — a stock-lug conversion and a purpose-designed cambered rig on a scaled-up hull — and their verdicts are recorded at [junk rigs actually built and sailed](#junk-rigs-actually-built-and-sailed) below.

## Why the stock rig is judged hard to beat with a Chinese lug

The Paradox's stock rig already reefs easily, is controlled through a gybe, and points well to windward, so a Chinese lug rig would need compelling advantages to be worth the change.[1] Several practical objections to fitting one stand on the record: a Chinese lug typically carries proportionally more sail forward of the mast, which would likely require moving the mast aft to keep the boat in balance; it needs sheets running aft from the battens, and since the stock sail already extends behind the transom, a lug conversion would need either a bumkin or a further-forward center of effort, upsetting the design's already near-perfect balance; and a Chinese lug's boom, yard, and sail would either stow awkwardly on the side deck (as the stock sail does) or swing loose overhead on the topping lift, in the way when standing.[1] Moving the mast forward to rebalance an alternative rig also raises a structural question of its own: whether to relocate the mast's own supporting bulkhead or add a further one, either of which risks disrupting the forward storage space and the ventilation system built around it.[2] A Chinese lug's luff typically sits anywhere from a few percent to about 20% forward of the mast, and more elaborate versions use a yard parrel and a luff parrel to shift the sail fore-and-aft for balance; the usual sheeting arrangement uses two mainsheets (one to windward, one to leeward) attached to the boom about 20% in from its aft end, without needing to move the sail's center of effort or the mast.[3]

## Lazy jacks are incompatible with the standing lug's rotating boom

A Hasler-type junk rig's lazy jacks run through eyes on the underside of the boom — an arrangement that cannot work on Matt Layden's standing lug, since that boom rotates for reefing.[4] The standing lug's center of effort also sits much lower than a Hasler-type junk's, and a junk rig is considerably heavier aloft, inducing heel more readily than the lug sail; the rolling-reefing lug's core advantage over a junk conversion is its simplicity.[4] After roughly 15 years of trial and error refining the Paradox, further design improvement is considered difficult; the only changes one longtime builder made were internal — reworking the under-floor storage so removable, slidable floor panels give clear access to the ballast areas from a raised working seat without needing hinges, whether the main hatch is open or closed.[4]

## A worked Hasler/McLeod junk-rig sail plan (April 2007), and why higher aspect ratio doesn't work

A second, independently developed Hasler/McLeod junk-rig sail plan for the Paradox — 105 sq ft, drawn up by a sailor with prior experience designing and building junk rigs for other small boats — was uploaded to the group's files section for comment.[5][6] The design choice was framed as a genuine test of the "sailing system" idea behind the stock rig: any boat with any rig is in some sense a sailing system, so a rig substitution is a legitimate variation to explore rather than a departure from the design's essential character, in the same way established yacht designers have long accommodated personalization of their own stock designs.[6]

Converting to a junk rig would remove the sail's dependence on the mainsail's own specific rolling-boom furling geometry, in principle simplifying some of the stock rig's construction — though a higher-aspect-ratio version was tried first and rejected: raising the aspect ratio moved the sail's center of effort forward of the hull's center of lateral resistance, and the rig could not be adjusted back from the lower-aspect-ratio version actually built without inducing substantial weather helm. How far aft Matt Layden placed the stock sail's own center of effort, by comparison, was noted as a striking design choice once seen up close.[7][8]

A junk rig on Paradox should carry a higher aspect ratio than usual specifically to keep the battens' after ends inboard of the transom.[9] Firsthand experience converting an Albin Vega to junk rig and cruising it for years found only a small loss of windward performance against the fore-and-aft rig it replaced, with a best noon-to-noon run of 173 nautical miles ahead of a 25-knot nor'easter; despite that, a lug-to-junk conversion was judged not worth making on a Paradox specifically, given the extra spar weight aloft, the extra line needed to manage a junk's sail, and the lug's own advantage of easy, tidy stowage on the side deck.[9]

Sheeting a junk sail this far aft on a Paradox would need a bumpkin pole, since the sail already extends well behind the transom because of the chine runners and the rudder's large role in balance; an experimental forward extension of the chine runners was once tried on Matt Layden's own original boat, but any windward-performance improvement from it proved too small to measure.[10][11] A centerboard added well forward, in the manner of Little Cruiser, or a leeboard, was floated as a way to make a junk rig easier to balance on Paradox, at the cost of moving further from the design's essential simplicity.[10] A straight-sided, square-headed junk sail — close to the shape of the stock lug but with more area at the top rear corner — was also floated as a way to let the mast keep its forward rake while holding the center of effort in roughly the same place; such sails have long been used on Chinese junks, including tall rectangular versions illustrated by G. Worcester from Yangtze-river junks, built for a river where wind often does not reach the water's surface.[12][13]

Attaching a boomkin to the rudder, as done on other boats, was one option considered for sheeting a Paradox junk rig, though a satisfactory solution was not settled on; fitting anything to the transom itself risks fouling the yuloh.[14] A junk rig's familiarity and ease of tuning, learned from cruising a junk-rigged boat for years, can outweigh an unfamiliar standard rig's own performance for a builder already comfortable with the Chinese lug.[14]

A light, purpose-built **arch** spanning the transom is a further boomkin alternative: with a junk rig's low sheet loads, even a tubing arch should be strong enough, drawing on a mast-gallows installation of stainless pipe set into stainless lifeline base receivers that had held up well on an unrelated boat.[15][16] The arch idea also keeps the sheeting clear of the raised rudder, an advantage over mounting a boomkin on the rudder itself.[16]

## A daggerboard-forward CLR experiment, and why Paradox's rudder was built large

A 10 ft sailing dinghy built with its daggerboard moved forward, to the bow, to lengthen the center of lateral resistance and let the mast move forward for a cat rig, tracked as straight as an arrow under that arrangement — with an unplanned side effect: thrown hard over, the forward daggerboard acted as a pivot point, stalling the bow while the stern swung around.[17] The same builder expected the junk rig's individual sail panels would need some camber built in to sail efficiently, beyond the flat panels of his own first attempt.[17]

Separately, Matt Layden's own reasoning for building Paradox's rudder as large as it is was relayed firsthand: sailing a Little Cruiser at Cedar Key and discussing the pros and cons of rudder size directly with Matt Layden, the rudder was placed well aft as part of the boat's lateral-resistance equation, and Matt held the Paradox hull specifically needed the larger rudder for increased lateral resistance.[18] This is a second, independent account of the same rudder-size rationale documented at [rudder and chine experiments — why the rudder was built large, and the trade-offs of its size](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size). The same exchange noted how far aft Matt Layden set the stock sail's own center of effort, cautioning that raising a junk conversion's aspect ratio risks moving the sail's center of effort forward of the hull's center of lateral resistance and inducing weather helm the rig cannot then be adjusted back out of.[18]

## A 2016 exchange: a cambered rig endorsed, then a heeling-moment objection raised

Asked directly, in April 2016, whether anyone had tried a cambered junk rig specifically on a Paradox, one answer drew on outside experience rather than the design itself: a cambered junk sail should do extremely well on Paradox, given how longitudinally stable the hull already is — "almost like a full keel boat" — citing a cambered junk rig built and sailed for years on an unrelated Marples 3m Sea Clipper trimaran, which performed well to windward, proved very easily (if not infinitely) reefable, and, its mechanics being old and well proven, was judged not especially finicky.[19][20]

A further, more skeptical objection followed the same week, while THU was still under construction: since it is the stock lug's own considerable power that tilts a Paradox to the heel angle her chine runners need in order to work at all, a junk rig — aerodynamically efficient, but engineered specifically to keep stress on the rig low — might simply lack that same heeling power, leaving a junk-rigged Paradox standing more upright and making leeway instead, her chine runners never properly employed; raised half as a joke, with the suggestion that this might be exactly the problem Matt Layden identified decades earlier. At the time the Paradox registry listed no boat built and sailing under junk rig at all, though, as just noted, one was already under construction.[21][22]

The reply addressed the mechanism rather than the worry directly: a junk sail's characteristically low rig stress comes from its battens acting as so many small additional booms, so each panel carries only a small area and a small share of the total load, unlike a lug sail's single full panel stretched between boom and yard alone — though a separate, opposite worry, relayed from a correspondent named only "Jim," held that the sheer weight of a junk sail's many battens plus its extra camber-forming cloth could tip the boat too far the other way regardless. Left open at the time — "hopefully we'll see" — the heeling/chine-runner question itself was never revisited on the list once THU was launched and sailing (below), and remains unsettled in the archive's record.[23]

## Answers to the heeling objection: leeway without heel, but a sheeting conflict remains

Jim's own weight-and-heel worry, once he saw it relayed, turned out to cut against a scaled-up hull less than a standard one: a bigger, 20%-larger boat should be less prone to the tenderness a heavier rig risks than a standard-sized Paradox.[24] On the leeway question itself, a different answer came back: Paradox does not need to be heeled over much at all to resist making leeway, since in light winds, sailing nearly level, her deep rudder and general hull shape already act as a keel in their own right; what she does need is enough way on to carry a tack through, since getting caught in irons can be hard to recover from.[25] The boat under construction, asked about directly, was confirmed to be the very one already mentioned above.[26]

A different kind of objection touched the sheeting geometry rather than the heeling moment: a traditional junk rig's sheets lead aft, clear of the sail and the battens' own ends — raising a question about how that squares with Paradox's own sail already sitting as far aft as balance allows, garbled in the archive's own text and left unanswered at the time.[27] Clarified the next day, the point turned out to be about balance rather than sheeting geometry as such — mangled by autocorrect rather than genuinely obscure — and its author accepted that the sail shape could be tweaked, or an aft-leaning sheeting spar used, to compensate.[28] Two untried, speculative fixes were floated regardless: cutting the sail wider at the head than at the foot, with the boom shorter than the yard, in case shifting the sail's greatest width upward and aft eases the conflict; or, alternatively, a sail cut narrow at both boom and yard and widest across the middle — a lens-shaped panel of the kind that gives some junk rigs their curved leech.[29][30]

A further, more skeptical assessment weighed the whole idea against Paradox's own low-windage design: at only about 100 sq ft, a junk sail is too small to be worth the added weight and windage of its battens and running rigging — windage itself judged, from experience on an earlier boat, the greater enemy of the two — and the standard junk-rig sheetlet arrangement will not work at all, since Paradox's centre of lateral resistance sits so far aft that the leech would fall behind the transom.[31] The only part of the idea judged worth keeping was a hybrid: fit lightweight, flexible battens down the sail's leech with fixed batten parrels, but keep the existing sail shape and sheeting arrangement unchanged — trading away roller furling and a clear boom at anchor (lazy jacks would be needed, and reefing would mean tying the battens down by hand with cringles) for freedom from high luff tension and mast chafe, since the battens rather than the sail itself would then bear against the mast. A similar rig had worked well on an earlier, unrelated 15 ft boat, but Paradox's boom sits close enough to the water that giving up the stock roller-furling boom's own taper — which keeps the clew clear of the water — and the ability to control the whole sail from inside the cabin both looked like real losses.[31]

## Further sheeting fixes, a trimaran's cockpit-only rig, and the cost case for conversion

A boomkin was offered again as the simplest fix for the sheeting conflict, paired this time with a sail-shape suggestion: cut the sail narrower and taller — raising its aspect ratio — so the reefed sail can simply be sheeted to the transom, reasoning that a junk sail's own quick, easy reefing makes a narrower shape's usual drawbacks easier to accept.[32]

The weight-and-windage worry drew two further, untried counter-ideas: lazyjacks used for reefing instead of roller furling, to eliminate the creases furling causes, and a system for laying the whole dropped rig over to lie alongside the cabin, cutting windage at rest; a promise followed to put the windage question to dedicated junk-rig forums for a wider opinion.[33] The worry that a junk conversion would cost the ability to control the whole sail from inside the cabin drew a firsthand rebuttal: a cambered junk rig built for an unrelated Marples 3 m Sea Clipper trimaran was raised, lowered, reefed, and stowed clear for paddling entirely from its cockpit, without ever leaving it — a junk rig does not, in itself, remove the option of running the whole sail from inside a boat.[34]

The cost case for the conversion was pressed again on different grounds days later: a junk rig avoids the wear a Bermudan-style sail takes — a plain jib for a 14 ft dinghy was put at around AUD 600 by comparison — and two thousand years of continuous use as a working rig was offered as its own kind of proof, with the standing lug's own furling and chafe troubles pointed to as the design's persistent weak point.[35]

Weighing in directly, one reply agreed with the skepticism above as it applied to a standard-sized Paradox specifically, while allowing that a larger, heavier hull — [THU](/boats/thu.md)'s, still under construction at the time — might turn out differently.[36]

A last comparison set the stock rig's own advantage against the junk conversion's: Matt Layden's roller-furling sail can be disconnected from the mast and dropped onto the cabin top quickly, where a junk sail needs every batten parrel released first — slower, though not needed at all if the sail is already stowed furled in lazyjacks — and for a cruising ground with frequent low bridges, being able to strike the mast repeatedly with the original rig's own quick-release was judged a real advantage worth keeping.[37]

A builder who had actually made the conversion — the same one behind [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md)'s own stock-to-junk rebuild, still under way at the time — weighed in directly on the thread: an earlier conversion of an unrelated eighteen-foot fibreglass keelboat had already convinced him his own Paradox would carry a junk rig too, for a reason not yet raised on this thread — some lug sails twist so much that only part of the sail is actually working, and curing that twist is central to why a junk rig is both simpler and more effective than a lug.[38] The point about lug-sail twist landed the same week a fellow correspondent, chasing the identical symptom on his own standard lug rig, added new tell-tales to the sail's leech for clearer feedback on where the twist was actually occurring — see [Mk2 sail — a 2016 recurrence](/components/rig/mk2-sail.md#a-2016-recurrence-furling-still-uneven-after-25-outings-and-a-sailmakers-flatness-check).

Arne Kverneland, the same designer credited with THU's own cambered-sail method (see [below](#thu-a-purpose-designed-cambered-rig-method-not-yet-fully-solved)), was noted as a well-known champion of the cambered junk rig within the wider Junk Rig Association community — mocked by at least one skeptic ("improving performance on junksails is like putting a spoiler on a tractor") and, by report, comfortably rebutting the jibe.[39]

## Junk rigs actually built and sailed

Everything above is argument in advance of a boat. **Two** junk-rigged Paradoxes were in fact built and sailed — both outside the list era, and by two opposite routes. [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md) took the stock lug and **converted** it; [THU](/boats/thu.md) was given a junk rig **designed from scratch** on a scaled-up hull. Between them they turn the page's long theoretical argument into two data points — but only the second records how the balance and sheeting problems were actually handled.

### Planktos: the stock lug converted (outcome, not method)

One Paradox was **built to the stock lug rig with roller furling and then changed to a junk rig** — a Western Australian build of 2013–2016, cruised on a shakedown passage to Shark Bay and presented to a home-boatbuilding club in April 2021.[40] It is the only junk-rig Paradox on record that was a **conversion** of the stock rig, and the only one **cruised**; the full build is at [Planktos](/boats/planktos.md). Four months before construction began, replying on the mailing list to a fellow Paradox owner's question about whether anyone had sailed a Paradox under junk rig, the same builder had already reported converting a different boat to junk rig and said he planned to fit one to his own, still-unbuilt Paradox too; see [people/roselt-croeser.md](/people/roselt-croeser.md#the-junk-rig-conversion) for the message in full.[41]

The verdict after cruising is favourable and specific: the junk rig performed well overall, proving **easy and quick to reef and unreef**, with **good sail twist control** and **cheap, lightweight fittings**.[40] The reefing speed and the low cost of fittings answer, in practice, two of the qualities the stock rig was defended on above.

The report does **not** settle the objections that dominate the rest of this page. How the sheeting was arranged, whether a bumkin or arch was needed, whether the mast was moved, and how the centre of effort was kept behind the hull's centre of lateral resistance are all unrecorded.[40] The boat's stated limit — **around 20 knots** of wind, beyond which conditions become survival sailing — is given as a whole-boat figure, not attributed to the rig.[40] So the outcome is documented while the method is not: a junk-rigged Paradox works, but this source does not show how the balance and sheeting problems were solved.

The [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) fills in several of the specifics the newsletter left open, posted by Croeser under his group handle, "Waldo Cruiser": the mast stayed in Planktos's original position through the conversion, the sail was sheeted to the end of each batten for what he described as excellent control, and in Western Australia's wind he was often reefed down to three of the four panels.[42][43] The hand-me-down sail, adapted from another project, never set well on its bottom panel, though most of it worked fine; set against the lug it replaced, he judged the junk rig less prone to twist, more reliable and quicker to reef, and, through better control of sail shape, more efficient.[42] The conversion drew wider recognition in December 2023, when the Junk Rig Association featured Planktos as its front-page boat of the month, a piece Croeser welcomed warmly.[44]

Planktos changed hands within the group in July 2023: Kevin Williamson, who had been planning his own build, instead struck a deal with Croeser and took over the same junk-rigged boat — confirmed at the time by a third member's recollection that Croeser had had a lot of fun in her and that the junk rig seemed to work well.[45] Williamson found the rig mostly good to live with but ran into the caution this page raises above about extra lines: the sail was bulkier to lower than a rolled lug, and unstepping the mast to clear a bridge meant disconnecting and re-tying enough running rigging that doing it afloat looked risky; his answer was a folding mast that lowers fully onto the cabin top and stands back up again without unrigging anything.[46] That November he went further and extended the mast itself, from 3.72 m to 4.43 m above the deck — 86 cm taller, most of it gained by relocating the halyard's attachment point rather than lengthening the spar — for more usable sail area and a clearer forward view over the cabin top; Croeser, estimating from the panel widths, put the enlarged sail at about 8.3 m², and expected the higher sail plan would make her somewhat more tender.[47] By Christmas the boat was sailing under a new name, Skye, and Williamson logged a first proper sail after the mast work with a 4.8-knot top speed on a downwind run.[48] The following March, Junk Rig Association member and writer Jan Wolstenholme cited the same boat, by then known on the group simply as Williamson's, as the JRA's recent Boat of the Month; Williamson credited the article and noted that his folding mast let him leave the whole rig permanently set up, cutting the time from trailer to sailing to a few minutes.[49]

### THU: a purpose-designed cambered rig (method, not yet fully solved)

Where Planktos gives the outcome, [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s **"[THU](/boats/thu.md)"** — a [20% scaled-up](/design/larger-paradox.md) Paradox built from 2015 and launched in 2017 — records the **method**. Rather than convert the stock lug, Mee designed a **six-panel cambered junk sail** from scratch to **Arne Kverneland**'s Junk Rig Association method, with **8% camber** built into the panels.[50] This directly follows the intuition recorded above — that a junk rig on Paradox needs [built-in camber, not flat panels](#a-daggerboard-forward-clr-experiment-and-why-paradoxs-rudder-was-built-large) — and Mee reached the same conclusion by trial: he rejected a flat-cut sample plan because "The HML is actually flat cut sail with not so good winward performance. It's the AK's camber that improves it."[51]

On the specific objections the rest of this page raises, THU is the first built boat to answer several:

- **Mast position.** The page predicts a junk's extra area forward of the mast would force the mast **aft** for balance. THU went the other way — the mast moved **forward of bulkhead 2**, driven by the taller 6 m spar (Mee wanted more mast below deck and clearance between the transom and the upper sheet).[51] Balance was then fiddly rather than automatic: one tack showed noticeable **lee helm**, which he cured by adding **water ballast forward** and tightening the **tack parrel** to move the centre of effort back.[52]
- **Sheeting and the transom overhang.** Mee needed **no bumkin or arch**; raising the mast opened ~10° of clearance between the transom and the upper sheet, and he sheeted to the boom directly. But this is exactly where THU is **not** finished — the **boom twisted** too much under sheet, and he moved block positions around without settling it.[52] The choice behind that geometry was already made before the mast moved: the boom reaches over the transom, but the sail's own leech line curves inward enough that amidships it sits above the deck rather than beyond it; he chose a **vertical-leech** sail plan over a fan type specifically to hold the centre of effort as far aft as possible and keep the aspect ratio low, moving the **chine runners forward** to match the centre of effort's own resulting forward shift. He named a real cost of the choice ahead of finishing the boat too: losing the stock rig's easy on-deck lowering of a reefed sail, since a junk's batten barrels have to be loosened first.[53]
- **Extra line to manage.** Confirmed, and quantified: "We have 5 camcleats in total, because of the junk sail. Most Paradoxes have 2 or 3," the lines led in two levels with a three-sheave deflector on the mast to keep them clear of the lower sail downwind.[52]
- **Tacking.** The one clear win: THU **tacked without tricks** on her first sail, where a loaded lug Paradox usually needs the sail backed.[54]

Mee's design work was not only a blog record: he also documented it in real time on the Junk Rig Association's own technical forum, in a thread he titled "Building a junkrigged Matt Leyden's Paradox," joined specifically to get help from experienced junk-rig designers and users — "that is pretty much what has happened in that thread," in his own words.[55][56]

So the two boats are complementary. Planktos shows a junk-rigged Paradox **cruises well**; THU shows **how one such rig was actually laid out and balanced** — with camber built in, the mast moved, and the line count higher — while leaving the sheeting-and-twist problem visibly unsolved and windward pointing explicitly unconcluded.[52][54]

### A third builder underway: a split lug from AYRS's "Catalyst 37"

A third junk-rig Paradox was reported under construction on the mailing list itself in September 2013, by **[Darren](/people/darren-victoria.md)** of Victoria, BC, nearing completion of the hull. Rather than a Hasler/McLeod-style rig, he chose a **split lug sail**, drawn from Slieve McGalliard's AYRS design "Catalyst 37," expecting better pointing and more drive than the stock lug, alongside beefier scantlings and spars for extended offshore work — the only Paradox rig deviation on record sourced directly from a published AYRS (Amateur Yacht Research Society) design rather than worked out from first principles on the list. No sailing report survives to confirm whether the boat was built and launched as planned.[57]

### The group's wider verdict

Beyond the two built boats, the [Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) records years of Paradox owners endorsing junk rig from direct sailing experience rather than as theory. Watching Croeser's Planktos out-sail a 28 ft traditional gaffer to windward off Perth, one member summed up the general prejudice against junk rigs and dismissed it: put a well-designed one on a good hull and it will point.[58] Croeser himself repeated the endorsement on separate occasions, urging other builders to try the rig, and once answered a lug-rig sailor's complaint of excessive sail twist by saying simply that a junk rig had solved the same problem for him.[59] The design worry this page raises repeatedly — that a junk's forward sail area would force the mast aft, or make balance hard to achieve — surfaced again in 2021, when John Hyndman reported trying to design a junk rig for his own stock-lug Paradox and judged it difficult specifically because the stock mast sits too far aft for the rig, planning to prototype the sail cheaply in tarp before committing to a permanent build.[60] The two boats that were actually finished answered the worry two different ways rather than settling it: Planktos's conversion left the mast where the stock rig put it, absorbing whatever balance shift resulted, while THU's purpose-built rig moved the mast forward instead (see [above](#thu-a-purpose-designed-cambered-rig-method-not-yet-fully-solved)). Set against the standing theoretical caution this page records — extra spar weight aloft, extra line to manage — the group's verdicts, from Croeser's conversion to Williamson's continued refinement of the same boat, land consistently on the favourable side.

## A 2017 objection: is the underlying windward question even settled?

Days after THU's launch, on the same "Another launch" thread, a skeptic reopened the debate on different grounds: rather than criticize the junk rig on its own terms, the objection was that Paradox's own chine-runner windward performance is itself unproven, so adding a second untested variable compounds the risk — the jury is still out on the windward performance of the chine runners, so introducing another rig of questionable windward ability would likely make an outboard a necessity. The junk rig itself was likened, less charitably, to "tying heavy straight sticks across the guts of a nice sail," with a pointer to a WoodenBoat forum thread debating whether a fully battened gaff main can be made as easy to use as a junk sail.[61]

The reply drew on the wider Junk Rig Association record to answer both halves of the challenge. On the junk rig: a fully battened lug sail has achieved remarkable windward performance in other hands, citing Slieve McGalliard's own published results.[62] On the chine runners: windward performance is not in doubt, though it falls short of a deep keel — Paradox points quite well despite its unusual sail, but with a lot of leeway, since the runners simply do not have enough area, and she tacks slowly through a wide turning circle; as a coastal cruising boat for shallow water this works very well, though by the standard of a modern racing dinghy it is poor. Full verdict: [chine-runners/windward-performance.md — a 2017 balanced verdict](/chine-runners/windward-performance.md#a-2017-balanced-verdict-adequate-for-coastal-cruising-no-match-for-a-modern-dinghy).[62] Separately, the reply traced the "vang" — the sheeting-control lines run to each batten on a Chinese lug, sometimes called "sheetlets" — to Thames-barge practice: vangs fell out of general use on small cruising yachts once paid hands became too expensive to keep them rigged, and the more competent Thames-barge skippers still use them to markedly improve a spritsail's windward performance the same way a junk's battens do.[62] The exchange closed on a plea for patience rather than a verdict: theory is a good start, but nothing trumps experiment, and a builder who had put as much thought and work into his boat as Eerik Mee deserved to be judged by how THU actually sailed, not condemned in advance.[62]

Continuing the same thread separately, a further correspondent made the case *for* conversion from direct experience: a cambered junk rig built for an earlier boat had worked so well he planned to build a second for his current one, retiring its balance lug — noting Phil Bolger's own conversion to a Chinese gaff rig for the same reasons, sheet-twist control and load spread across the battens, besides easier reefing — and dismissing, by contrast, a tall Bermuda-style rig's dependence on "a whole tangle of wires and connections" as worthwhile only "to satisfy a racing rule when you don't even race."[63]

A further skeptical reply doubted a junk rig offers anything a well-executed full-battened gaff or Bermudan sail could not match given a topping lift, lazyjacks, luff parrels, or weighted reef points, calling the stock Paradox rig itself "a marvel of elegant simplicity" — light for its sail area and encouraging early, frequent reefing — and questioning the junk rig's own parts count, weight aloft, and number of unfamiliar named lines; the same reply credited Paradox's oversized rudder, rather than the chine runners, with countering most of the boat's leeway.[64] The rebuttal reframed the whole comparison around handling off the wind rather than to windward — where a junk rig's real edge lies, especially running dead downwind — argued the weight-aloft worry is overstated once a lower centre of mass and the absence of spare headsails and standing rigging are accounted for, itemised a simple junk's genuinely short parts list against a sloop's much longer one, and traced the standing lug's own sail-twist weakness to its downhaul sitting at the luff itself, rather than forward of it as on a balance lug.[65][66]

[65] [Paradox builders mailing list](/references/mailing-list.md) message 9088 (tdsoren / "TDSoren" / tom sorensen, `paradoxbuilders`, 2017-07-13) — "Re: Another launch (drift to Junk rig)": a junk rig's real advantage is off the wind, particularly running dead downwind, not to windward; its weight aloft is offset by a lower centre of mass than a sloop rig of equal area and by carrying no spare headsails or standing rigging; itemises a simple junk's short parts list — unstayed mast, one sail, batten parrels, halyard, yard parrel, mainsheet, lazyjacks, and (optionally) a light downhaul — against a sloop's much longer one, noting only the sheet, halyard, and yard parrel need touching in ordinary sailing and reefing.
