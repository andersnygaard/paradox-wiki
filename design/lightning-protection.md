# Lightning protection

The [Paradox](/paradox.md) plans specify a lightning-dissipation rod at the masthead, wired down through the mast to a grounded plate or fitting at the waterline (see also the mast's [reuse as a permanent HF radio groundplane](/operations/radio-and-electronics.md#a-permanent-hf-antenna-using-the-masts-lightning-protection-wire), which depends on the same wiring run).

## De-ionization, not attraction

The masthead fitting is properly a **de-ionization rod**, not a "lightning rod," and works by a common misconception's opposite: it does **not** attract a strike and conduct it safely to ground, but instead **repels** a strike so it never reaches the boat at all.[1]

- A visible lightning strike wanders across the sky rather than falling in a straight line because the discharge follows wherever the air's ions happen to be — and those ions are usually falling.[1]
- The rod **grounds the air directly above its own tip**, effectively shorting out the ions in the air within roughly **20 degrees all the way around the boat**. With no charged path left for a strike to follow down to the boat, the lightning is forced to discharge somewhere else instead. The volume of air below the rod's tip that this protects is called the **cone of protection**.[1]
- A boat correctly fitted with a system like Matt Layden's should, on this theory, **never have to worry about taking a direct strike**.[1]

## A firsthand account: strikes without symptoms, until the ground plate was insulated

A catamaran fitted with a system just like Matt Layden's, grounded to a pair of **aluminum skegs** ahead of the rudders, went years without a single strike while moored in **Jacksonville, Florida** — a self-described "lightning capital" — while the grounding path stayed intact.[1]

- At a haul-out, tired of the skegs corroding, they were **faired with Bondo and encapsulated in epoxy** — inadvertently insulating the grounding plates from the water.[1]
- Roughly **six weeks later**, out sailing, the radio and other electronics turned up dead; it was written off to equipment age and the gear replaced. About **two months** after that, the replacement radio and a CD player failed the same way. Looking aloft while puzzling over the problem, the antenna itself was gone entirely — "toast, fried, nothing left but the mount."[1]
- Once what had happened was recognized, the epoxy was **scraped off the skegs** with a knife over the side as soon as the boat returned to the mooring, restoring the ground path. No further problem occurred afterward.[1]

The sequence — silent equipment failures with no witnessed strike, traced only in hindsight to a lost ground connection — reads as indirect evidence that the boat was, in fact, being struck (or subjected to induced surges) repeatedly once the de-ionization system was accidentally disabled, and that the system had been doing its job silently before that.[1]

## A grounding plate seen under the glass

A photograph of a Paradox circulating online, showing a **copper sheet laid under the glass** as its grounding path, is a second data point, external to the mailing list itself, for the grounding plate's standard use in Matt Layden's generally thorough approach to seaworthiness — evidence enough to warrant grounding a Bolger "Oldshoe" with tall aluminum masts the same way.[2]

The photographed plate itself is cut from 6 in copper flashing, laid flush with the bottom and faired in, with the vacuum bag sealed using mastic rather than 5200 — see [construction techniques](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md#construction-techniques) for the full technique.

## Where the copper flashing sits in the layup, and copper's bonus as an antifoulant

One layer-order question is exactly where the copper flashing should sit relative to the hull's other layers: beneath the fiberglass roving (against the bare wood), beneath the bottom paint but on top of the glass, or exposed directly to the water.[3] General references on lightning protection for small boats do not address the layer-order question directly.[4] The standard build answers it in practice: the copper flashing is bonded straight to the bare wood, then faired over the top with **microballoons** and sanded fair — it sits **exposed at the hull's outer surface**, not buried beneath glass or bottom paint (see [construction techniques](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md#construction-techniques) for the vacuum-bagging method itself).[5]

Left exposed at the surface, the copper grounding plate turned out to double as an effective **antifoulant** — a copper-sheathed bottom needs no separate anti-fouling paint or its yearly maintenance.[6] Whether copper *powder* mixed into the bottom epoxy would give the same benefit was left for a future building season rather than answered on the record.[7]

The same layer-order question recurred from a newcomer still only studying the plans, before starting to build.[8] It recurred again in 2009, from a newcomer who had just started his own build, this time drawing a direct answer: the grounding plate should stay in bare electrical contact with the water rather than being glassed over — the same principle behind building the drain itself from copper tubing so it can double as the ground conductor (see [components/vent-box.md](/components/vent-box.md#a-2009-drain-routing-question-and-a-mast-step-electrical-contact-detail)).[9] It recurred once more in 2010, prompted directly by "[Zoë](/boats/zoe.md)"'s own grounding plate: an established builder, having noticed it was attached with a vacuum bag, asked for the process to be described.[13] The full step-by-step vacuum-bagging method — drilling and countersinking the plate's edge, then sealing a makeshift vacuum bag over it with mastic and bubble wrap — is recorded in full at [construction techniques](/people/glen-maxwell/build-and-zoe.md#construction-techniques).[14]

## The rod itself need not be bronze

The plans' masthead rod fitting can be made of **marine-grade aluminium** rather than bronze without a functional penalty — a substitution that came up alongside a broader round of plans-hardware material questions; see [furling system — hardware materials: stainless steel and tufnol as bronze/plastic substitutes](/components/rig/furling.md#hardware-materials-stainless-steel-and-tufnol-as-bronzeplastic-substitutes) for the full exchange, including a decision to fit one despite judging the local lightning risk low.[10]

## Mounting the rod so it can be removed or swiveled down

A further, practical question recurred in December 2010: how the rod itself is actually attached to the mast, wanting it either removable or hinged to swivel down rather than permanently fixed.[15] One answer: the rod is held at two points and comes off easily. Its upper end passes through a hole in a 3/4 in plywood plate, glassed in at the top of the mast just above the block, which doubles as the mounting point for the masthead light fitting. About 4 in below that plate, a 5/16 in copper bolt protrudes roughly 1/2 in from the mast — permanently connected, inside the mast, to the same length of copper tube running down to the grounding plate. The rod's own lower end is screwed to a short length of copper tube, flattened at the tip, which slips over the bolt and is secured with a nut.[16]

## Skipping the system entirely, and a jury-rigged grounding chain

Not every builder fits the plans' lightning-protection system. A low-latitude, storm-prone home port is exactly the case the system was designed for, but at least one UK builder left it off entirely, reasoning that the kind of storm it guards against is rare enough in southern England that he has never personally met an owner whose boat had been struck — "YMMV."[11] Absent any grounded rod or plate at all, a length of chain dangled from the standing rigging into the water whenever thunderheads are seen building is a workable jury-rig: one owner of a non-Paradox sharpie built with no lightning-ground fitting has watched electricity visibly running up and down the mast stays at night this way, without ever taking a strike.[12] The recommended storm practice where a rig carries no grounding at all: stay clear of all standing and running rigging once a storm is close; don't furl a jib that isn't already down, since handling it then means being in contact with the rigging at the worst possible moment; and reef the main and set a storm jib well ahead of the weather rather than waiting for it to arrive.[12]
