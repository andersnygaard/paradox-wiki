# Building Paradox larger: the designer-sanctioned scale-up

Enlarging [Paradox](/paradox.md) up to 20% is sanctioned by the designer, via a document bundled separately with the stock plans — not an annotation on the drawing sheets themselves — on the reasoning that no new drawings are needed because every enlarged dimension is simply the stock plan's own dimension multiplied by the same scale factor. Because the sanction is for a uniform scale-up rather than a change of proportions, this is a *property of the stock design*, not a variant departing from it; builders above about 6 ft and 200 lb have used it to size the boat to their own frame.

Much of the outside-experience reasoning gathered below — the ±10% guidance from the stock-plans trade, the squared-and-cubed scaling arithmetic, and the structural consequences of wider bulkhead spacing — was brought to the list by [Andres Espino](/people/andres-espino.md#enlarging-paradox), a sharpie owner who never built a Paradox himself.

## Scale length only, not beam or freeboard

One approach is to **increase length only** and leave beam and freeboard alone. Under this approach mass grows **linearly** with length rather than as the **cube** of the overall dimensions (as it would under uniform 3-D scaling), so a length-only stretch is far less punishing on weight — at the cost of producing what would likely be a **faster, straighter-tracking** boat rather than a true enlargement in every dimension.[1]

This differs from the standing advice recorded in [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md), that scaling length **without** beam "would alter the overall qualities of the design and come out with something new."

## The 10% enlargement, and why it's a practical ceiling

A 6'3", 210 lb build (partway through its own 10%-upsized "Paradox 1.1" — see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)) settled on a **10% enlargement**, judging anything larger problematic for two concrete reasons:[2][3]

- Beyond 10%, the **side panels** need an **additional scarf** to reach the required length.
- The **bottom panel's width** (already a tight fit at 100% and 110% scale — see [stretched Paradox](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md)) would **exceed 4 ft** once the chines are added, at any enlargement beyond 10%, forcing more scarf joints and more wasted plywood.

Short of those caveats, a 10% enlargement is straightforward — a practical ceiling drawn from a build already partway through execution, rather than a theoretical estimate. A 10% enlargement has since been carried a long way in practice: [Anders Nygaard](/boats/miramis.md)'s Norwegian "[Miramis](/boats/miramis.md)," under construction from 2020, is a full ×1.1 scale-up sized for a 196 cm builder and kept deliberately within a road trailer's limits — the first well-documented 10% build the archive can follow from bulkheads to fit-out, though it is not yet launched.[4]

## A tall sailor's hesitation, and reassurance that fit-driven scaling is welcome

A large sailor who had held stock Paradox plans for several years, put off starting a build by negative reaction on the list to modifying the original design, asked what would be wrong with a Paradox built 10-20% larger while keeping every internal detail of the original — having found no other design with Paradox's own combination of features.[5] The concern is misplaced: scaling a design to fit a sailor's own size is a different matter from customizing its interior, sail, or propulsion system, and Paradox's own designer found what worked for his own body and sailing style — a builder who plans to sail and live aboard the way the designer did is well served following the plans as closely as possible, but adapting the boat to a different body is not itself a departure from that design intent.[6]

## A 2008 restatement: no formal "stretched" Paradox, and a modest alternative to a full scale-up

A newcomer's question about a "stretched version" of Paradox, and where to find its design sketches and specifications,[7] drew the plain restatement that there is no official stretched Paradox — she comes in only the one size, 13'10", with [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md) as her "big sister."[8] The "stretched version" actually meant was this page's own scale-up document from the group's Files section, not Little Cruiser and not Matt Layden's separate Microcruiser project, a different and unrelated design.[9] Weighing the trade-off for himself as a 6'2" sailor, one correspondent found the scale-up tempting but judged the resulting boat would be too heavy; rather than accept the weight penalty needed to keep an enlarged hull self-righting and her chine runners biting deep enough, he leaned toward a more modest fix within the stock hull instead — cutting down the forward bulkhead opening and reducing the side bins to gain sleeping space, and perhaps raising the house a smidgen.[10]

## A worked 20% scale-up: dimensions, sail area, and displacement

A ready-worked scale-up already existed in the group's Files section by 2008 — computed and filed by its own author roughly ten years before a 2013 repost, dating the document's own creation to about 2003.[149] Multiplying every major dimension by 1.2 (a 20% increase) gives an LOA of 16'6.4" (5.04 m), LWL of 16'1.7" (4.92 m), beam of 4'11.3" (1.48 m), and draft of 10.8" (0.276 m), for a sail area of 168 sq ft, a displacement of 2,321 lb, a prismatic coefficient of .56, and a displacement/length ratio of 261. The cabin's living/sleeping area grows by only 15 in in length and 10 in in width at this scale — enough to matter but not enough to force a new interior layout — while the stock scantlings remain usable throughout except for the spars, which scale by the same 1.2 factor; leaving the water supply and stores lockers at their stock size yields a 7.5 in gain in headroom under the hatch. Bulkhead-to-bulkhead spacing grows by only 5.5-7.5 in at each bay, too small an increase on its own to justify a full redesign. The hardest part of the calculation was working out the new sail area (a 68% increase) and displacement (a 60% increase) needed to put the boat on her new lines while preserving the stock sail-area-to-displacement ratio. A smaller step is worked out the same way: at a 1.16 scale factor (a 16 ft boat), displacement comes to 2,075 lb and sail area to 148 sq ft.[11]

## A further 2011 confirmation of the scale-up's displacement/length ratio

Working backward from the plans independently while planning his own 10% enlargement, one correspondent recalculated the stock hull's own displacement/length ratio at about **261**, not the **270** published on sheet 1 — the identical 261 figure already carried by the 20% scale-up document above — and asked whether the ratio should even be expected to change for an overall size increase that keeps the same scantlings.[119]

## How far the designer's sanction actually reaches, and what the record does not settle

Most accounts converge on a **20% scale-up approved in principle**: the scale-up document bundled with the stock plans and posted to the group's Files section as "larger paradox" claims Matt Layden's blessing directly[11], an independent member confirmed the same 20% figure the same day in 2008[12], and the earliest report on record — from 2004 — already had Matt Layden giving the "all clear" to scale the boat 20% on the same scantlings[13], with a separate 2004 account of support for enlarging the design following shortly after[14]. One account, from a builder who put the question to Matt Layden directly, caps his approval at **10%**, reasoning that anything larger would amount to designing a new boat[15] — matching the practical ceiling reached independently above from side-panel and bottom-panel scarfing constraints. A single builder has claimed that **33-50%** is achievable without difficulty, but this is his own stated opinion, not something Matt Layden endorsed: it followed only a partial reply about an unrelated 21 ft "ultimate cruiser" concept, with no answer to a follow-up offering to pay for engineering time[16]. The scale-up document's own author confirmed authorship of it directly in 2013, restating Matt Layden's blessing in his own words rather than at second hand[149]; pressed further the same week, he went beyond any specific figure and recalled a personal conversation in which Matt Layden called Paradox **"infinitely scale-able,"** with the caveat of having the knowledge to address the myriad problems that come with an increase in scale[150] — a general design philosophy, offered by a fellow Paradox owner and former professional boat builder who knows the designer personally, rather than a documented number like the 10%, 20%, or 33-50% figures above.

**A 10% enlargement is uncontroversial under every account on record.** A 20% enlargement rests on the plans document (now confirmed in its own author's words) and two further independent confirmations, weighed against the one account capping the designer's own approval at 10%. **Nothing in the record supports designer sanction beyond 20% as a specific, actionable figure** — the 33-50% figure is one builder's own opinion, not designer-sanctioned, and the broader "infinitely scale-able" philosophy comes with no number attached to act on. The fuller case for stopping at 10% — including the practical cost of going bigger at all — is worked out at [stretched Paradox — carrying company aboard, and the practical cost of going bigger](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#carrying-company-aboard-and-the-practical-cost-of-going-bigger).

## Matt Layden's approval, and why the scale-up went unbuilt through the list era

Matt Layden has given his approval in principle to a 20% scale-up of this kind, on the reasoning that no new set of drawings is needed — every dimension required is already on the stock plans as drawn.[11][12] Despite the idea recurring on the mailing list roughly forty times over the years, no one built a scaled-up Paradox to these figures and reported the results back to the group during the list era.[12] Scaling up is more involved than redrawing dimensions at a fixed factor: sail area and displacement each have to grow well beyond the linear scale factor to preserve the stock sail-area-to-displacement ratio — a 68% and 60% increase respectively, in the worked 20% example above[11] — and the side and bottom panels need additional scarf joints once the increase passes 10%,[2][3] which plausibly accounts for the gap between recurring interest and an actual completed build. That gap was eventually closed, but outside the list and years later — see the next section.

Two further, independent confirmations of that same gap arrived within days of each other in early 2009. Asked directly whether anyone had built the enlarged Paradox, one correspondent could answer only "not to the best of my knowledge."[17][18] Days later, a different newcomer's version of the same recurring question drew Dave Bolduc's own restatement of the designer's blessing: Matt Layden had told him a 20% scale-up would be fine without increasing the dimensions of the lumber involved, though Bolduc himself likewise knew of no one who had actually done it — guessing it is simply easier to build the already-solved stock design than work through a scale-up from scratch.[19][20]

## The scale-up finally built: Eerik Mee's "THU"

The scale-up did eventually get built and sailed — not on the mailing list, and years after it. From **2015**, working at home in **Estonia**, [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md) built **"[THU](/boats/thu.md),"** enlarging the plans **uniformly by 1.2** on every measurement, and **launched her in 2017**.[21] His reported result — **LOA ≈ 5.05 m, beam ≈ 1.5 m** — lands within centimetres of the worked 20% figures above (5.04 m / 1.48 m), so THU is in effect that paper boat realised.[21] As the sanction assumes, he needed no new drawings: he "multiplied just all measurements with 1.2," recalculated only plywood consumption before buying material, kept the **stock scantlings**, and scaled the **spars** with the hull (the mast growing to 6 m).[21]

THU is therefore the archive's **only built-and-sailed example of this scale-up**, and it bears out both the promise and the practical cost recorded above. On the promise: the enlarged boat floats on her lines and sails, and Mee sized her up specifically to fit himself, exactly the fit-driven scaling the design's own reasoning welcomes. On the cost: the larger hull and heavier spars are harder to handle ashore — Mee launched solo by tying the boat to his car and reversing to push her in, judged the heavier mast a possible "hassle in heavy wind and slippery cabin top," and intended her to be **moored rather than trailered**.[22] The one caution in reading THU as a test of the paper figures is that she is **also** a heavy departure in rig — a purpose-designed cambered [junk rig](/design/ideas/junk-rig.md) rather than the stock lug — and Mee reported the hull dimensions but not an independent sail-area or displacement figure, so the worked 20% sail-area and displacement targets remain unconfirmed by a built boat. The full boat is at [THU](/boats/thu.md), drawn from [Eerik Mee's blog](/references/eerik-mee-blog.md). A second built scale-up is now under way at the smaller 10% step — Nygaard's "[Miramis](/boats/miramis.md)" (above) — but it has not yet sailed, so THU remains the only scale-up so far tested on the water.[4]

## Naming an enlarged Paradox, and speculation about the designer's own workload

A larger Paradox built to this scale would need a name distinct from the stock boat to avoid confusion — "ParadoxPlus," "ParadoxToo," and "ParadoxForTwo" have all been floated,[23] as has "Paradox Max"[24] — though the thread that floated it was itself named after the enlargement question generally, not any one builder's boat: the 20%-enlargement now under construction was independently pre-named "Manatee" months before her keel was laid (see below). One guess, offered as speculation rather than established fact, was that Matt Layden had not himself drawn up and marketed an official larger set of plans — for this scale-up or for Enigma — because he was fully occupied building and sea-trialing his own next boat for a race he called the "Around in Ten Race," with its start still roughly a year away; an alternative guess was that he judged the market too small to be worth his time.[23]

## Correcting the 20% figures for plywood curvature, and a beam-widening alternative

The worked 20% figures above measure length along the plywood panel's straight-line dimension; accounting for the panel's actual curvature once bent into the hull brings the true overall length down to roughly 15'9" rather than a flat 16'6".[24] A further alternative to scaling beam uniformly with length was floated for two-up comfort: 4 ft at the chine and 5 ft at the deck, reasoned to make the boat noticeably more comfortable for two users without otherwise changing the 20% scale-up.[24] The same reasoning was applied to Matt Layden's own [Enigma 460](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-enigma-family-the-360-and-the-plywood-cabin-460): judged close to the right overall size already but too narrow in the beam, widening her to 5 ft and taking the deckhouse to full width was suggested as a way to improve her interior in the same spirit.[24]

## A far larger, unsanctioned build: doubling every stock dimension for a live-aboard

Beyond every scale-up discussed above, one live-aboard project was built to roughly **double** every stock dimension — 28 ft by 8 ft, against the stock 13'10" LOA and roughly 4 ft beam — with no scale-up document consulted and no sanction sought from the designer, only the builder's own translation of every plan measurement to twice the stock scale. The hull used the **skeg** variation with a small open cockpit added; **Divinycell foam core** replaced plywood for the sides and decks (with a thin Divinycell inner lining bonded to the plywood bottom for added insulation and flotation), the internal framing was **reclaimed hardwood salvaged from a church**, joined with peg-and-hole, mortise-and-tenon, and dovetail joints and double-bonded with fibreglass and resin, and the chine runners were hand-carved from two solid pieces of reclaimed oak, hollowed out and filled with cast lead ballast before being glassed into the hull. Roughly £8,000 had gone into materials and tools by the time most of the hull, sides, and decks were built and glassed, and moving aboard was expected within a few months — at which point doubt set in: would a boat built to twice the stock scale actually float and sail as intended, and keep any of the design's own qualities?[25]

The reassurance given was that a well-proportioned hull generally works regardless of absolute size — an old naval-architecture maxim holds that "boats are generally boat shaped," varying mainly with local conditions and intended use, and that many good boats have been designed by eye alone, by builders with no formal training; any imbalance can be corrected afterward by adjusting the ballast once the boat has had some time in the water, since a builder able to build a boat can also fix whatever is wrong with it. At the stock length-to-beam ratio of roughly **3½ to 1**, preserved automatically by uniform scaling, the doubled hull was judged "sweet and slippery" and expected to sail fine.[26] The standard Paradox tuning method was recommended regardless of scale, "especially" at this size: launch heavily overballasted, then reduce weight progressively until she performs well.[27] A further correspondent added encouragement, judging the design well suited to being built larger and domestic small-boat design and construction generally too conservative; a question about why the skeg variation specifically had been chosen went unanswered on record.[28]

This is by far the largest scale-up attempt on record in the archive — roughly double the stock boat's every dimension, well beyond even the 33-50% figure floated as one builder's own unsanctioned opinion above. No boat name has been reported for her. A fellow prospective builder later asked to see photographs of her, unanswered on record.[29]

## Reactions at scale: rudder and sail balance, mast handling, and structural cautions

The reaction to the doubled-scale live-aboard was broadly encouraging, since the hull itself was already built. A narrower hull — around 6 ft of beam rather than the fully scaled 8 ft — was suggested as likely to sail somewhat better, though called a minor point next to the build's other choices, and getting the chine runners to bite effectively at this size was expected to need proportionally more ballast to submerge them, consistent with the overballast-then-reduce tuning method above. Paradox's resistance to leeway comes partly from a rudder area larger than "normal" for a boat her size, a factor that scales up along with everything else; a furling stock rig reefed down at double scale would end up carrying a very large roll of sail wound around the mast, of doubtful practicality past a certain sail area, and a stable, close-to-centerline mounting point for an outboard kicker matters more the larger a boat gets.[30][31]

A boat-design perspective added several further cautions specific to building at large scale. Structural loads and displacement scale with the **cube** of a linear dimension rather than its square, so a hull translated to double scale carries roughly four times the loading its shape alone might suggest — worth remembering when sizing masts and spars, and an argument for a counterbalanced, pivoting mast tabernacle, hinged several feet above the deck and ballasted at its heel so the short end offsets the long one, to keep raising and lowering the mast practical at this size. Sail and rudder balance matter more too: a lug sail overlapping the mast by about a quarter of its foot rather than a fifth, and a rudder blade with its leading edge set several inches ahead of the pivot line, both reduce the load felt at the tiller and mainsheet — see also [rudder and chine experiments — why the rudder was built large](/components/rudder.md#why-the-rudder-was-built-large-and-the-trade-offs-of-its-size) for the same trade-off already established at stock scale. Low length-to-beam-ratio hulls can rack and whip from side to side in a seaway as they grow larger, a problem Paradox's own short length keeps her from suffering regardless of scale, though hand-holds inside a large, open cabin, a way to get back aboard given the added freeboard, strong ground tackle for the added windage, and strong fendering for the added mass were all recommended regardless of hull proportions — Phil Bolger's AS29 addresses the same reboarding problem at a similar scale with a walk-through bow transom and cockpit; see [similar boat designs — Phil Bolger's Black Skimmer and Martha Jane](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#phil-bolgers-black-skimmer-and-martha-jane).[32]

A separate comparison recalled a Wooden Boat Magazine feature on an unrelated 28 ft cold-molded, round-bilge sharpie fitted with a centerboard and a freestanding cat rig, one more example of a design at this length making a workable liveaboard; scaling Paradox by the same doubling factor was worked out to a displacement of about 11,200 lb on an 18 in draft and 400 sq ft of sail area. Adding the skeg variation at this scale moves the hull's center of lateral resistance aft, in turn calling for the sail plan's own center of effort to move aft with it — grounds for consulting a naval architect directly, or for a schooner rig instead of a single stayless mast, since Paradox's center of effort already sits well aft of comparable designs; a small forward centerboard was floated as a lighter-touch alternative. A 32 ft chine-runner design, "Hogfish," was suggested as a further point of comparison for a boat built at this scale — one of the Hogfish family is in fact a 32-footer; see [similar boat designs — "Hogfish Maximus"](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md#the-hogfish-family).[33][34]

## An unanswered structural question: bulkhead spacing, panel thickness, and interior redesign at scale

Having read of the doubled-scale live-aboard above and found the same plans-bundled scale-up document central to this page, one reader pressed a structural doubt the reactions above had not directly addressed: a larger Paradox surely needs added bulkheads (stations) in proportion to the degree of enlargement, since simply stretching the stock plan leaves 5-6 ft of unsupported panel between bulkheads at something close to double scale, inviting the plywood to flex noticeably in a seaway; a larger hull would also seem to call for thicker plywood and heavier structural members such as a keelson, and a substantially redesigned interior layout to match. Put twice over a nine-day span — the second time restating the question almost verbatim while making explicit a personal wish to eventually build a larger Paradox himself — it went unanswered on record.[35][36]

## The wider debate: a do-not-scale doctrine, and why the interest in enlarging Paradox specifically persists

The same "Larger Paradox" thread broadened, without resolving the structural question above, into whether to scale the design at all rather than how. Several builders restated the stock plans' "do not scale" caution in its strictest sense — build to plan, or to a design purpose-drawn at the size wanted, since taking a rule to the drawings and stretching every measurement by hand will in all likelihood leave nothing fitting together properly.[37][38] General boatbuilding-plans guidance, independent of Paradox, was brought to the thread by a builder of five completed boats (three from published magazine designs, two from Glen-L plans): most stock designs tolerate roughly ±10% variation in length with little trouble, since length can be added by respacing a hull's stations while its center of buoyancy stays close to fixed, but beam and depth are a different matter — altering either changes a hull's basic hydrostatics (displacement, center of buoyancy, center of gravity, stability) closely enough that the result is, in effect, a new design rather than a stretched version of the old one, and a sailboat's length change also has to be weighed against keeping the sail plan's center of effort and the hull's center of lateral resistance in balance.[39] Enlarging Paradox to the extent of 28 ft was judged likely to lose many of her good features and require reworking the interior for comfort; echoing the alternative-design advice already given elsewhere in the archive (see [similar boat designs — John Welsford's Swaggie](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#john-welsfords-swaggie)), the practical suggestion was to build an already-larger design purpose-drawn at the size wanted instead, John Welsford's Swaggie or Phil Bolger's Birdwatcher among the usual candidates.[40]

The same exchange floated Phil Bolger's AS29 as a further comparison point, its underwater hull shape judged "much like the paradox," useful for guessing appropriate scantlings on a scaled-up Paradox by comparison with an existing, similarly-shaped design.[41] The AS29 itself was traced to an earlier Bolger design, "Jessie Cooper," preferred by one correspondent for lacking the AS29's blunt bow.[42] That correspondent's own reason for wanting a bigger Paradox rather than one of the alternative designs was Paradox's full self-righting and self-rescuing recovery from a complete capsize — a quality most commercial production sailboats lack past about 90 degrees of heel (see [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md)) — combined with a wish to build a shallow-draft, internally-ballasted hull capable of a shorthanded Atlantic crossing while carrying real cargo and stores.[42]

A further objection went beyond hydrostatics to ergonomics, and had not been raised in the structural cautions recorded above: Paradox's celebrated ease of handling depends on every control being within reach while seated, and doubling the hull's length would put the mast bulkhead far enough away that trimming sail stops being a simple forward reach and becomes a walk, unless the rope leads were separately redesigned to compensate — and every such change carries its own knock-on effect elsewhere in the design. Paradox works as well as it does, on this view, precisely because its scale and its controls were designed together, so a scaled-up boat could easily turn out less satisfactory than the stock design even where the hull itself scales up cleanly.[43] The same debate was cast, separately, as a chicken-and-egg problem: a builder capable of correctly modifying a stock design to this degree already has what it takes to draw an original one, raising the question of why scale at all rather than design from scratch — with a joking nod to the *Vasa*, the 17th-century warship whose own late design changes famously capsized her on launch day, as the risk of getting a modification wrong.[44] Weighing the hydrostatic and ergonomic objections together, one correspondent concluded that a Paradox enlarged to 28 ft, built properly — meaning with the interior genuinely reworked for the extra room rather than simply duplicated at double scale — would no longer really be a Paradox at all, but "a 28 foot sharpie based on the Paradox design."[45]

The thread's closing message confirmed the underlying motive behind the whole debate: its own author owned a 26 ft stretched sharpie of his own design that was not self-righting or self-rescuing, had looked up Swaggie and a further, smaller 21 ft John Welsford custom design in the meantime, but wanted Paradox's own combination of internal water ballast, self-righting recovery, and inside steering for beaching and river use — a combination of qualities, not simply more room, that keeps the interest in enlarging Paradox specifically recurring even after the alternative designs are pointed out.[46]

A further reply arrived a day later, echoing the Vasa comparison and closing the exchange on the same note reached above: no need to mess with "a good thing," with a further practical point added — a boat kept at her original size is also easier to live a nomadic, trailer-towing life with, rather than one enlarged and harder to tow.[47]

The correspondent whose original question had opened this whole debate replied to that same message the same day, stating his own motive outright: a planned solo Atlantic crossing that would need room for one more person aboard and far more stores and fresh water than a stock Paradox can carry for two people over 30 days at sea.[48]

He returned to the debate over a week later with a fuller self-portrait: having just turned 60, he already owns and regularly beaches a 26 ft modified Glen-L sharpie, self-righting only to about 35 degrees and not self-rescuing; the group's own worked scale-up file recorded above, stretching Paradox to roughly 16'6", still fell well short of the 28 ft he had in mind, and further research into Phil Bolger's designs only deepened the doubt — restating a preference for Bolger's earlier "Jessie Cooper" over the AS29 as its own ancestor and the better-shaped hull of the two (see [similar boat designs — Phil Bolger's Black Skimmer and Martha Jane](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#phil-bolgers-black-skimmer-and-martha-jane)). His own crew estimate had grown looser in the retelling — provisions for two on a month-long passage in the first reply, "3 people, a cat and a dog" in this one — but the conclusion matched the one already reached from the opposite direction above: enlarging Paradox enough to cross an ocean and a coastline in comfort would very likely cost her the sailing characteristics that made her worth enlarging in the first place, and "would end up turning into another boat anyway."[49]

The same correspondent, a further month on, drew the debate to its practical close for himself: the more he studied Paradox, the more convinced he became that stretching her past the sanctioned 20% would need thicker, stronger materials that in turn alter buoyancy and center of gravity enough to amount to redesigning the whole boat, and that a 29-footer's cost would run to many times a 15-footer's. Paradox, like the SF Pelican series, was judged "a wonderful boat for what it was designed" — an affordable pocket cruiser, and structurally far better than the Stevens Weekender — but not, on reflection, an economical route to the bigger boat he actually needed for a fully-provisioned, multi-person, 30-45-day Atlantic crossing. Economics alone, on this reasoning, ruled out building a 28 ft Paradox for himself; buying and refitting an older fiberglass production hull instead — a Pearson, Hunter, Irwin, or Grampian of the hand-laid-fiberglass era, available for under $4,000 — was left as the likelier path to the room he wanted.[50]

A further, more extreme precedent surfaced from outside the Paradox family entirely: the 12 ft San Francisco Pelican — the same design already noted above as stable but not self-righting — has reportedly been built at up to 24 ft, a full 100% enlargement, cited as evidence that plywood small-boat designs generally can be stretched well past any percentage Paradox's own designer has sanctioned, provided the construction itself (cold-molded, multi-layer bonded panels rather than a single heavier sheet) is scaled to match; see [plywood grades — a 2009 recurrence: doubling thinner sheets](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-2009-recurrence-doubling-thinner-sheets-instead-of-a-single-thick-panel-and-marine-versus-ac-grade-from-a-working-shipwrights-experience) for the fuller construction-technique discussion.[51]

## A 2009 coda: two further declared attempts, a towing-weight fix, and the Enigma 460 alternative

Two independent replies to the original question arrived within moments of each other: one suggested Matt Layden's own longer, lighter Enigma 460 instead, as an easier and quicker alternative to scaling Paradox up at all — provided reinforced with a layer of biax cloth — pointing to its builder's own construction blog as evidence of a real, if still-prototype, build in progress;[52] the other restated the group's own Files-section scale-up document and Glen-L's published stretch guidance already established above, adding a personal interest in stretching as far as 28 ft.[53]

Reassured, the correspondent whose question had prompted Dave Bolduc's restated blessing settled on scaling up 20% himself, with a few modifications of his own.[54] A second, newly arrived correspondent — plans already purchased — announced the identical intent independently within hours, reporting he had already begun working out the enlarged dimensions from the stock plans.[55]

The now-familiar towing objection followed close behind, restated by the same 6'2" correspondent from the 2008 exchange above with two concrete new details: at over a ton, the scaled-up boat's displacement was reckoned too heavy for an ordinary family minivan to tow.[56] A direct fix was offered for that specific problem: fit removable water-ballast tanks, filled by opening a valve once launched and drained again before hauling out — the same variable-ballast technique used aboard production Catalina sailboats to keep trailer weight down.[57]

A different, length-only alternative to a full scale-up was also on offer: butting the side panels instead of scarfing them, concentrating the resulting gain — about eight inches — in the cabin amidships, where the extra room is most wanted, an idea one builder was reportedly attempting in practice; see [a fuller account of this technique](/people/john-gilbert.md#scaling-and-an-enlarged-paradox).[58]

A follow-up asking where Enigma 460 plans could actually be bought drew an answer a day later: not yet, since the design remained at the prototype stage, with plans expected to follow once the prototype itself was proven; see [Enigma — the Enigma family](/matt-layden/enigma.md#the-enigma-family-the-360-and-the-plywood-cabin-460) for the fuller history.[59][60] Working the tow-ability question from the opposite direction, the same correspondent asked for the stock Paradox's estimated dry weight — as distinct from her full cruising displacement, which includes crew, stores, and water ballast — against the worked 20% scale-up's own figures: 1,410 lb stock versus 2,321 lb enlarged, both already established elsewhere in the archive (see [weight, ballast, and displacement](/design/weight-and-ballast.md#independent-weight-and-displacement-estimates)); a rough, self-described possibly-invented estimate of about 450 lb dry weight for the stock boat was offered in reply, with no firmer figure on record.[61][62]

A new correspondent introducing himself the same month gave yet another instance of the same pattern: in a perfect world he would build a 120% Paradox, or larger, but his own car could not tow it — illustrating again how a scale-up's added weight, rather than the hull itself, is usually what rules it out for a given builder; see [Enigma — Paradox and Enigma compared](/matt-layden/enigma.md#paradox-and-enigma-compared-overbuilt-strength-versus-light-displacement-simplicity) for the fuller exchange in which he weighed Paradox against Enigma instead.[63] The reply raised a towing subtlety not previously recorded here — that ballast carried inside the tow vehicle itself, not just the boat, counts against that vehicle's own rated towing capacity — see [trailering — tow-vehicle capacity: ballast carried in the car counts too](/operations/trailering.md#tow-vehicle-capacity-ballast-carried-in-the-car-counts-too) for the technique in full.[64]

## Whether a scaled-up Paradox needs a different sail plan

The worked 20% scale-up's own sail-area figure — 168 sq ft, a 68% increase over stock (see [a worked 20% scale-up](#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement) above) — prompted a fresh question: whether a single lug sail that size would prove unwieldy in practice, and whether an alternate, divided rig — a cat yawl, of the kind used on the Bolger Micro — might suit a scaled-up Paradox better than stretching the stock rig as drawn.[66] A single sail that much larger would need mechanical purchase (2:1 or 3:1 blocks) just to raise by hand, in turn requiring a good deal more halyard to be stored somewhere aboard.[67] A divided rig does carry one genuine advantage, from firsthand experience aboard an unrelated cat ketch: with the main let fly and a mizzen sheeted in, such a rig will weathervane and lie hove-to "like the most serene duck," parkable this way with room to leeward for as long as wanted — though a true yawl's own mizzen, being so small, would not subtract much sail area from the main in the first place.[68] Reefed down, a single sail of that size was expected to leave "quite a large, and perhaps sloppy, roll of canvas" wound around the boom; [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own sail plan was floated as a possible model for a cat-yawl rig on a scaled-up boat, with the suggestion to ask Matt Layden directly for his own thoughts — left unanswered on record.[69] The same divided-rig debate also produced a fresh, quantified account of how the *stock* rig already achieves a comparable parked, steady state without any change of sail plan at all; see [heavy-weather tactics — a 2009 return to the parking question](/operations/heavy-weather-tactics.md#a-2009-return-to-the-parking-question-a-quantified-account-across-four-rudder-and-sail-combinations).

## A further length-only proposal: stretching the living area without adding beam

A December 2009 exchange returned to the length-only approach introduced above (see [scale length only, not beam or freeboard](#scale-length-only-not-beam-or-freeboard)) in more concrete detail. A builder newly in possession of the stock plans wanted only a little more living space rather than the full 20% scale-up's much larger displacement (2,321 lb over the stock hull), proposing to keep the beam and both ends unchanged and add roughly 2 ft to the living area at the hull's widest and deepest point — a flat-bottomed addition chosen specifically to avoid working out new curved panel shapes from scratch — and estimating the added displacement at only about 390 lb.[72] The simplest way to achieve exactly that was confirmed: respace the bulkheads evenly between stations 2-3 and 3-4 to open up the extra length, leaving the new side-panel shape to be found empirically during trial assembly, though whether doing so is a good idea, and what it would do to the boat's sailing ability, was left an open question.[73]

A concern followed: lengthening the hull without widening it creates a longer "lifting hull" profile, where Paradox's own leeway prevention depends substantially on the chine runners assisting the curved bottom's Bernoulli-style resisting force against the flat leeward side; a little more waterline length might, on the other hand, let the boat sail a little faster, partly offsetting any flattening of that effect — chine runners are easily mistaken at first for a pair of miniature long keels, when in fact they act that way only marginally.[74] Pointed back to the group's own Files-section 20% scale-up document already established above, the length-only proposal was clarified as deliberately narrower in scope: the documented scale-up enlarges every dimension at once, for a much larger displacement and a different tow category, for comparatively little gain in living space and hull speed, where the length-only approach aims at the same extra room more cheaply.[75][76] A concrete precedent for the length-only approach exists outside Paradox: Phil Bolger stretched his own Micro design from 15.5 ft to 19.5 ft by adding 4 ft of length amidships on the same beam, producing a faster boat whose extra speed was judged to reduce the "time" available for leeway to develop over a given distance, with the added displacement also letting her carry more sail for still more speed — a result that reportedly impressed Bolger himself.[77] A second length-only precedent followed from the same exchange: John Watkinson extended his own Lugger design by a metre to create the Drascombe Longboat — further evidence the technique has already worked outside Paradox.[92]

A further reply added a broader caution against enlarging generally, beyond the length-only technique itself: more building space is needed, the boat becomes heavier to trailer, launch, and retrieve, and a bigger motor and costlier sail may follow, with cost and weight climbing exponentially rather than in step with the size increase; the stock rig's own reefed roll of sail wound around the boom, a factor even at stock size, only grows more unwieldy as the boat gets bigger.[78]

A different length-only technique was offered as an alternative to respacing the bulkheads: framing with steel angle instead of plywood webs, which frees up interior room by making the vertical frame parts thinner and lets a bulkhead at the rear of the sleeping area be built without a plywood wall at all, gaining usable length from within the stock principal dimensions — the same technique, in one already-documented build, that gave the boat her name, "[Ironsides](/boats/ironsides.md)."[79] The idea drew interest as a way to gain width as well as length, alongside a separately floated idea of opening up the end bulkheads instead.[80]

A broader caution followed: enlarging a boat in one, two, or three dimensions is technically possible, but doing so by any method invalidates the stock cutting plan, so plywood ends up cut inefficiently and wasted unless extra layout work is done first; the resulting boat will also be heavier, probably needing more ballast and possibly more water carried, and while a stock Paradox is close to the largest-displacement boat towable behind a small car — a non-issue with a larger tow vehicle — any added displacement needs added sail area to drive it, which is not simple to work out since the hull's dynamics differ from most boats'. Modifying the design in any significant respect without knowing the consequences risks ending up with something that does none of what the original does as well, since the original was worked out by someone with both the experience and the knowledge to do it right.[81]

A further precedent came from outside Paradox: sharpies are not always enlarged proportionally, and a 21 ft sharpie design personally stretched 5 ft on the same beam — by a builder who added stations into the center of the hull and re-faired it — sailed markedly better afterward; Bolger favored a 4-to-1 length-to-beam ratio, against the 3-to-1 or tighter many designs use to gain interior room (the Nordic Folkboat, at roughly 4-to-1, famously left no room to swing a cat below). Sharpies are meant to sail on a heel, with the hard chine forming a vee that keeps the hull tracking — the same mechanism a chine runner exploits by extending that chine downward once the boat heels. Widening the spacing between bulkheads specifically was flagged as the riskier way to gain length on a Paradox, since those bulkheads also serve as the hull's stations, restating Glen-L's own published guidance already established above; stretching this way also adds buoyancy that must be compensated with more ballast, moving the center of buoyancy aft and the center of gravity upward.[82] Adding length amidships, per the Bolger Micro precedent above, was restated as common practice generally among dories and their sharpie cousins, not a one-off technique.[83]

A further reply restated, as a direct and personal challenge to the correspondent behind the length-only proposal, the recurring gap between interest and an actual larger-Paradox build already established above: for years people had wanted a larger Paradox without anyone yet finding the courage to build one, and every argument for or against would remain opinion until someone actually got sailing in one — an invitation to be the first, and go down in boating history for it.[84]

A supporting figure, offered separately, put the length-only proposal's own displacement estimate on firmer ground: water weighs roughly 62-63 lb per cubic foot, consistent with the roughly 390 lb already worked out above for the proposed 2 ft x 4 ft x 0.75 ft addition.[85]

Having just received the plans, the same builder found their complexity intimidating enough to defer enlarging the boat at all for now, leaning instead toward keeping the stock hull and adapting it to his own large frame later — once his sons no longer wanted to crew with him — with a set of modifications already documented elsewhere in the archive: Ironsides-style bracing, a lowered stateroom-bulkhead opening, a modified bulkhead 4, and Johanna's own aft-cabin washboard; the same message's general sailing-performance questions (minimum sailing depth, typical heel angle, and speed across a range of wind strengths) drew firsthand answers recorded at [sailing characteristics — draft, heel, and speed across conditions](/operations/sailing-characteristics.md#draft-heel-and-speed-across-conditions-from-a-stock-owners-firsthand-account).[87] Pressed on whether he would go further and adopt the butt-rather-than-scarf lengthening trick reported above, he thanked the caution and settled, "on mature thought," on going no further than that one change.[88]

An independent illustration of the same cubic-scaling principle already established above arrived from outside the Paradox family: a published account of scaling Jim Michalak's own AF2 skiff up 20% into the "AF4 Grande," offered as the same kind of exercise as scaling up Paradox, states the general rule plainly — panel areas grow by the scale factor squared (a 44% increase at a 1.2 factor) while enclosed volume, and so displacement, grows by the scale factor cubed (a 73% increase at the same factor), which is why a modest linear enlargement produces a much larger jump in weight and cost than the dimensions alone would suggest.[89]

A follow-up refined the water-weight figure behind the length-only estimate with more precise numbers: an imperial gallon weighs about 10 lb against roughly 8 lb for a US gallon, and a cubic foot of fresh water comes to about 62.5 lb against roughly 64 lb for salt water.[90] Working the same figure from his own long-standing practice, the length-only proposal's own author said he had used 65 lb per cubic foot for salt water for years, closer to the salt-water figure just given than to the fresh-water one, allowing he might be misremembering it.[91]

## A further May 2011 exchange: an extreme length proposal, and the 20% ceiling restated

## An August 2011 recurrence: LOA-only rescaling, an old-sharpie doubling proposal, and Paradox's monocoque construction distinguished from sharpie practice

A further exchange asked specifically about lengthening the LOA alone rather than scaling every dimension, and the best way to do it.[110] The group's own Files-section scale-up document was restated in answer, together with Glen-L's own published guidance against enlarging a design more than 10%.[111] The same correspondent who had proposed the 5 x 20 ft stretch above returned with a far more radical length-only step instead — doubling the stock ~14 ft LOA outright to 28 ft, reasoning that period sharpie design rules, many drawn around that same length, could be adapted from there.[112]

The fuller reply distinguished Paradox's own construction from sharpie practice outright: she is a **monocoque** design, like many "instant boats," not a sharpie built of plywood over frame with a keelson, so building more than about 20% over the design risks inadequate internal bracing on top of the extra wood thickness needed to sustain the greater stress — problems fixable only by adding ribs or extra bulkheads after the fact. Paul Fisher's own "eggbox" method (`selway-fisher.com`) is one way a purpose-drawn multichine sharpie design solves the same bracing problem directly, by design, rather than retrofitting it onto a stretched hull.[113]

A real precedent for stretching a design past its own guidance came from the same reply's own 26 ft sharpie — already on record elsewhere in the archive as sailed the length of the southern US coast and through the Caribbean (see [sharpie lineage — fishing-boat origins, restated from outside experience](/design/sharpie-lineage.md#sharpies-fishing-boat-origins-restated-from-outside-experience-2010)) — built as a stretched version of Glen-L's 21 ft "Fancyfree" design. Built past Glen-L's own 10% stretch limit, the modification added extra stations, moved and enlarged the centerboard trunk to the boat's new center of buoyancy, and refaired the lines while leaving the beam unchanged; Glen-L has since drawn a purpose-sized 27 ft ketch, "Chessie Flyer," offered as the better route to a boat that size rather than repeating the same ad hoc stretch.[113]

Larger sharpies generally keep cabin height low, around 4 ft, for stability, sitting or scooting below rather than standing — though the stretched 26-footer's extra length allowed a full head and closet the stock Fancyfree lacks. Past a certain size most are not self-righting beyond about 50 degrees of heel and not self-rescuing, since a swing keel of around 400+ lb cannot supply enough righting weight, and their masts are correspondingly kept short and gaff- or sprit-rigged. Paradox avoids the same limit by a different route: her much lower mass and displacement let internal water ballast do a swing keel's job, the same advantage already established at [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md). Jim Michalak's own sharpie designs solve the ballast problem with iron rebar rods bolted under the floor or bulkheads, while keeping the type's shoal beaching draft; his plans, sold through Duckworks, were noted as among the least expensive available, at around $30.[113]

## A 140% hypothetical: a small mizzen, forward centerboard, and chine runners kept

A further, purely hypothetical proposal weighed enlarging the hull to **140%** — the largest size one prospective builder's own garage could store — while adding a small mizzen and a small, forward-placed centerboard and keeping the chine runners, inviting comment on what else such an oversized "paracruiser" might still need (more water ballast among the possibilities) despite staying smaller than many production trailerable sailboats.[114] [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own diagrams were pointed to directly in reply.[115] The same exchange led to a first visit to the Yahoo `microcruising` group's own files section, turning up the **"Big Cruiser"** sketches already on record above (see ["Big Cruiser": Matt Layden's own unfinished enlargement cartoons](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#big-cruiser-matt-laydens-own-unfinished-enlargement-cartoons)) independently — found to be "more than expected," but of genuine further interest.[116]

A reply to the same thread restated the practical ceiling above in more itemized form, drawing directly on a boat-plans company's own published guidance against stretching a design more than 10%: a "domino effect" of compounding problems follows — bulkheads spaced farther apart risking unnecessary flexing, material too thin for the larger vessel, thickened material adding buoyancy and weight, a shifted center of gravity and center of buoyancy, and heavier standing and running rigging — with the same guidance separately warning that stitch-and-glue and other designs built from pre-determined hull-panel shapes, Paradox's own chine-log construction among them, cannot simply have those panels expanded proportionately without redesigning them from scratch, "much like creating a new hull."[117][118] Weighed against a personal precedent for stretching past a stock design's own recommended limit — the same unrelated 21 ft Glen-L "Fancyfree" sharpie already recorded above, stretched 5 ft to 26 ft on an unchanged beam by adding stations and refairing the hull (see [the wider debate](#the-wider-debate-a-do-not-scale-doctrine-and-why-the-interest-in-enlarging-paradox-specifically-persists) above for the fuller build) — the archive's own 20% figure was read as roughly the practical ceiling before a stretch starts accumulating problems faster than it is worth; past that point, buying a purpose-drawn larger design outright, such as Jim Michalak's inexpensive Duckworks-published "Viola" 22 and 26 ft sharpie plans, was judged the better route.[117]

## A 2012 recurrence: the 16.5 ft document revisited, and two fit-driven size questions

A new correspondent, having found the Files-section document describing the 20% "Larger Paradox," asked in August 2012 whether anyone had actually built the resulting 16.5 ft boat, and whether its wider bottom and side panels could still be cut from two sheets of ply apiece rather than needing two scarfed lengths per panel.[120]

A fellow prospective builder, still at the planning stage, answered from his own parallel decision: several existing builds already depart from the stock plan in size, schedule, fastening, or framing. His own build, aiming otherwise for a fairly strict adherence to the design, had settled on a 10% increase after a cardboard mock-up found the stock interior too tight for his 6'4", 245 lb frame — willing at first to keep to stock size with minor accommodations, until discovering a cased guitar would not fit the forward "stateroom" at all. Even a 10% increase raises the sheet-goods count meaningfully past the stock plan's efficient panel layout (confirmed against the plans' own sheet-6 diagram), made workable by the availability, at a price, of 5×10 marine plywood; larger glass and epoxy quantities push the cost up further. Working the numbers through gave a displacement of 1,877 lb at 10%, with proportionate increases in ballast water and lead, mast/boom/yard stiffness requirements, sail dimensions, stowed-sail spool size, and windage — on top of the sheer mass, area, and volume that has to be machined, rolled over, fastened, covered, and sanded by hand. He expects the build to run well past a stock-size build in both time and cost, a trade made peace with, having been drawn back into the design for years by the same Files-section 20% document.[121]

Replying, the newcomer explained his interest was originally just the extra waterline length and interior room, but a recent lower-back injury — already making it hard to get in and out of his own car comfortably — had him wondering whether he could sit inside a stock-size Paradox for hours at a stretch. His plan: mock up the stock hull full-size in cardboard, heel it over, and sit inside reading for six hours (possibly camping a night or two in it) before deciding; if stock size felt just a little too small, he would remock at 20% and repeat the test, on the reasoning that the available scale-up should be taken in full if taken at all. Aware that a length increase raises cost and build time out of proportion to intuition, he noted that if the resulting boat proved too heavy to tow behind his other car, that would simply be an excuse to leave her on a mooring and sail more often.[122]

A further reply agreed many on the list, himself included, would be watching both builds with interest — sharing the same profile of a 6'2" frame and lower-back pain requiring frequent movement, with his own stock plans "getting a bit dusty."[123]

The newcomer confirmed he shares this exact profile and would report back once he had sat six hours in a mock-up.[124] An established owner, from his own long-passage sailing, cautioned that a static mock-up on land cannot reproduce a boat's constant motion once actually underway.[125] A further reply from the same owner offered a real alternative to prolonged sitting: standing and resting the elbows on the open hatch, or leaning over the side to watch the water go by.[126]

## A March 2013 recurrence: a newcomer's 20% plan, and renewed caution from an experienced builder and a standard-size owner

A March 2013 newcomer to the list — new to sailing but working toward a license, and already holding the plans — introduced himself set on a Paradox enlarged by roughly 20%, with reinforced bulkheads to suit, and had pre-named the intended boat before any keel was laid; the goal was a genuinely livable long-range cruiser, aimed eventually at a Mediterranean and Atlantic voyage.[127]

An experienced small-craft builder — forty years in the trade, having built several boats including two James Wharram "Tiki 26" catamarans — urged caution in reply: a stock design's specifications are the product of study, experience, and calculation, and are best trusted rather than modified on a hunch, since every change to one part of a boat is reflected throughout the rest of her.[128] Pressed further, the newcomer restated his reasoning — the boat was meant to be genuinely lived aboard for extended stretches, not merely camped in, and only a little more room, gained mainly through reinforced bulkheads, would make that practical.[129] The reply went further into the underlying arithmetic: even a modest linear increase, worked through on a 30 cm example, compounds into a considerably larger rise in volume, sail area, and ballast for comparatively little living-space benefit.[130]

[Alexander Fadeev](/boats/odyssey.md) — whose own standard-size Paradox, "[Odyssey](/boats/odyssey.md)," had by then completed two extended East Coast cruises — weighed in from the opposite direction. Built with only minor modifications and launched in 2010, "Odyssey" already carries a large man comfortably along with a full load of food, water, cooking gear, and clothing, and he could not think of a single reason a bigger Paradox would suit a solo cruiser better: a larger hull means a larger sail with heavier loads on the lines, a heavier mast harder to slide in and out to clear low bridges, a bigger anchor, more ballast, a bigger trailer and tow vehicle, and a materially higher build cost, all while giving up some of the snugness that keeps a sailor from being thrown around the cabin when she heels or rolls in a big surf.[131]

Still unpersuaded, the newcomer restated the size he actually wanted — something closer to [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own footprint, judged necessary for a genuinely long stretch aboard — and, weighing the alternative of building a catamaran instead, judged Paradox the cheaper and faster route to being on the water by a wide margin.[132] The experienced builder closed his side of the exchange there: advice, once given, does not bear repeating, since the choice and its consequences belong to the builder alone; the more honest recommendation was to find a design already drawn at the larger size wanted rather than stretch this one.[133]

A further reply agreed with the standard-size case in substance while pointing out that Paradox may already be roomier below than her nearest rival despite being a foot shorter — [Little Cruiser](/matt-layden/little-cruiser.md)'s own centreboard case eats into cabin space Paradox does not have to give up — but cautioned against being talked out of trying regardless: plenty of people had floated the idea of a bigger Paradox over the years but, so far as anyone on the list knew, none had actually built one, and reporting back with the result, either a capable cruiser or a disaster, would itself be worth having on record. The likelier outcome, on this view, was a boat too heavy to make a good beach cruiser while remaining more cramped than a conventional cruising yacht.[134]

Weighing every reply, the newcomer settled firmly on the roughly 20% step already worked out above (see [a worked 20% scale-up](#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement)) and asked the list for help with a new sail plan and revised dimensions for the engine, yuloh, and mast. His own approach avoided adding stations altogether, widening the existing bays by only a few centimetres apiece instead — the same respacing technique already worked out for a smaller gain elsewhere in this debate (see [a further length-only proposal](#a-further-length-only-proposal-stretching-the-living-area-without-adding-beam)) — with the added volume wanted specifically for stores and gear (diving equipment, a generator, tools) needed to live aboard for extended stretches; he remained uncertain how much heavier the finished boat, and so her trailer, would end up.[135] The same thread's weight tangent drew two direct replies: a one-word answer, "Stability,"[136] followed by a fuller explanation — the hull is narrow and carries no weighted keel, so ballast (some of it lead, low and amidships) has to sit deep enough to give a wide range of stability and true self-righting (see [self-righting and capsize recovery](/design/self-righting-and-capsize-recovery.md)); the hull is also its own keel for lateral plane, an effect the chine runners assist, and likewise needs enough immersion to hold leeway down (see [chine runners — why heavy displacement and heel are essential](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md#why-heavy-displacement-and-heel-are-essential)) — concluding there are not many other good ways to build a cruising boat of such shoal draft capable in open water, and Paradox is one.[137] A further correspondent — who began building his own Paradox, "Planktos," that same year — replied to the newcomer directly with a stretch technique not yet canvassed above (keep the existing frame spacing, simply lengthen the side panels) and general encouragement: experimenting is what it is all about, and Matt Layden himself only arrived at Paradox after quite a few earlier boats, so there was no reason not to continue that journey; see [Roselt Croeser](/people/roselt-croeser.md#a-march-2013-mailing-list-exchange-a-stretching-idea-and-general-encouragement) for the exchange in full.[138] The panel-lamination half of the weight tangent carried on separately at [plywood grades — a March 2013 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-march-2013-recurrence-laminated-thin-panels-compared-against-a-lightweight-catamarans-construction).

## A further April 2013 recurrence: a newcomer's own scale-up plan, and a fuller account from the same experienced builder

Ten days after the March 2013 exchange above, the same question recurred from a new correspondent already committed to a specific plan: an expedition traveller of 25 years' experience, aiming to spend 4-9 months at a stretch aboard, set on the sanctioned 20% enlargement.[139] The same experienced builder tracked above repeated his caution: even a modest linear increase compounds into a considerably larger rise in ballast, mast, and sail area for comparatively little extra room, and modifying an already-larger, already-resolved design would serve the same end better.[140]

Unpersuaded, the newcomer restated his case: the added ballast and weight were an acceptable trade, the sail would stay as drawn since a Paradox's own unhurried pace was the whole reason for choosing her over another boat, and three years of his own research lay behind the decision.[141] His intended use spanned two continents — European rivers and coastal cruising first, later shipped to Canada for the Great Slave and Great Bear Lakes, the Inside Passage, and the Intercoastal Waterway, mostly under engine — with shallow draft as the one non-negotiable requirement; his own target figures, LOA 5.04 m, LWL 4.92 m, and beam 1.47 m, land almost exactly on the worked 20% scale-up already recorded above (see [a worked 20% scale-up](#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement)). He also intended to redesign the stock rudder trunk, wary of an opening through the transom — a concern the archive's builders have already answered in practice; see [vent baffle — two 2011 DIY tiller-port baffle designs](/components/vent-baffle.md#two-2011-diy-tiller-port-baffle-designs).[142]

The experienced builder, closing his side of the exchange, revealed he had by then bought the Paradox plans himself, and filled out his own credentials: a Sztrandek-design sailing canoe of his own build — 16.5 ft, roomy, built to a free blueprint, rigged from a Hobie Cat 14's mast, sail, and jib — and a 26 ft James Wharram "Tiki 26" catamaran, his current boat. What drew him to Paradox was her sail-from-inside cabin and her simple rig and ground-tackle handling; what gave him pause was her weight for her size, next to his own thin-laminate-panel building experience — the fuller comparison is carried at [plywood grades — a further April 2013 recurrence](/construction/plywood-grades-and-foam-flotation.md#a-further-april-2013-recurrence-full-construction-specifics-from-the-same-builder).[143]

## A further April 2013 exchange: a size comparison rejected, professional-builder-assisted construction, a second newcomer joins, and the scale-up document's own author restates the designer's blessing

Continuing the same exchange, the newcomer rejected the experienced builder's own Sztrandek canoe as a guide for his own plans — judged too big and too wide, with 1.5 m as his own hard beam ceiling — restating that no other design serves as an alternative to Paradox for his purposes.[144] A further correspondent, new to the thread, raised a caution not yet voiced in the debate: enlarging a hull changes its proportions as well as its overall size, particularly the beam-to-waterline aspect ratio, rather than simply multiplying every dimension by a constant factor.[145]

Answering that caution, the newcomer set out his actual construction plan for the first time: not a solo build, but one carried out together with a professional boat-builder, who would handle the technical work while he assisted — reasoning that his own city offered no space to build in, comparable in his own words to building a boat in downtown New York, and that renting suitable space would cost more than simply paying a professional outright; with his own life depending on the finished boat, he judged the safer, costlier route worth it.[146]

A further correspondent joined the debate at this point, asking the proportions objection to be spelled out further and restating his own understanding of the sanctioned scale-up already established above — every dimension increased 20%, with ballast added to restore the boat to her original designed waterline, approved by Matt Layden — while revealing that he too was seriously weighing a bigger Paradox build of his own, joking that none of those considering it really want to be the first guinea pig.[147] Promising, should the build go ahead, to document the whole process on the same website he used for his other boats, the newcomer repeated his call for information on the 20% enlargement, having found nothing useful searching on his own.[148]

The document underlying the worked 20% scale-up table above (see [a worked 20% scale-up](#a-worked-20-scale-up-dimensions-sail-area-and-displacement)) was reposted to the list a further time in direct answer to that call — this time by its own author, a Paradox owner and former professional boat builder, who confirmed personally computing every figure roughly ten years earlier and restated, in his own words as its author, that Matt Layden had given his blessing to a scaled-up version needing no new set of drawings, since every dimension required is already on the stock plans as drawn.[149] Thanking him, the newcomer judged the repost had answered his question "tremendously." The same author then closed the exchange with the strongest sanction claim on record: from personal experience having built and sailed his own Paradox, and knowing Matt Layden personally, he was confident the newcomer would have the designer's blessing — recalling that Matt Layden had told him directly Paradox was "infinitely scale-able," with the caveat of having the knowledge to address the many problems that come with increasing scale; as a former professional boat builder, he judged himself qualified to make that call, understanding how to move bulkheads, add support where needed, and recalculate sail area and displacement to keep a design properly balanced at any size.[150] See [people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md](/people/glen-maxwell/matt-layden-eyewitness.md#matt-laydens-own-words-on-scaling-the-design) for the fuller, attributed account.

## A further April 2013 coda: a newcomer's declared intent, and the same builder's own timeline

One of the newcomers who had followed the whole exchange declared himself persuaded, intending to build a Paradox himself using the newly worked-out enlarged measures.[151] The correspondent whose scale-up plan had prompted the debate confirmed his own build would begin only in early 2014, once an extended 2013 trip through Alaska and Canada was finished; in the meantime, the figures already worked out on the list, together with photographs contributed by other builders, gave him material to plan how he would outfit the boat.[152]

## A May 2013 recurrence: construction begins on "Manatee," and a reported conversation with Matt Layden

Construction began on the pre-named 20%-enlargement "**[Manatee](/boats/manatee.md)**" two months after the plan was first announced (see [a March 2013 recurrence](#a-march-2013-recurrence-a-newcomers-20-plan-and-renewed-caution-from-an-experienced-builder-and-a-standard-size-owner) above): plywood and epoxy were ordered for delivery within the week, mast timber to follow in June, and the wide Okoume sheets a scale-up of this size needs — 3100 x 1530 mm — proved hard to source locally except in bulk, eventually secured as two 18 mm sheets that size for the bottom panel. Before cutting, the archive's other documented hulls were studied in detail, [Odyssey](/boats/odyssey.md) singled out as a particularly successful boat alongside Johanna, Faith, Cachivache, Ironsides, the Taiwan Paradox, ParaPlastic, Zoë, and the Aussie Paradox.[153]

The announcement drew a report of a personal conversation with Matt Layden three years earlier, aboard a Wharram Tiki 46: Matt gave no sense a larger Paradox would be difficult, a poor performer, or otherwise a mistake to build, and had himself sketched larger versions at 21-plus feet — matching the 21 ft and 23 ft "Big Cruiser" cartoons already on record (see [stretched Paradox — Matt Layden's own unfinished enlargement cartoons](/design/ideas/stretched-paradox.md#big-cruiser-matt-laydens-own-unfinished-enlargement-cartoons)).[154]

A further reply judged even the sanctioned 20% step a "mixed blessing" — a heavier boat needing a bigger tow car, no easier to launch and recover, and still leaving standard-size Paradox's besetting living-aboard problem unsolved: no room to leave a berth permanently made up. What would really be worth having, on this view, is a full doubling of every dimension, for eight times the internal volume, making a genuine live-aboard cruiser — at the cost of a revised rig, since the stock sail plan would be too big to handle comfortably at that scale; a junk rig was floated as a possible fix.[155] Weighing that suggestion, Manatee's own builder judged a doubled size too big for his purposes, preferring a catamaran at that scale instead, and confirmed construction itself under way: the side panels cut, with the bulkheads next.[156]

## A further January 2014 recurrence: a far larger, unrelated German build, and a mechanical-purchase caution restated

A further recurrence in January 2014 opened with the same standing question — whether a bigger Paradox had ever been built — answered again by pointing to Matt Layden's own Enigma, by then separately built in a larger version alongside the original.[158] A far larger, unrelated project was then recalled from memory: a German builder's own roughly 24 ft "Paradox" or 'Dox — well past every scale-up figure recorded above — whose progress had been slowed by a skin reaction to epoxy, the same hazard already documented at [epoxy safety and personal protection — a firsthand skin allergy](/construction/epoxy-safety-and-ppe.md#a-firsthand-skin-allergy-2013), and who was recalled as having judged, early in his own build, that 24 ft was too big a step given the sail such a hull would need. Matt Layden's approval was restated at its familiar 10% figure, with the added caution that enlarging the sail punishes exactly the sailor gaining the extra room.[159] The reaction to a 24 ft Paradox was blunt — "a HUGE jump" — met with encouragement regardless, and a restatement of the already-established point that mechanical purchase (block and tackle) would likely be needed to cope with the greater up-hauling and reefing loads such a build implies.[160][161] Asked for the build's own webpage, the project was at first mistaken for the already-documented 20%-enlarged "[Manatee](/boats/manatee.md)" (above) — a distinct, unrelated, and considerably smaller build, corrected on the spot to 16 ft, not 24 ft — with [Chris Morejohn](/people/chris-morejohn.md)'s 44 ft "[Hogfish Maximus](/similar-boat-designs/hogfish-maximus.md)" offered again as the standing point of comparison for a boat built genuinely big.[162][163] Believed at first to survive only as photographs in the group's own albums, the German build's own website surfaced the same evening: `bigornick.wifeo.com/plywood-sharpie.php`.[164][165]

## A walked-back "24 ft" figure, and the standing sail-scaling caution restated

Days later, the 24 ft figure above was walked back as an uncertain memory rather than a firm fact, with the underlying physics restated in its place: a boat built some fraction bigger demands more than that same fraction of extra sail — the standing caution behind Matt Layden's 10% guidance throughout this page.[166]

## A worked example: cubic displacement scaling, and the draft increase it implies (February 2014)

Checking a 20% scale-up's expected dimensions against "[Manatee](/boats/manatee.md)"'s own build photographs turned up a mismatch: most of her individual measurements did not track the 20% factor directly.[167] The answer confirmed only the three principal dimensions — length, width, and height — had been scaled uniformly by 20%, with material thickness left to personal discretion rather than scaled in step, insulation increased to 50mm, and bulkheads kept at their plan position shifted by the same 20% (full detail: [Manatee — a further February 2014 exchange](/boats/manatee.md#a-further-february-2014-exchange-the-allergys-treatment-and-scaling-specifics-confirmed)).[168]

Asked next for the resulting new displacement, and how it would affect draft and the chine runners,[169] the theoretical answer was worked from first principles rather than measured: unless badly mistaken, new displacement follows the product of the percentage increase along all three axes multiplied together — 1.2 cubed for a 20% scale-up, or about **1.7 times** the original, roughly **2400 lb** for a build Manatee's size — "a BIG little boat." Doubling the thickness of the flotation foam was judged a good precaution at that new displacement.[170] The extra displacement means more stores and ballast are needed to load a scaled-up hull down to her designed draft and keep the chine runners submerged and working, raising the question of whether draft could be reduced instead;[171] in theory, draft increases proportionally too, by about another **inch and a half** over the stock 9 in.[172]

## Why the added capacity is wanted, and why bigger builds have stayed rare

The 20% scale-up applies across every plan dimension, the chine runners included, and it is not sought for its own sake: the extra room and displacement are meant to carry genuinely more load — diving gear, additional food, and drinking water — for a boat intended to work open-water passages as well as coastal runs, not a recalculated or reduced version of the same 20% figure.[173] What has kept a bigger Paradox rare through the list's whole run is probably simpler than any single objection raised along the way: weight climbs very quickly with scale, and full draft — on the order of **1.2 times** the design draft, consistent with the inch-and-a-half increase worked out above — is needed just to keep enough lateral resistance for the boat to sail properly.[174]

## A March 2014 exchange: Welsford's Swaggie compared, and the correct scaling law for stability

Whether an overweight build's waterline needs raising was asked directly, alongside a stated preference for the scale-up's own heavier displacement, reasoning that a heavy-displacement boat is more comfortable than a light one over a long passage.[175] Weighed against a specific comparison boat, even a theoretical 2,400 lb was not judged a particularly heavy displacement: draft aside, a similarly-sized Welsford Swaggie (see [similar boat designs — John Welsford's Swaggie](/similar-boat-designs/comparisons.md#john-welsfords-swaggie)) carries more.[176]

A fresh pass through the 20% scale-up's own arithmetic worked the 1.2 linear factor two ways: cubed, it reproduces the roughly 170% displacement increase already established above (see [a worked example](#a-worked-example-cubic-displacement-scaling-and-the-draft-increase-it-implies-february-2014)); squared, applied to hull stability, was first reckoned to give a 140% increase, with wetted-area drag, sail area, and spar scantlings all following that same 140% figure to keep the boat in proportion — reasoning that in light air, the wind-force increase on a 140%-larger sail should in theory be met by the matching rise in wetted-area drag. On that reasoning, scaling the rig by only 140% while the hull grows the full 170% would put the sail's centre of area too high and too far forward, increasing heeling moment, lowering drive, and raising lee helm, hydrodynamic drag, leeway, and man-overboard risk; scaling the rig fully with the hull, by 170%, raises the centre of area even further, again increasing heeling moment, on top of the added rig weight and height further reducing stability. The larger, 170%-scaled hull was reasoned to carry a higher theoretical hull speed, which — if the chine runners function as foils rather than fences — could let them be sized somewhat below the full 170% increase, since lift increases with speed; any such reduction trades against wetted-area drag, and a heeled runner's own increased draft (width) widens the distance between the sail's centre of area and the hull's centre of lateral resistance, further increasing heeling moment and affecting sheeting, reefing, stability, and drive under way.[177][178]

The square-law reasoning for stability was corrected: **hull stability actually scales with the fourth power of the linear factor, not the square** — reasoned from a multihull analogy, where the righting mass scales with the cube and the righting arm itself contributes a further power.[179] The correction was accepted, complicating the picture further: hull weight in practice increases somewhat less than the cube, since scantlings are not usually all increased by 20% in step with the hull's principal dimensions, shifting KB, KG, and KM (the vertical centre of buoyancy, centre of gravity, and metacentre) relative to a purely linear scale-up; added ballast, needed anyway to set a lighter-than-expected hull on her lines, tends to push those relationships further toward increased stability.[180] Carrying the corrected exponents forward, the heeling effect of wind on the sail was reasoned to scale with the cube of the linear factor, matching the displacement increase, while stability scales with the fourth power — confirmed as correct: heeling moment does scale to the third power and stability to the fourth, so a scaled-up boat's chine runner will be less heeled, not more, than the stock boat's own at any given wind strength, leaving margin either to enlarge the sail somewhat beyond a strict linear scale-up, or simply to reef later.[181] What the corrected stability margin means for the boat's lateral plane — whether it calls for more sail area to heel the hull enough to submerge the lee side adequately, or for more ballast to increase draft instead — was raised as a follow-on question and left open on record; no reply settling it is on record within the archive as consulted here.[182]

A further correction sharpened the same exchange's own sail-rig arithmetic, catching two figures that had been conflated. A claimed 40% rise in the sail centre-of-effort's height in fact describes the increase in sail **area**, not the vertical dimension, which holds at the scale-up's own 20%; the centre of area will also shift aft somewhat, by an amount that cannot be fixed without the sail's actual dimensions, and the real height increase should run slightly under 20% once the sail's own shape is accounted for. A second mixup ran through the same rig-dimension figures: **120%, not 170%, is the correct linear scale factor** for the rig — 170% is the cubed displacement multiplier established earlier in the thread, not a length or dimensional figure at all.[183]

## A reported reason for the 20% ceiling: the chine runners

The list era settled 20% as the designer's sanctioned limit without ever recording **why** the limit sits there. A 2022 exchange in the [Paradox Sailboat Facebook group](/references/paradox-facebook-group.md) supplies a reported answer, and it is worth holding loosely: asked why a boat that sails as well as the Paradox cannot simply be scaled further, an experienced Australian owner replied that **"the chine log system won't operate if scaled greater than 20%"** — his understanding of a statement by **Matt Layden**, offered with the explicit caveat that he did not understand the mechanism himself and that Layden "would know."[157] It is **second-hand and unexplained**, and no primary source in the archive attests it; but it is the only account on record of a *physical* reason for the ceiling, and it points at the [chine runners](/chine-runners/how-they-work.md) rather than at structure, trailering, or rig — the grounds the list actually debated. The one built 20% boat, [Eerik Mee](/people/eerik-mee.md)'s "[THU](/boats/thu.md)," sits exactly at the stated limit and so does not test it.
